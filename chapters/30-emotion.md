# Chapter 30 — Emotion, Imagination, and the Embodied Mind

> “The bodily changes follow directly the perception of the exciting fact, and … our feeling of the same changes as they occur IS the emotion.”
> — William James, *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), II, p. 449

> “Emotions are not simply energies or impulses; they are intelligent responses to the way things are.”
> — Martha Nussbaum, *Upheavals of Thought* (2001), p. 1

> “The world is not an object such that I have in my possession the law of its making.”
> — Maurice Merleau-Ponty, *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945), p. xviii

---

Part IV has examined the mind through its *most abstract* concepts: consciousness (Ch. 24), intentionality (Ch. 25), the self (Ch. 26), freedom (Ch. 27), rationality (Ch. 28), and other minds (Ch. 29). This chapter examines the mind through its *most concrete*: **emotion**, **imagination**, and the **body**.

The order is not arbitrary. The five previous chapters share a *picture*: the mind as a *computational* or *representational* system, housed in a *body* that is *contingent* to the mind's operations. The picture is *powerful*, and it is *incomplete*. The *emotions* are the *clearest* case in which the *body* is not *contingent*: the *felt* character of *fear* is *constituted* by *visceral* and *somatic* changes (on some accounts), and the *appraisal* that *generates* the emotion is *itself* a *bodily* *process*. The *imagination* is the *clearest* case in which the mind's *content* is *not* *reducible* to *perception*: we *represent* what is *not there*, and the *representation* is *not* a *defective* *perception* but a *distinct* *capacity*. And the *body* is the *clearest* case in which the *boundary* of the mind is *contested*: the *notebook*, the *phone*, the *prosthesis* may be *part* of the *cognitive* *system*, and the *boundary* between the *inner* and the *outer* is *not* *given* by the *skull*.

The chapter's *thesis* is that the *classical* *conception* of the mind — *rational*, *disembodied*, *representational* — is a *caricature* of the *actual* mind, and that the *caricature* was *sustained* by a *philosophical* *tradition* that *separated* the *cognitive* from the *affective*, the *mental* from the *bodily*, and the *inner* from the *outer*. The *contemporary* *literature* has *reversed* the *separations*, and the *reversal* is the *most* *important* *development* in the *philosophy* of *mind* since the *computational* *revolution*.

The chapter proceeds in eight movements. §1 examines the *theories* of *emotion*. §2 examines the *rationality* of *emotion*. §3 examines *imagination* and *pretense*. §4 examines the *4E* *mind*. §5 examines *skill* and *know-how*. §6 examines *attention* and the *subject*. §7 examines *perception*, *affect*, and the *lived* *world*. §8 gives an *honest* *verdict* and *closes* **Part IV**.

## 1. The Theories of Emotion

### 1.1 The four families

The *philosophy* of *emotion* is a *field* in which *four* *families* of *theory* *compete*, and the *families* differ on *what* *emotion* *is*.

| Family | The *claim* | The *paradigm* | The *proponent* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Feeling** | Emotion is the *perception* of *bodily* *changes* | The *racing* *heart*, the *sweaty* *palms* | James (1884); Lange (1885); Damasio (1994) |
| **Judgment** | Emotion is an *evaluative* *judgment* | The *judgment* that the *situation* is *dangerous* | Stoics; Solomon (1976); Nussbaum (2001) |
| **Appraisal** | Emotion is a *pattern* of *appraisal* *processes* | The *evaluation* of the *situation* *relative* to *goals* | Arnold (1960); Lazarus (1991); Scherer (2001) |
| **Enactive** | Emotion is a *dynamic* *pattern* of *embodied* *interaction* | The *action* *tendency*, the *bodily* *orientation* | Colombetti (2014); the *contemporary* *literature* |

**The *significance*.** The *families* are *not* *mutually* *exclusive* (a *theory* can *combine* the *feeling* and the *judgment* *components*), but they *differ* on the *nature* of the *emotion* and on the *role* of the *body* in *constituting* it.

### 1.2 The James–Lange theory

**The *thesis*** (James, “What Is an Emotion?”, 1884; *The Principles of Psychology*, 1890, II; Lange, *Om Sindsbevægelser*, 1885). The *emotion* is the *perception* of *bodily* *changes*: the *stimulus* *produces* the *bodily* *changes* (the *heart* *rate*, the *respiration*, the *muscle* *tension*), and the *perception* of the *changes* *is* the *emotion*.

**The *structure*.

$$
\text{Stimulus} \ \to \ \text{Bodily change} \ \to \ \text{Perception of the change} = \text{Emotion}
$$

**The *argument*** (James 1884, the *famous* *passage*).

> “We feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble, and not that we cry, strike, or tremble, because we are sorry, angry, or fearful.”

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *grounds* the *emotion* in the *body*, and it *fits* the *phenomenology* of *strong* *emotion* (the *visceral* *feel*, the *bodily* *upheaval*).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*undifferentiated*’* *objection*** (Cannon, “The James–Lange Theory of Emotions,” 1927; Schachter & Singer, “Cognitive, Social, and Physiological Determinants of Emotional State,” 1962). The *bodily* *changes* are *undifferentiated* across *different* *emotions* (the *heart* *races* in *fear*, *anger*, and *excitement*), and the *differentiation* requires a *cognitive* *component*.

**The *reply*.** The *bodily* *changes* are *more* *differentiated* than *Cannon* *thought* (the *specific* *autonomic* *signatures*; the *contemporary* *literature*; the *meta-analyses* of *the* *recent* *literature*), and the *differentiation* *supports* the *theory*. The *reply* is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*timing*’* *objection***. The *emotion* can *precede* the *bodily* *changes* (the *rapid* *evaluation* *precedes* the *arousal*), and the *timing* *undermines* the *theory*.

**The *reply*.** The *bodily* *changes* *begin* *before* the *conscious* *emotion* (the *unconscious* *appraisal* *triggers* the *arousal*, and the *conscious* *emotion* *follows*), and the *timing* *supports* the *theory*. The *reply* *relies* on the *distinction* between the *unconscious* *appraisal* and the *conscious* *feeling*.

**(iii) The *‘*recalcitrant*’* *objection*** (§2.2). The *emotion* can *persist* *against* the *judgment* (the *fear* of a *spider* while *judging* it *harmless*), and the *theory* *cannot* *explain* the *conflict*.

**The *reply*.** The *recalcitrant* *emotion* is a *bodily* *response* that *persists* (the *arousal* *conditioning*), and the *response* is *not* a *judgment*; the *reply* *supports* the *feeling* *theory* and *undermines* the *judgment* *theory*.

### 1.3 The judgment theory

**The *thesis*** (the *Stoic* *tradition*; Solomon, *The Passions*, 1976; Nussbaum, *Upheavals of Thought*, 2001). The *emotion* is an *evaluative* *judgment* — a *judgment* that a *situation* *bears* on the *agent*'s *flourishing* in a *certain* *way*.

**The *structure*.

$$
\text{Emotion}(E) = \text{Judgment}(\text{the situation is } F, \text{ where } F \text{ is evaluatively loaded})
$$

**The *argument*** (Nussbaum 2001, Ch. 1). The *emotions* are *about* *objects* (they have *intentionality*; Ch. 25), and the *relation* to the *object* is *evaluative* (the *grief* is *about* the *loss*, and the *loss* is *evaluated* as *significant*). The *judgment* is the *best* *explanation* of the *intentionality* and the *evaluation*.

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *captures* the *intentionality* and the *evaluative* *structure* of the *emotions*, and it *grounds* the *rationality* of *emotion* (§2).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*recalcitrance*’* *objection*** (Greenspan, *Emotions and Reasons*, 1988; the *contemporary* *literature*). The *emotion* can *conflict* with the *judgment* (the *fear* of the *spider* *persists* while the *agent* *judges* the *spider* *harmless*), and the *conflict* *shows* that the *emotion* is *not* the *judgment*.

**The *reply***. The *recalcitrant* *emotion* is a *residual* *judgment* (the *unconscious* *judgment*, or the *“*in* *the* *throat”* *judgment*), and the *conflict* is between the *conscious* and the *unconscious* *judgments*. The *reply* *qualifies* the *theory*, and it is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*too* *intellectual*’* *objection***. The *emotions* are *felt* (they have a *phenomenology*), and the *judgment* *does* not *capture* the *feeling*.

**The *reply***. The *judgment* is *felt* (the *judgment* *involves* the *bodily* *changes*; the *judgment* *is* the *feeling*). The *reply* *blurs* the *distinction* between the *judgment* and the *feeling*, and it is *contested*.

**(iii) The *‘*animal* *emotion*’* *objection***. The *animals* *feel* *emotions* (the *fear*, the *anger*), and the *animals* do *not* *judge*. The *objection* *undermines* the *judgment* *theory*.

**The *reply***. The *animals* *have* *evaluative* *representations* (the *non*-*conceptual* *appraisals*; Ch. 25, §5), and the *representations* *are* *judgments* in a *broad* *sense*. The *reply* *weakens* the *theory*, and it is *contested*.

### 1.4 The appraisal theory

**The *thesis*** (Arnold, *Emotion and Personality*, 1960; Lazarus, *Emotion and Adaptation*, 1991; Scherer, “Appraisal Considered as a Process of Multilevel Sequential Checking,” 2001). The *emotion* is a *pattern* of *appraisal* — a *multi*-*component* *evaluation* of the *situation* *relative* to the *agent*'s *goals*, *values*, and *coping* *resources*.

**The *structure*.

$$
\text{Emotion}(E) = \text{Appraisal}(\text{relevance}, \text{congruence}, \text{coping}, \ldots)
$$

**The *components*** (Scherer 2001, the *Component Process Model*).

| Component | The *question* | The *example* (fear) |
|---|---|---|
| **Relevance** | Does the situation matter? | Yes (a threat) |
| **Implication** | Is it good or bad for my goals? | Bad |
| **Coping potential** | Can I handle it? | Uncertain |
| **Normative significance** | Does it fit my values? | Yes (a threat is bad) |

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *captures* the *differentiation* of the *emotions* (the *fear* and the *anger* *differ* in the *appraisal* *pattern*), and it *fits* the *empirical* *literature*.

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*subpersonal*’* *objection***. The *appraisal* is *subpersonal* (the *process* is *automatic*, *fast*, and *not* *accessible* to the *agent*), and the *emotion* *requires* the *personal* *level* (the *conscious* *feeling*).

**The *reply***. The *appraisal* *has* a *personal* *level* (the *conscious* *evaluation*), and the *subpersonal* *process* *supports* the *personal* *evaluation*. The *reply* *relates* the *two* *levels*, and it is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*no* *phenomenology*’* *objection***. The *appraisal* *theory* *describes* the *evaluation* but *not* the *feeling*.

**The *reply***. The *feeling* is the *subjective* *aspect* of the *appraisal* (the *appraisal* *is* *felt*), and the *reply* *connects* the *appraisal* to the *feeling*.

### 1.5 The enactive theory

**The *thesis*** (Colombetti, *The Feeling Body*, 2014; the *contemporary* *literature*). The *emotion* is a *dynamic* *pattern* of *embodied* *interaction* — a *self*-*organizing* *process* that *integrates* the *bodily* *changes*, the *appraisal*, and the *action* *tendency*.

**The *structure*.

$$
\text{Emotion}(E) = \text{Dynamic pattern of embodied interaction}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *avoids* the *reduction* of the *emotion* to a *single* *component* (the *feeling*, the *judgment*), and it *fits* the *phenomenology* of *emotion* (the *emotion* is a *whole*-*body* *response*, *oriented* *toward* *the* *world*).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*vagueness*’* *objection***. The *notion* of a *dynamic* *pattern* is *vague*, and the *theory* *does* not *explain* the *differentiation* of the *emotions*.

**The *reply***. The *pattern* *is* the *differentiation* (the *fear* and the *anger* *differ* in the *dynamics*), and the *theory* *fits* the *dynamical* *systems* *literature*. The *reply* is *promising* and *underdeveloped*.

**(ii) The *‘*hard* *problem*’* *objection***. The *theory* *explains* the *functional* *integration* but *not* the *phenomenal* *character* (Ch. 24, §2).

**The *reply***. The *phenomenal* *character* is the *lived* *body* (the *body* *as* *experienced*, not the *body* *as* *object*), and the *reply* *rests* on the *phenomenological* *tradition* (§7).

### 1.6 The perceptual theory

**The *thesis*** (de Sousa, *The Rationality of Emotion*, 1987; Tappolet, *Emotions, Values, and Agency*, 2016; Döring, “Seeing What to Do,” 2007). The *emotion* is a *perceptual* *experience* of an *evaluative* *property* — the *fear* *perceives* the *danger* (the *dangerousness*), and the *anger* *perceives* the *offense*.

**The *structure*.

$$
\text{Emotion}(E) = \text{Perception}(\text{the situation instantiates the evaluative property } F)
$$

**The *argument*.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The emotions are about objects (the intentionality).} \\
2. & \text{The emotions present the objects as having evaluative properties (the danger, the offense, the loss).} \\
3. & \text{The presentation is perceptual (the immediacy, the non-inferential character, the recalcitrance).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The emotions are perceptions of evaluative properties.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *explains* the *phenomenology* of *emotion* (the *immediacy*, the *passivity*, the *recalcitrance*), and it *connects* the *emotions* to the *theory* of *value* (Ch. 44, §6).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*no* *evaluative* *properties*’* *objection***. There are *no* *evaluative* *properties* (the *error* *theory*; Ch. 44, §3), and the *perception* *theory* *presupposes* *realism* about *value*.

**The *reply*.** The *theory* *can* *be* *combined* with a *response*-*dependence* *account* of *value* (the *evaluative* *properties* *are* *grounded* in the *emotional* *responses*), and the *combination* *is* *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*no* *perceptual* *organ*’* *objection***. The *emotions* *lack* a *sensory* *organ* and a *sensory* *field*, and the *perception* *model* *does* not *fit*.

**The *reply*.** The *perception* is *multimodal* (the *interoception*, the *proprioception*), and the *emotions* *use* the *bodily* *senses*. The *reply* *fits* the *predictive* *processing* *account* (§7).

### 1.7 The state of the debate

**The *assessment*.** The *theories* are *all* *live*, and *none* is *decisive*.

| Theory | The *core* *claim* | The *strength* | The *weakness* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Feeling (James)** | Emotion = perception of bodily changes | Fits the phenomenology | Undifferentiated |
| **Judgment (Nussbaum)** | Emotion = evaluative judgment | Fits the intentionality | Recalcitrance |
| **Appraisal (Scherer)** | Emotion = appraisal pattern | Fits the differentiation | The subpersonal level |
| **Enactive (Colombetti)** | Emotion = dynamic embodiment | Fits the holism | Vagueness |
| **Perceptual (Tappolet)** | Emotion = perception of value | Fits the immediacy | The evaluative properties |

**The *most* *defensible* *position*.** A *hybrid*: the *emotion* is a *perceptual* *appraisal* — a *perceptual* *experience* of an *evaluative* *property*, *realized* by a *dynamic* *pattern* of *bodily* *and* *cognitive* *processes*. The *position* *combines* the *strengths* of the *perceptual* and the *enactive* *theories*, and it is *incomplete*.

## 2. The Rationality of Emotion

### 2.1 The three questions

The *rationality* of *emotion* *raises* *three* *distinct* *questions*, and the *distinction* is *essential*.

| Question | The *form* | The *domain* |
|---|---|---|
| **Fittingness** | Is the emotion *appropriate* to its object? | The *relation* between the emotion and the world |
| **Warrant** | Is the emotion *justified* by the evidence? | The *epistemic* status of the emotion |
| **Coherence** | Is the emotion *consistent* with the agent's other states? | The *internal* structure of the mind |

**The *significance*.** The *three* *questions* *diverge*: an *emotion* can be *fitting* (the *danger* is *real*) but *unwarranted* (the *agent* *has* *no* *evidence*), and *warranted* (the *evidence* *supports* the *danger*) but *incoherent* (the *emotion* *conflicts* with the *agent*'s *values*).

### 2.2 Recalcitrant emotions

**The *phenomenon***. The *recalcitrant* *emotion* *persists* *despite* the *agent*'s *judgment* that it is *unwarranted*: the *fear* of *flying* *persists* while the *agent* *knows* that *flying* is *safe*.

**The *argument*** (the *contemporary* *literature*; Greenspan 1988; D'Arms & Jacobson 2003).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The emotion persists against the agent's judgment.} \\
2. & \text{If the emotion were a judgment, the conflict would be a contradiction (the agent would judge both that the flight is safe and that it is dangerous).} \\
3. & \text{The conflict is not a contradiction (the agent does not believe the contradiction).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The emotion is not a judgment (the perceptual theory is better).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *consequence*.** The *argument* *supports* the *perceptual* *theory*: the *emotion* *presents* the *situation* *as* *dangerous*, and the *presentation* can *conflict* with the *judgment* without *contradiction* (the *perception* *presents* the *illusion*, and the *judgment* *corrects* it; Ch. 5, §2.1).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*no* *conflict*’* *objection***. The *agent* *does* *judge* the *flight* *dangerous* (the *judgment* is *implicit*, *unconscious*, or *“*in* the *gut”*), and the *conflict* is between *two* *judgments*.

**The *reply***. The *implicit* *judgment* is *not* a *full*-*fledged* *judgment* (it is a *representation* *below* the *level* of *belief*), and the *reply* *blurs* the *distinction* between *judgment* and *perception*. The *reply* is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*rational* *recalcitrance*’* *objection***. The *recalcitrant* *emotion* can be *rational* (the *fear* *persists* *because* the *risk* is *real*, even if the *agent* *judges* it *low*), and the *rationality* *undermines* the *perceptual* *model* (the *perception* is *not* *corrected* by *evidence*).

**The *reply***. The *recalcitrance* is a *feature* of *perception* (the *illusion* *persists* despite the *judgment*), and the *reply* *supports* the *perceptual* *model*. The *reply* is *contested*.

### 2.3 The fittingness of emotion

**The *thesis*** (D'Arms & Jacobson, “Sentiment and Value,” 2000; *the* *contemporary* *literature*). The *fittingness* of an *emotion* is a *sui* *generis* *evaluative* *property*: the *emotion* *fits* its *object* when the *object* *has* the *evaluative* *property* that the *emotion* *presents* (the *fear* *fits* the *danger*, the *grief* *fits* the *loss*).

**The *structure*.

$$
\text{Emotion } E \text{ fits } O \quad \text{iff} \quad O \text{ has the evaluative property that } E \text{ presents}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *notion* *grounds* the *rationality* of *emotion* in an *evaluative* *relation* (not in *truth* or *consistency*), and it *avoids* the *reduction* of the *rationality* to the *belief*-*desire* *coherence*.

**The *objection* (the *wrong* *kind* *of* *reason*; D'Arms & Jacobson 2000, §3). The *fittingness* can be *manipulated*: a *reason* can *make* the *emotion* *fitting* *without* *making* it *appropriate* (the *“*malevolent*”* *demon* who *rewards* *fear*; the *fear* is *fitting* to the *demon*'s *threat*, but the *fear* is *not* *appropriate*). The *objection* *shows* that the *fittingness* is *not* *sufficient* for the *rationality*, and the *theory* *needs* a *further* *condition* (the *“*right* *kind* *of* *reason*”*).

**The *reply***. The *appropriate* *emotion* is the one that *responds* to the *value* (the *danger*), not to the *reward* (the *demon*'s *threat*), and the *distinction* is *grounded* in the *value* *itself*. The *reply* *presupposes* a *theory* of *value* (Ch. 44), and the *reply* is *contested*.

### 2.4 The fact/value structure of the emotions

**The *thesis***. The *emotions* *present* the *world* *as* *having* *evaluative* *properties*, and the *presentation* *is* *entangled* with the *descriptive* *properties* (Ch. 44, §7).

**The *argument* (the *contemporary* *literature*; the *“*thick*”* *concepts*; Ch. 44, §7).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The emotion presents the object as having a thick evaluative property (the danger, the offense, the cruelty).} \\
2. & \text{The thick property is descriptive and evaluative (it describes the situation and evaluates it).} \\
3. & \text{The entanglement is constitutive of the presentation.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The emotions present a fact-value entangled world.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *captures* the *phenomenology* of *emotion* (the *world* *appears* *value*-*laden*, not *value*-*neutral*), and it *fits* the *contemporary* *literature* on *thick* *concepts* (Ch. 44, §7).

**The *objection*.** The *view* *slides* into *non*-*cognitivism* or *expressivism* (the *emotions* *project* value onto the *world*; Ch. 44, §3), and the *slide* *undermines* the *objectivity* of *value*.

**The *reply***. The *emotions* *disclose* the *evaluative* *properties* (they are *perceptual* *apprehensions* of *real* *value*), and the *reply* *supports* *realism* about *value* (Ch. 44, §2). The *reply* is *contested*.

### 2.5 The *emotions* and *moral* *knowledge*

**The *argument***. If the *emotions* are *perceptions* of *evaluative* *properties*, they are a *source* of *moral* *knowledge* (the *moral* *perception*; Ch. 44, §6).

**The *structure*.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The emotions present evaluative properties (the danger, the cruelty).} \\
2. & \text{The evaluative properties ground the moral judgments.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The emotions are a source of moral knowledge.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *grounds* *moral* *knowledge* in the *affective* *experience*, and it *fits* the *phenomenology* of *moral* *judgment* (the *revulsion* at *cruelty*, the *admiration* at *courage*).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*disagreement*’* *objection***. The *emotions* *disagree* (the *same* *situation* *elicits* *different* *emotions* in *different* *people*), and the *disagreement* *undermines* the *claim* to *knowledge*.

**The *reply***. The *perception* can be *mistaken* (the *illusions*; Ch. 5, §2), and the *disagreement* *requires* a *calibration* (the *reflective* *equilibrium*; Ch. 3, §4). The *reply* *weakens* the *claim*, and it is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*contamination*’* *objection***. The *emotions* are *shaped* by *bias*, *culture*, and *manipulation*, and the *shaping* *undermines* the *claim* to *knowledge* (the *debunking* *argument*; Ch. 3, §5).

**The *reply***. The *shaping* *affects* *all* *perception* (the *theory*-*ladenness*), and the *emotions* are *not* *uniquely* *contaminated*. The *reply* *relativizes* the *objection*, and it is *contested*.

### 2.6 The state of the debate

**The *assessment*.** The *rationality* of *emotion* is *unresolved*, and the *most* *defensible* *position* is a *qualified* *perceptualism*: the *emotions* *present* *evaluative* *properties* (the *fittingness*), and the *presentation* *is* *subject* to *calibration* and *correction* (the *warrant*), *qualified* by the *admission* that the *calibration* is *hard* and *contested*.

## 3. Imagination and Pretense

### 3.1 The varieties of imagination

**The *distinction***. The *imagination* is *not* a *single* *capacity*; it is a *family* of *capacities*, and the *family* *includes*.

| Kind | The *definition* | The *example* |
|---|---|---|
| **Sensory imagination** | The *imagery* (the *visual*, the *auditory*) | The *image* of an *apple* |
| **Propositional imagination** | The *representation* of a *possibility* | The *imagination* that *there* is a *cat* on the *mat* |
| **Dramatic imagination** | The *imagination* of a *situation* *from* the *inside* | The *imagination* of *being* *in* a *battle* |
| **Motor imagination** | The *imagination* of an *action* | The *imagination* of *swinging* a *bat* |
| **Affective imagination** | The *imagination* of an *emotion* | The *imagination* of *grief* |

**The *significance*.** The *varieties* *differ* in their *content* and their *role*, and the *theories* of *imagination* *must* *account* for the *differences*.

### 3.2 Pretense and make-believe

**The *thesis*** (Walton, *Mimesis as Make-Believe*, 1990; the *contemporary* *literature*). The *pretense* is a *game* of *make*-*believe*, in which *props* (the *objects*, the *texts*) *generate* *fictional* *truths* *according* to *principles* of *generation*.

**The *structure*.

| Element | The *role* | The *example* |
|---|---|---|
| **Prop** | The *object* that *generates* the *fiction* | The *doll*, the *text*, the *painting* |
| **Principle of generation** | The *rule* that *determines* the *fictional* *truths* | “The *doll* is a *baby*” |
| **Game** | The *practice* of *make*-*believe* | The *children*'s *play* |

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *explains* the *fiction* (the *truth* *in* the *fiction*; Ch. 49, §4) and the *imagination* (the *participation* in the *game*), and it *fits* the *ordinary* *practice* of *pretense*.

**The *objection*.** The *theory* *makes* the *imagination* *derivative* of the *fiction* (the *fiction* *comes* *first*, and the *imagination* *follows*), and the *priority* is *contested* (the *imagination* *may* be *prior*; the *contemporary* *literature*).

### 3.3 Imaginative resistance

**The *phenomenon*** (Gendler, “The Puzzle of Imaginative Resistance,” 2000; Moran, “The Expression of Feeling in Imagination,” 1994; Weatherson, “Morality, Fiction, and Possibility,” 2004). The *reader* *can* *imagine* *that* the *world* is *different* (the *talking* *animals*, the *magic*), but the *reader* *resists* *imagining* *that* the *morally* *repugnant* is *true* (the *narrator* *who* *says* that *slavery* is *just*, and the *reader* *refuses* to *accept* the *fiction*).

**The *argument*.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The reader accepts the fiction (the world is different, the talking animals).} \\
2. & \text{The reader resists the moral fiction (the slavery is just).} \\
3. & \text{The resistance requires an explanation.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The imagination is constrained by the moral judgment (the resistance shows the constraint).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *explanations*.

**(i) The *cantian* *explanation*** (Gendler 2000). The *reader* *cannot* *imagine* the *moral* *fiction* (the *imagination* is *not* *free*; the *moral* *judgment* *blocks* the *imagination*).

**(ii) The *wontian* *explanation*** (Gendler 2000). The *reader* *will* *not* *imagine* the *moral* *fiction* (the *imagination* is *free*, but the *reader* *refuses* to *engage*).

**(iii) The *conditional* *explanation*** (Weatherson 2004). The *reader* *can* *imagine* the *moral* *fiction* *if* the *fiction* *is* *presented* *differently* (the *reader* *resists* the *narrator*'s *evaluation*, not the *fiction* *itself*).

**The *significance*.** The *debate* *illuminates* the *nature* of the *imagination* (the *relation* between the *fiction* and the *belief*), and it *connects* to the *theory* of *fiction* (Ch. 49, §4) and to *moral* *psychology* (Ch. 46, §4).

### 3.4 Simulation and mindreading

**The *question***. How do we *understand* the *minds* of *others* (Ch. 29, §1.5)?

**The *positions*.

| Position | The *claim* | The *proponent* |
|---|---|---|
| **Theory-theory** | We *use* a *folk*-*psychological* *theory* to *infer* the *mental* *states* of *others* | the *classical* *cognitive* *science* *tradition* |
| **Simulation theory** | We *simulate* the *mental* *states* of *others* (we *put* ourselves *in* their *shoes*) | Gordon (1986); Goldman (2006) |
| **Hybrid** | We *use* both *theory* and *simulation* | the *contemporary* *literature* |

**The *argument* (simulation theory).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The understanding of others is fast and automatic.} \\
2. & \text{The speed suggests that we simulate the states (we run the same process offline).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The simulation explains the understanding.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *fits* the *phenomenology* of *empathy* (we *feel* what the *other* *feels*), and it *connects* to the *mirror*-*neuron* *literature* (the *contemporary* *literature*).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*egocentric*’* *objection***. The *simulation* *projects* our *own* *states* onto *others*, and the *projection* *is* *inaccurate* (the *others* *differ* from *us*).

**The *reply*.** The *simulation* is *calibrated* (we *adjust* for the *differences*), and the *adjustment* *corrects* the *egocentrism*. The *reply* is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*theoretical*’* *objection***. The *understanding* *requires* the *theory* (the *concepts* of *belief*, *desire*), and the *simulation* *presupposes* the *theory*. The *objection* *supports* the *hybrid* *view*.

### 3.5 The state of the debate

**The *assessment*.** The *imagination* *debate* is *unresolved*, and the *most* *defensible* *position* is a *pluralist* *view*: the *imagination* is a *family* of *capacities*, the *pretense* is a *game* of *make*-*believe* (Walton), the *resistance* is a *constraint* on the *imagination* (Gendler), and the *simulation* is one *mode* of *understanding* *others*.

## 4. The 4E Mind

### 4.1 The four Es

**The *thesis***. The *mind* is **embodied**, **embedded**, **enacted**, and **extended** (the *4E* *framework*).

| E | The *claim* | The *paradigm* | The *proponent* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Embodied** | The *body* *shapes* *cognition* | The *body* *schema*, the *sensorimotor* *contingencies* | Merleau-Ponty; Noë; Ch. 5, §5.1 |
| **Embedded** | The *environment* *supports* *cognition* | The *scaffolding*, the *environmental* *affordances* | Hutchins (1995); the *situated* *tradition* |
| **Enacted** | The *cognition* is *constituted* by *action* | The *sensorimotor* *contingencies*, the *active* *perception* | Noë (2004); Ch. 5, §5.1 |
| **Extended** | The *cognition* *extends* *beyond* the *skull* | The *notebook* (Otto), the *phone* | Clark & Chalmers (1998); Ch. 11, §5 |

**The *significance*.** The *framework* *challenges* the *classical* *conception* of the *mind* as a *disembodied* *computer*, and it *fits* the *phenomenological* *tradition* (§7).

### 4.2 The extended mind

**The *thesis*** (Clark & Chalmers, “The Extended Mind,” 1998). The *cognitive* *processes* *extend* *beyond* the *brain*: the *notebook*, the *phone*, the *prosthesis* *can* *constitute* *part* of the *cognitive* *system*.

**The *parity* *principle*** (Clark & Chalmers 1998, p. 8).

> “If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognising as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process.”

**The *argument*.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The notebook (Otto's) functions as a memory (it stores and retrieves the information).} \\
2. & \text{The function is the same as the memory (the information is accessible, reliable, and integrated into the agent's behavior).} \\
3. & \text{The function is the cognition (the functionalist premise).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The notebook is part of the cognitive system.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *grounds* the *cognitive* *status* of the *external* *scaffolding*, and it *fits* the *empirical* *literature* (the *distributed* *cognition*; Hutchins 1995).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*cognitive* *bloat*’* *objection*** (Rupert, “Challenges to the Hypothesis of Extended Cognition,” 2004). The *parity* *principle* *admits* *too* *many* *systems* (the *phone*, the *environment*, the *other* *people*), and the *admission* *bloats* the *cognitive* *system*.

**The *reply***. The *cognition* *requires* the *integration* (the *access*, the *reliability*, the *automatic* *endorsement*), and the *integration* *excludes* the *bloat*. The *reply* *qualifies* the *principle*, and it is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*coupling*–*constitution*’* *fallacy*** (Adams & Aizawa, *The Bounds of Cognition*, 2008). The *coupling* of the *brain* and the *notebook* *does* not *entail* that the *notebook* *constitutes* the *cognition* (the *coupling* is *causal*, not *constitutive*).

**The *reply***. The *constitution* *is* a *form* of *coupling* (the *functional* *integration*), and the *distinction* *between* the *causal* and the *constitutive* is *contested* (Ch. 21, §6). The *reply* is *contested*.

### 4.3 Predictive processing and the embodied mind

**The *thesis*** (the *predictive* *processing* *framework*; Ch. 5, §5.2; Clark, *Surfing Uncertainty*, 2016; Seth, *Being You*, 2021). The *mind* is a *prediction* *machine* that *minimizes* *prediction* *error*, and the *body* *shapes* the *predictions* (the *interoceptive* *inference*, the *active* *inference*).

**The *structure*.

| Element | The *role* | The *example* |
|---|---|---|
| **Generative model** | The *brain*'s *model* of the *world* *and* the *body* | The *body* *schema* |
| **Prediction error** | The *mismatch* between the *model* and the *input* | The *surprise* |
| **Active inference** | The *action* that *reduces* the *error* | The *reaching*, the *exploration* |

**The *virtue*.** The *framework* *unifies* the *perception*, the *action*, and the *embodiment*, and it *explains* the *interoception* (the *bodily* *feelings*; §7).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*hard* *problem*’* *objection***. The *framework* *explains* the *functional* *integration* but *not* the *phenomenal* *character* (Ch. 24, §5).

**The *reply***. The *phenomenal* *character* is the *content* of the *predictions* about the *body* (the *“*beast* *machine”*; Seth 2021), and the *reply* *identifies* the *phenomenal* with the *predictive* *content*. The *reply* is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*instrumentalist*’* *objection***. The *framework* is an *instrumentalist* *model* (it *predicts* the *behavior*, but it *does* not *describe* the *mechanism*), and the *instrumentalism* *undermines* the *metaphysical* *claim*.

**The *reply***. The *model* *is* a *description* of the *mechanism* (the *hierarchical* *prediction* *error* *minimization*), and the *reply* *fits* the *empirical* *literature*. The *reply* is *contested*.

### 4.4 The phenomenological contribution

**The *thesis*** (Merleau-Ponty, *Phenomenology of Perception*, 1945; Heidegger, *Being and Time*, 1927; the *contemporary* *literature*). The *body* is *not* an *object* in the *world*; it is the *medium* of our *access* to the *world* (the *lived* *body*, the *corps* *vécu*).

**The *argument*.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The body is experienced as the medium of perception (the “I can”; the motor intentionality).} \\
2. & \text{The medium is not an object (it is not observed, but inhabited).} \\
3. & \text{The lived body is the condition of the experience (the “body schema”).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The body is constitutive of the experience (not a contingent vehicle).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *captures* the *phenomenology* of *embodiment* (the *body* *as* *mine*, the *body* *as* the *medium* of *action*), and it *challenges* the *Cartesian* *dualism* (Ch. 23, §2).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*first*–*person*’* *objection***. The *phenomenological* *description* is *first*-*personal*, and the *first*–*personal* *data* are *not* *intersubjectively* *checkable* (Ch. 3, §6.1).

**The *reply***. The *phenomenology* *constrains* the *theory* (the *theory* *must* *accommodate* the *experience*), and the *constraint* is *not* a *proof* but a *condition* of *adequacy*. The *reply* is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*scientific*’* *objection***. The *phenomenology* is *not* a *science*, and the *philosophy* *should* *follow* the *empirical* *sciences*.

**The *reply***. The *phenomenology* *and* the *science* are *complementary* (the *phenomenology* *describes* the *experience*, and the *science* *explains* the *mechanism*), and the *complementarity* is *defensible* (Ch. 3, §6.4).

### 4.5 The state of the debate

**The *assessment*.** The *4E* *framework* is *influential*, and the *most* *defensible* *position* is a *qualified* *embodiment*: the *body* *shapes* the *cognition* (the *embodied* *insight*), the *environment* *scaffolds* the *cognition* (the *embedded* *insight*), and the *cognition* *extends* *beyond* the *skull* *in* the *cases* of *reliable* *integration* (the *extended* *insight*), *qualified* by the *admission* that the *extension* is *not* *universal* and the *boundary* is *contested*.

## 5. Skill, Expertise, and Know-How

### 5.1 Ryle's regress

**The *thesis*** (Ryle, *The Concept of Mind*, 1949, Ch. 2). The *know*-*how* is *not* a *form* of *know*-*that*: the *skill* is a *capacity* that is *exercised* in *practice*, and the *capacity* *cannot* be *reduced* to *propositional* *knowledge*.

**The *argument* (the *Rylean* *regress*).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{If know-how were know-that, the exercise of the skill would require the prior knowledge of the rules.} \\
2. & \text{The knowledge of the rules would require the knowledge of the rules for applying the rules.} \\
3. & \text{The regress is infinite.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The know-how is not know-that (it is primitive).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *argument* *grounds* the *skill* in *practice*, and it *fits* the *phenomenology* of *expertise* (the *expert* *acts* *without* *deliberating*).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*regress* *is* *not* *vicious*’* *objection***. The *regress* can *terminate* in a *basic* *level* of *know*-*how*, and the *regress* is *not* a *refutation* of the *intellectualism* (the *intellectualism* *can* *accept* a *basic* *level*; Stanley & Williamson 2001, §5).

**(ii) The *‘*expertise* *requires* *knowledge*’* *objection***. The *expert* *knows* the *rules* (the *chess* *master* *knows* the *openings*), and the *knowledge* *is* *propositional*. The *objection* *supports* the *intellectualism*.

### 5.2 Intellectualism

**The *thesis*** (Stanley & Williamson, “Knowing How,” 2001; Stanley, *Know How*, 2011). The *know*-*how* is a *species* of *know*-*that*: *knowing* *how* to $\phi$ is *knowing* a *way* to $\phi$ (a *proposition* *under* a *practical* *mode* of *presentation*).

**The *structure*.

$$
\text{Know-how}(A, \phi) = \exists w\, \mathrm{Know}(A, \langle w \text{ is a way for } A \text{ to } \phi \rangle, \text{practical mode})
$$

**The *argument* (Stanley & Williamson 2001, §2).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The know-how ascribes a relation between the agent and a way of acting (the linguistic data: “Hannah knows how to ride a bicycle”).} \\
2. & \text{The relation is a form of know-that (the propositional knowledge of the way).} \\
3. & \text{The practical mode of presentation distinguishes the know-how from the ordinary know-that.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The know-how is a species of know-that.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *explains* the *intelligence* of the *skill* (the *skill* *is* *guided* by *knowledge*), and it *avoids* the *mystery* of the *primitive* *know*-*how*.

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*non*-*propositional* *content*’* *objection* (Fridland, “Problems with Intellectualism,” 2013; the *contemporary* *literature*). The *skill* *involves* a *non*-*propositional* *content* (the *motor* *representation*, the *sensorimotor* *contingencies*), and the *content* is *not* *propositional*.

**The *reply***. The *motor* *representation* is a *form* of *knowledge* (the *practical* *mode*), and the *reply* *accommodates* the *non*-*propositional* *content* within the *intellectualist* *framework*. The *reply* is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*animal* *knowledge*’* *objection***. The *animals* *know* *how* to *do* *things* (the *dog* *knows* *how* to *fetch*), and the *animals* *lack* *propositional* *knowledge*. The *objection* *undermines* the *intellectualism*.

**The *reply***. The *animal* *knowledge* is a *form* of *know*-*how* that is *not* *propositional* (the *intellectualism* *applies* to the *human* *case*), and the *reply* *limits* the *theory*. The *reply* is *contested*.

### 5.3 The *knowing*-*how*/*knowing*-*that* *distinction* *and* *the* *sciences*

**The *question***. Are the *sciences* *forms* of *know*-*that* (the *theories*) or *know*-*how* (the *practices*)?

**The *answer* (the *contemporary* *literature*). The *sciences* *involve* *both*: the *theories* (the *know*-*that*) and the *practices* (the *know*-*how*; the *experimental* *skills*, the *instrumental* *techniques*). The *distinction* *structures* the *philosophy* of *science* (Ch. 38–40), and the *integration* is *contested*.

### 5.4 The state of the debate

**The *assessment*.** The *know*-*how* *debate* is *unresolved*, and the *most* *defensible* *position* is a *pluralist* *view*: the *know*-*how* *involves* *propositional* *knowledge* (the *intellectualist* *insight*), *non*-*propositional* *content* (the *anti*-*intellectualist* *insight*), and *practical* *skill* (the *Rylean* *insight*), and the *combination* is *the* *best* *account*.

## 6. Attention and the Subject

### 6.1 The varieties of attention

**The *distinction***. The *attention* is *not* a *single* *capacity*; it is a *family* of *mechanisms*.

| Kind | The *definition* | The *example* |
|---|---|---|
| **Selective attention** | The *focus* on a *subset* of the *stimuli* | The *cocktail*-*party* *effect* |
| **Sustained attention** | The *maintenance* of the *focus* *over* *time* | The *vigilance* *task* |
| **Executive attention** | The *control* of the *focus* (the *top*-*down* *selection*) | The *Stroop* *task* |
| **Mindfulness** | The *non*-*judgmental* *awareness* of the *present* | The *meditative* *practice* (§6.3) |

**The *significance*.** The *attention* *structures* the *experience* (the *attention* *selects* the *content* of *consciousness*), and the *structure* *connects* the *attention* to the *theory* of *consciousness* (Ch. 24, §4).

### 6.2 Attention and consciousness

**The *question***. Is the *attention* *necessary* or *sufficient* for *consciousness*?

**The *positions*.

| Position | The *claim* | The *proponent* |
|---|---|---|
| **Attention is necessary** | The *consciousness* *requires* the *attention* | the *global* *workspace* *tradition* |
| **Attention is sufficient** | The *attention* *produces* the *consciousness* | the *higher*-*order* *tradition* |
| **Attention is neither** | The *consciousness* *overflows* the *attention* | Block (2007); Ch. 24, §1.3 |

**The *argument* (the *attention*-*as*-*necessary* *view*).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The unattended stimuli are not consciously experienced (the inattentional blindness; the change blindness).} \\
2. & \text{The unattended stimuli are processed only locally (the local workspace).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The attention is necessary for consciousness.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *fits* the *empirical* *literature* (the *inattentional* *blindness*), and it *grounds* the *consciousness* in the *global* *access*.

**The *objection* (the *overflow* *debate*; Block 2007; the *critique* in Cohen & Dennett 2011). The *phenomenal* *consciousness* *overflows* the *attention* (the *richness* of the *experience*), and the *overflow* *undermines* the *necessary* *condition*. The *objection* is *contested* (Ch. 24, §1.3).

### 6.3 Mindfulness and contemplative attention

**The *thesis*** (the *Buddhist* *tradition*; the *contemporary* *literature*; Ch. 24, §7). The *mindfulness* is a *cultivated* *form* of *attention* (the *non*-*judgmental* *awareness* of the *present* *experience*), and the *practice* *produces* *data* about the *nature* of *attention* and *consciousness*.

**The *structure* (the *Buddhist* *distinction*).

| Practice | The *aim* | The *effect* |
|---|---|---|
| **Samatha** (calm abiding) | The *concentration* on a *single* *object* | The *stability* of *attention* |
| **Vipassanā** (insight) | The *observation* of the *changing* *experience* | The *insight* into the *impermanence* |

**The *argument*.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The meditative practice cultivates the attention (the empirical premise).} \\
2. & \text{The cultivation produces reports about the experience (the phenomenological data).} \\
3. & \text{The reports constrain the theory of attention and consciousness.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The contemplative data are relevant to the philosophy of mind.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *opens* the *philosophy* to the *first*-*person* *methods* (Ch. 3, §6.1), and it *connects* the *attention* to the *ethics* of *attention* (the *contemporary* *literature*).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*first*–*person*’* *objection***. The *data* are *first*-*personal*, and the *data* are *not* *intersubjectively* *checkable* (Ch. 3, §6.1).

**The *reply***. The *data* are *constrained* by the *empirical* *research* (the *neural* *correlates*; the *behavioral* *measures*), and the *constraint* *grounds* the *reliability*. The *reply* is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*therapeutic*’* *objection***. The *mindfulness* is a *therapeutic* *practice*, not a *philosophical* *method*, and the *practice* *does* not *produce* *knowledge*.

**The *reply***. The *practice* *produces* *data* about the *nature* of *attention* (the *phenomenological* *reports*), and the *data* are *relevant* to the *philosophy* (the *descriptions* of *experience*). The *reply* is *contested*.

### 6.4 The metaphysics of the experiencing subject

**The *question***. What *is* the *subject* of *experience* (Ch. 26, §1.1)?

**The *theses*.

| Position | The *claim* | The *proponent* |
|---|---|---|
| **Substance** | The *subject* is a *substance* | Descartes; Ch. 26, §2 |
| **Bundle** | The *subject* is a *bundle* of experiences | Hume; Ch. 26, §3.1 |
| **Self*-*model** | The *subject* is a *model* the *brain* *constructs* | Metzinger (2003) |
| **Minimal self** | The *subject* is the *minimal* *self*-*awareness* | Gallagher (2000); the *contemporary* *literature* |

**The *argument* (the *self*-*model* *theory*).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The brain constructs a model of the organism (the self-model).} \\
2. & \text{The model represents the organism as a subject (the “transparent” self-model).} \\
3. & \text{The transparency makes the model invisible (we experience the world through the model).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The subject is a model (a real pattern, not a substance).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *fits* the *empirical* *literature* (the *split*-*brain* *cases*, the *“*self*”-*related* *neural* *activity*), and it *avoids* the *mystery* of the *substance*.

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*hard* *problem*’* *objection***. The *model* *explains* the *self*-*representation* but not the *phenomenal* *character* (Ch. 24, §2).

**The *reply***. The *phenomenal* *character* is the *content* of the *self*-*model* (the *“*what* *it* *is* *like”* *is* the *model*'s *content*), and the *reply* *identifies* the *two*. The *reply* is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*no* *subject*’* *objection***. If the *subject* is a *model*, there is *no* *subject* (the *model* is a *representation*, not a *thing*), and the *view* *collapses* into *eliminativism* (Ch. 23, §6).

**The *reply***. The *model* is a *real* *pattern* (it *has* *causal* *efficacy*, and it *grounds* the *behavior*), and the *reply* *preserves* the *subject* as a *construct*. The *reply* is *contested*.

### 6.5 The state of the debate

**The *assessment*.** The *attention* and the *subject* *debate* is *unresolved*, and the *most* *defensible* *position* is a *qualified* *self*-*model* *theory*: the *subject* is a *constructed* *model* (the *contemporary* *insight*), *grounded* in the *body* and the *attention* (the *embodied* *insight*), and *irreducible* to the *third*-*personal* *description* (the *phenomenological* *insight*).

## 7. Perception, Affect, and the Lived World

### 7.1 The affective background

**The *thesis*** (Ratcliffe, *Feelings of Being*, 2008; the *contemporary* *phenomenological* *literature*). The *experience* is *not* *affectively* *neutral*: the *world* *appears* *through* an *affective* *background* (the *“*feeling* of *being”*), and the *background* *shapes* the *perception* and the *cognition*.

**The *argument*.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The experience is always affectively toned (the mood, the feeling).} \\
2. & \text{The tone shapes the content of the experience (the world appears threatening, inviting, flat).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The affect is constitutive of the experience (not an add-on).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *captures* the *phenomenology* of *mood* (the *depressed* *world*, the *anxious* *world*, the *joyful* *world*), and it *challenges* the *classical* *separation* of the *cognitive* and the *affective*.

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*phenomenological* *fallacy*’* *objection*** (Ch. 3, §6.1). The *description* of the *lived* *world* is *first*-*personal*, and the *description* is *not* *intersubjectively* *checkable*.

**The *reply***. The *description* is *constrained* by the *empirical* *research* (the *depression* *literature*, the *anxiety* *literature*), and the *constraint* *grounds* the *description*. The *reply* is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*no* *content*’* *objection***. The *affective* *background* is a *feeling*, not a *representation*, and the *feeling* *does* not *have* *content*.

**The *reply***. The *affective* *background* *has* *content* (it *represents* the *world* *as* *having* *evaluative* *properties*), and the *reply* *fits* the *perceptual* *theory* of *emotion* (§1.6). The *reply* is *contested*.

### 7.2 Predictive processing and affect

**The *thesis*** (Seth, *Being You*, 2021; Barrett, *How Emotions Are Made*, 2017; Clark 2016). The *affect* is the *interoceptive* *inference*: the *brain* *predicts* the *internal* *state* of the *body*, and the *prediction* *error* *is* the *feeling*.

**The *structure*.

| Element | The *role* | The *example* |
|---|---|---|
| **Interoception** | The *perception* of the *internal* *state* | The *heartbeat*, the *breathing* |
| **Prediction** | The *brain*'s *model* of the *internal* *state* | The *expected* *heart* *rate* |
| **Core affect** | The *valence* and *arousal* | The *pleasant*/*unpleasant*; the *calm*/*activated* |

**The *argument*.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The brain predicts the internal state (the interoceptive inference).} \\
2. & \text{The prediction error is experienced as affect (the valence, the arousal).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The affect is the content of the interoceptive prediction}.
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *grounds* the *affect* in the *body*, and it *fits* the *empirical* *literature* (the *interoception*; the *core* *affect*).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*construction*’* *objection*** (Barrett 2017). The *emotions* are *constructed* by the *conceptual* *categories* (the *fear* is a *construction* of the *core* *affect* and the *concept*), and the *construction* *undermines* the *basic*-*emotion* *view*. The *objection* is *contested* (the *basic*-*emotion* *tradition*; Ekman).

**(ii) The *‘*hard* *problem*’* *objection***. The *theory* *explains* the *functional* *integration* but not the *phenomenal* *character* (Ch. 24, §2). The *reply* is *contested*.

### 7.3 The shape of the lived world

**The *thesis***. The *world* *appears* *differently* to *different* *affective* *states*, and the *difference* is *not* a *mere* *coloring* but a *structural* *transformation*.

**The *examples*.

| State | The *world* | The *structure* |
|---|---|---|
| **Depression** | The *world* is *flat*, *gray*, *meaningless* | The *affordances* *disappear*; the *future* *closes* |
| **Anxiety** | The *world* is *threatening*, *unpredictable* | The *possibilities* *multiply*; the *salience* *shifts* |
| **Joy** | The *world* is *inviting*, *abundant* | The *affordances* *expand*; the *future* *opens* |
| **Grief** | The *world* is *empty*, *diminished* | The *loss* *shapes* the *perception* of the *surroundings* |

**The *argument*.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The affective states change the perception of the world (the empirical literature).} \\
2. & \text{The change is structural (the affordances, the salience, the future).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The affect shapes the lived world (it is constitutive of the experience).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *connects* the *philosophy* of *emotion* to the *philosophy* of *perception* (Ch. 5) and to the *phenomenology* (Ch. 30, §4.4), and it *fits* the *clinical* *literature* (the *depression*, the *anxiety*).

**The *objections*.

**(i) The *‘*causal*’* *objection***. The *affect* *causes* the *perception* *to* *change*, but it *does* not *constitute* it, and the *distinction* *is* *constitutive*.

**The *reply***. The *distinction* *between* the *causal* and the *constitutive* is *contested* (Ch. 21, §6), and the *affect* *may* be *constitutive* of the *experience* (the *perceptual* *theory*; §1.6). The *reply* is *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘*no* *content*’* *objection***. The *affective* *background* is a *feeling*, not a *representation*, and the *feeling* *does* not *have* *content*.

**The *reply*** (the *perceptual* *theory*). The *feeling* *has* *content* (it *presents* the *world* *as* *having* *evaluative* *properties*), and the *reply* *grounds* the *content* in the *perception*. The *reply* is *contested*.

### 7.4 The state of the debate

**The *assessment*.** The *affect* and the *lived* *world* *debate* is *unresolved*, and the *most* *defensible* *position* is a *qualified* *perceptualism*: the *affect* *presents* the *world* *as* *having* *evaluative* *properties* (the *perceptual* *insight*), and the *presentation* is *shaped* by the *body* (the *embodied* *insight*), *qualified* by the *admission* that the *content* is *not* *fully* *specified*.

### The hinge

The *chapter* — and *Part IV* — *ends* with a *hinge*. > “The world is not an object such that I have in my possession the law of its making.”

**The *application*.** The *mind* is *not* an *object* such that we can *specify* the *law* of its *making*; it is the *medium* *through* which the *world* *appears*, and the *medium* is *constitutive* of the *appearance*.

**The *closing* *thought*.** The *philosophy* of *mind* is *not* a *branch* of *philosophy*; it is the *place* where the *metaphysical*, the *epistemological*, and the *ethical* *questions* *meet*. The *next* *part* *begins* from *this* *meeting*, and it *asks* *what* *language* *must* be *like* for the *meeting* to be *possible*.

## The State of the Debate

### What is settled

- **The *theories* of *emotion* are *all* *live*.** The *feeling*, *judgment*, *appraisal*, *enactive*, and *perceptual* *theories* are *all* *defended*, and *none* is *decisive*.
- **The *recalcitrant* *emotion* *is* a *central* *datum*.** The *phenomenon* *supports* the *perceptual* *theory*, and it *undermines* the *judgment* *theory*.
- **The *fittingness* of *emotion* is a *sui* *generis* *evaluative* *property*.** The *notion* is *central* to the *rationality* of *emotion*, and it is *contested* (the *wrong* *kind* *of* *reason*).
- **The *imagination* is a *family* of *capacities*.** The *sensory*, *propositional*, *dramatic*, *motor*, and *affective* *varieties* are *distinct*.
- **The *imaginative* *resistance* is *real*.** The *phenomenon* is *documented*, and the *explanation* is *contested* (the *cantian*, the *wontian*, the *conditional*).
- **The *4E* *framework* is *influential*.** The *embodied*, *embedded*, *enacted*, and *extended* *theses* are *all* *defended*, and the *extension* is *contested*.
- **The *know*-*how* *debate* is *unresolved*.** The *Rylean* *regress* and the *intellectualist* *reply* are *both* *serious*.
- **The *self*-*model* *theory* is *influential*.** The *account* *fits* the *empirical* *literature*, and it *faces* the *hard* *problem*.
- **The *affective* *background* *shapes* the *lived* *world*.** The *phenomenon* is *documented* (the *depression*, the *anxiety*), and the *metaphysical* *interpretation* is *contested*.

### What is open

- **The *nature* of *emotion*.** Is it a *feeling*, a *judgment*, an *appraisal*, an *enactment*, or a *perception*?
- **The *rationality* of *emotion*.** Is the *emotion* *justified* by the *evidence*, or by the *fittingness*?
- **The *nature* of the *imagination*.** Is it a *single* *capacity* or a *family*?
- **The *explanation* of *imaginative* *resistance*.** The *cantian*, the *wontian*, and the *conditional* *explanations* are *contested*.

- **The *structure* of the *lived* *world*.** Is the *affect* *constitutive* of the *experience*, or a *causal* *influence*?
