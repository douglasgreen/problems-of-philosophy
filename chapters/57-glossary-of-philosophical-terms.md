# Appendix B — Glossary of Philosophical Terms

**Conventions.** Each entry gives the *standard technical sense* — what the term means in the literature, not an endorsement of any view. Parenthetical references give the chapter where the term is introduced or chiefly used. A right arrow (→) points to a related entry. Entries marked ***Misuse:*** flag the confusion the term most often attracts in student writing, in the popular press, or in adjacent disciplines.

**A caution.** Philosophical terms are *tools*, not decorations. The most common failure in philosophical writing is not ignorance of a term but the use of a term with a technical air while meaning something looser. When in doubt, define the term in your own words in a footnote — and notice whether the argument still works.

---

### A

**abduction** (*Ch. 10*) — Inference to the best explanation: inferring the hypothesis that would, if true, best explain the data. Ampliative, not deductive; distinct from enumerative induction, though often lumped with it. → inference to the best explanation. ***Misuse:*** treating abduction as a licence to assert whatever story one finds satisfying, without comparing rivals.

**Abhidharma** (*Ch. 15*) — The Buddhist scholastic project of analysing experience into its constituent *dharmas* (momentary factors), rejecting substance in favour of a process-ontology. The most sustained ancient exercise in revisionary mereology.

**absolutism, moral** (*Ch. 44*) — The view that some moral prohibitions hold without exception. ***Misuse:*** equated with "having strong moral views," which is not a metaethical position at all.

**abstract object** (*Ch. 15*) — An entity with no spatiotemporal location and no causal powers: numbers, sets, propositions, universals (on some views). Contrast → concrete. ***Misuse:*** "abstract" as a synonym for "vague" or "theoretical."

**access consciousness** (*Ch. 24*) — Block's term for a state's being poised for use in reasoning, report, and the control of behaviour. Contrast → phenomenal consciousness. The distinction is what makes the "hard problem" hard to state: access is functionally explicable, phenomenal character arguably is not.

**accident, ontological** (*Ch. 15*) — A property a thing has but could lack, as opposed to its → essence.

**acquaintance** (*Ch. 12*) — Russell's knowledge *of* things, by direct presentation, as opposed to knowledge *that* something is the case or knowledge by description. Russell held that we are acquainted only with sense-data, memories, and (perhaps) the self.

**act/omission distinction** (*Ch. 51*) — The distinction between doing and allowing. Killing vs. letting die. Defended by some deontologists, doubted by consequentialists, and central to medical ethics. ***Misuse:*** assuming the distinction has a single canonical formulation; in fact it fragments into several.

**act utilitarianism** (*Ch. 45*) — The view that each act is right iff it maximizes aggregate welfare. Contrast → rule utilitarianism; both are species of → consequentialism.

**ad hoc** (*Ch. 38*) — Of a hypothesis: introduced only to save a theory from a specific counterexample, with no independent support. Ad hocness is a matter of degree and of explanatory integration, not a simple property. ***Misuse:*** "ad hoc" as a general term of abuse for any auxiliary hypothesis.

**ad hominem** (*Ch. 4*) — Arguing against a claim by attacking its source. Formally a fallacy; but testimony-based and credibility-based arguments can legitimately consider the source, so not every reference to a speaker's standing is fallacious.

**adverbialism** (*Ch. 5*) — The view that undergoing an experience is a *way of sensing* ("sensing redly"), not a relation to a mental object. A response to → sense-datum theory that avoids positing private objects.

**aesthetic judgement** (*Ch. 49*) — Judgement of beauty or artistic merit. Kant held it to be subjective yet universal, disinterested yet normative; the combination remains the central puzzle.

**a fortiori** (*Ch. 2*) — "With stronger reason": if a claim holds in a weak case, it holds in a stronger one.

**agent causation** (*Ch. 27*) — The libertarian view that persons (substances), not events, are the causes of free actions. Contrast event-causal libertarianism. → incompatibilism.

**agnosticism** (*Ch. 50*) — Suspension of judgement on the existence of God. Huxley's coinage. ***Misuse:*** "agnostic" as a synonym for "atheist but polite"; the positions differ.

**akrasia** (*Ch. 28*) — Weakness of will: acting against one's own better judgement, knowingly. ***Misuse:*** extending the term to any failure of self-control, including cases of compulsion where no judgement is contravened.

**ampliative inference** (*Ch. 2*) — Inference whose conclusion goes beyond what the premises entail: induction, abduction, statistical inference. Contrast → deductive validity.

**anattā** (*Ch. 26*) — The Buddhist doctrine of non-self: no persisting substantial self is found on analysis, only a stream of conditioned events. Not nihilism about persons, but a → no-self view with an ethical rationale.

**anicca** (*Ch. 17*) — Impermanence: all conditioned things arise and pass away. Metaphysically a process view of reality; psychologically the ground of the claim that clinging causes suffering.

**anomalous monism** (*Ch. 28*) — Davidson's view: mental events are identical with physical events, but mental descriptions are not governed by strict laws, so there is no psychophysical law. Attempts to keep → mental causation without → reduction.

**anthropic principle** (*Ch. 50*) — The observation that observed features of the universe are constrained by the existence of observers. Used in fine-tuning arguments; its evidential force is disputed and depends heavily on how selection effects are modelled. ***Misuse:*** treating the principle as an explanation rather than as a constraint on what data are available.

**anti-realism** (*Ch. 22*) — In Dummett's sense: the view that truth is epistemically constrained, so that bivalence may fail for some domains. Contrast → realism. ***Misuse:*** the word covers at least three distinct positions (semantic, metaphysical, epistemic), and arguments for one rarely transfer.

**apoha** (*Ch. 31*) — The Buddhist theory (Dignāga, Dharmakīrti) that universals are exclusion-classes: a word applies by excluding what is other, not by naming a shared entity. A sophisticated → nominalism.

**aporia** (*Ch. 1*) — A genuine impasse: a problem in which equally strong arguments support incompatible answers. In Plato, sometimes a stage to be passed through.

**a priori / a posteriori** (*Ch. 9, 36*) — A distinction in *justification*: a priori knowledge is warranted independently of experience; a posteriori depends on experience. ***Misuse:*** (1) reading "a priori" as "before experience in time" (that would make it → innate); (2) assuming a priori knowledge concerns only definitions.

**argument** (*Ch. 2*) — A set of premises offered as support for a conclusion. ***Misuse:*** "argument" for a quarrel, or for any assertion of disagreement. Also: a premise set is not an argument until the support relation is stated.

**argument map** (*Ch. 2*) — A diagram of an argument's structure: premises, sub-conclusions, objections, and the inferential links between them.

**armchair method** (*Ch. 3*) — Inquiry conducted by reflection on concepts and cases rather than empirical investigation. ***Misuse:*** "armchair" as a slur implying no evidence is involved; conceptual competence is itself a kind of evidence, on many views.

**assertion** (*Ch. 34*) — A speech act that presents a proposition as true and undertakes responsibility for its warrant.

**atomism, logical** (*Ch. 31*) — The Russell–Wittgenstein view that complex propositions are truth-functions of independent atomic propositions. Undermined by holism about confirmation and by the failure to identify genuinely independent atoms.

**attributability** (*Ch. 27*) — The dimension of responsibility concerned with whether an action expresses the agent's evaluative stance, as distinct from accountability (liability to praise and blame). → moral responsibility.

**autonomy** (*Ch. 47*) — Self-governance: acting from reasons one endorses as one's own. ***Misuse:*** in bioethics, autonomy is often reduced to "patient choice," which is one application, not the concept.

**axiology** (*Ch. 44*) — The theory of value: what is good, bad, and neutral, and in virtue of what. Broader than ethics, since it includes aesthetic and prudential value.

### B

**basic belief** (*Ch. 8*) — In → foundationalism, a belief justified without deriving its justification from other beliefs. ***Misuse:*** assuming "basic" means "unshakeable" or "obvious"; it means only "not inferentially justified."

**Bayesianism** (*Ch. 10*) — The framework in which credences are probabilities updated by conditionalization on evidence: $P(H \mid E) = P(E \mid H)\,P(H) / P(E)$. Includes a *normative* claim (credences should be probabilities) and a *methodological* one (this is how evidence should change belief).

**begging the question** (*Ch. 4*) — Assuming the conclusion, or a premise that could only be justified by the conclusion. ***Misuse:*** "begs the question" for "raises the question" (a lost cause in popular usage, but not in philosophy); also, any argument with a premise someone disputes is *not* thereby question-begging.

**behaviourism, logical** (*Ch. 23*) — The view that statements about mental states are analysable into statements about behavioural dispositions. Its failure to handle occurrent thoughts and unmanifested beliefs is a standard lesson.

**belief** (*Ch. 25*) — A propositional attitude that represents its content as true and guides inference and action. Contrast → desire, whose → direction of fit is the reverse.

**bivalence** (*Ch. 35*) — The principle that every proposition is determinately true or false. ***Misuse:*** conflated with → excluded middle. Bivalence is about truth-value assignment; excluded middle is the schema $p \lor \neg p$. Intuitionists reject one but not the other.

**block universe** (*Ch. 17*) — The B-theoretic view that past, present, and future are equally real, time being a dimension of a four-dimensional manifold. Contrast → presentism (in A-theory).

**bootstrapping** (*Ch. 10*) — (Cohen) A reliability problem: a subject who repeatedly reads a reliable instrument can "confirm" the instrument's reliability from its own readings alone, which seems too easy to be knowledge.

**brute fact** (*Ch. 14*) — A fact that admits of no further explanation within a theory. ***Misuse:*** treating "brute fact" as a confession of failure; every theory terminates in something unexplained, and the question is which terminus is most defensible.

**bullshit** (*Ch. 11*) — Frankfurt's term: speech indifferent to truth, as opposed to lying, which is oriented to truth by opposing it. A distinct and philosophically interesting category for the epistemology of testimony.

**bundle theory** (*Ch. 15*) — The view that an object is nothing over and above its properties (or tropes), with no further substratum. Contrast → substratum theory.

**burden of proof** (*Ch. 2*) — Who must support a claim. Distribution of the burden is a substantive methodological question, not a matter of etiquette; "the burden lies with whoever asserts" is a default, not a law.

### C

**capability approach** (*Ch. 47*) — Sen and Nussbaum: justice is assessed by real freedoms to do and be certain things (capabilities), not by resources or preference-satisfaction alone.

**Cartesian dualism** (*Ch. 23*) — Substance dualism of mind and body, with interaction. Its central problem is the impossibility of a coherent account of causal interaction between an unextended and an extended substance.

**casuistry** (*Ch. 51*) — Case-based moral reasoning by analogy with paradigm cases. ***Misuse:*** "casuistry" as a sneer for specious reasoning; in fact it is a method, and contemporary applied ethics is largely casuistical.

**categorical imperative** (*Ch. 45*) — Kant's principle of morality: act only on maxims that could be willed as universal law. The Formula of Humanity (treat persons as ends) is a distinct formulation whose equivalence to the first is contested.

**causal closure** (*Ch. 19*) — The thesis that every physical event has a sufficient physical cause. The crucial premise in the → causal exclusion argument against non-physical mental causation.

**causal exclusion** (*Ch. 21*) — Kim's argument: if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, non-physical mental properties are excluded from causal work. The central threat to non-reductive → physicalism.

**ceteris paribus** (*Ch. 39*) — "Other things equal." Used to state laws with implicit exceptions. ***Misuse:*** a blank cheque for vaguely hedged generalizations; a ceteris paribus law must specify what the interference conditions are, or it explains nothing.

**charity, principle of** (*Ch. 2*) — Interpret a speaker's words so as to maximize the truth or rationality of what is said. ***Misuse:*** using charity to rescue an argument the author plainly did not give; charity concerns *interpretation*, not repair.

**Chinese Room** (*Ch. 23, 29*) — Searle's thought experiment: symbol manipulation by rule cannot by itself produce understanding, since the room-bound operator follows syntax without semantics. Central to the debate about → strong AI.

**circularity** (*Ch. 2*) — Inferring a conclusion from premises whose justification depends on the conclusion. Not always vicious: → reflective equilibrium and some → coherentist structures permit mutual support.

**coherentism** (*Ch. 8*) — Justification consists in membership in a sufficiently coherent system of beliefs, with no foundational layer. The standard objection is the isolation problem: a coherent fiction may satisfy the relation.

**commensurability** (*Ch. 40*) — Kuhn's thesis that rival paradigms may lack shared standards for comparison. Often overstated as "incommensurable = incomparable"; the defensible version concerns partial untranslatability of vocabulary.

**communitarianism** (*Ch. 47*) — The view that the self is constituted by social attachments, and that justice must be assessed against shared conceptions of the good. Associated with MacIntyre, Sandel, Taylor.

**compatibilism** (*Ch. 27*) — Free will and moral responsibility are compatible with → determinism. Distinguished from → libertarianism, which denies the compatibility.

**compositionality** (*Ch. 31*) — The meaning of a complex expression is a function of the meanings of its parts and their mode of combination. The property that explains linguistic productivity and systematicity.

**composition, special composition question** (*Ch. 14*) — Van Inwagen's question: under what conditions do some things compose a further thing? Answers range from universalism (always) to nihilism (never).

**conceivability** (*Ch. 16*) — The apparent possibility of a scenario's being imagined coherently. ***Misuse:*** conflating conceivability with imaginability; many things (e.g., water that is not H₂O) can be imagined but are arguably not conceivable, and vice versa for some abstract scenarios.

**conceptual analysis** (*Ch. 3*) — The decomposition of a concept into necessary and sufficient conditions, or into a structure of more basic concepts. Classical model, criticized by Quine, Wittgenstein, and the experimentalists.

**conceptual engineering** (*Ch. 33*) — The project of revising or replacing concepts rather than describing them (Haslanger, Cappelen, Chalmers). Its normative standards — amelioration for what purpose? — are the live question.

**conceptual role semantics** (*Ch. 31*) — Meaning is determined by the inferential role a term plays in thought and language. Faces the problem of holism and of distinguishing meaning-constitutive from incidental roles.

**conditional** (*Ch. 37*) — A sentence of the form "if $p$ then $q$." The material conditional of classical logic diverges from the indicative conditional of natural language, especially on the paradoxes of implication.

**consequentialism** (*Ch. 45*) — The rightness of acts depends solely on the value of consequences. ***Misuse:*** treated as a synonym for → utilitarianism; utilitarianism is the welfarist species, and there are consequentialisms about non-welfare goods.

**conservatism, methodological** (*Ch. 4*) — A criterion of theory choice: prefer the theory that preserves more of what we already believe, other things equal. A defeasible presumption, not a veto on revision.

**constructivism** (*Ch. 44*) — Metaethical: normative truths are constituted by procedures of practical reasoning or agreement (Kantian, contractualist). ***Misuse:*** the word also names views in philosophy of science (facts are constructed) and in mathematics (objects are constructed); the three have almost nothing to do with one another.

**content, narrow vs. wide** (*Ch. 25*) — Narrow content is fixed by the subject's internal states; wide content depends on the environment (Twin Earth, Burge's arthritis cases). Which notion does psychology need is the substantive question.

**contingent** (*Ch. 16*) — Could have been otherwise. ***Misuse:*** confused with "uncertain"; a contingent truth may be known with certainty.

**counterexample** (*Ch. 2*) — A case in which the premises of a claim hold but the claim fails. The standard engine of conceptual revision. ***Misuse:*** treating any imagined case as decisive before checking whether it involves the relevant kind, and whether the intuition it elicits survives reflection.

**counterfactual** (*Ch. 37*) — A subjunctive conditional about what would have been the case had something (counterfactually) been otherwise. Standard semantics: Lewis–Stalnaker possible worlds with a similarity ordering.

**counterpart theory** (*Ch. 16*) — Lewis's view that an individual exists in only one world and has counterparts elsewhere; denies → transworld identity.

**criterion, problem of the** (*Ch. 13*) — How do we identify a criterion of knowledge without already knowing which particular cases are knowledge? Sextus' dilemma; revived by Chisholm's methodism/particularism.

**counterpart** (see counterpart theory).

### D

**dao** (*Ch. 22, thread*) — In classical Chinese thought, the way: the pattern of things and the course of right action. Not a metaphysical entity so much as an ordering that is followed or missed.

**de dicto / de re** (*Ch. 16*) — Modality concerned with the proposition (de dicto: "it is necessary that whoever is $F$ is $F$") versus with a thing and its properties (de re: "$a$ is necessarily $F$"). The distinction governs essentialism and the necessary a posteriori.

**defeater** (*Ch. 8*) — A consideration that undermines or removes a justification. Rebutting defeaters count against the conclusion; undercutting defeaters count against the link between evidence and conclusion.

**deflationism** (*Ch. 32*) — Truth is a thin device for semantic ascent, not a substantive property: "'Snow is white' is true iff snow is white." The residual question is whether deflationism can account for the *normative* role of truth in inquiry.

**demarcation problem** (*Ch. 40*) — What distinguishes science from non-science and pseudoscience. Popper's falsifiability is one answer; the consensus is that no single criterion works and a cluster of features is more defensible.

**deontology** (*Ch. 45*) — Rightness is constrained by duties and permissions not reducible to consequences. Includes Kantian, contractualist, and rights-based versions.

**dependent origination** (*Ch. 15, thread*) — *Pratītyasamutpāda*: phenomena arise only in dependence on conditions, with no independent essence. Nāgārjuna's → śūnyatā argument turns this into a systematic anti-foundationalism.

**determinism** (*Ch. 27*) — Every event is necessitated by prior events and laws. ***Misuse:*** conflated with → fatalism (events happen regardless of what we do) and with predestination (a theological doctrine). Determinism is a claim about causal sufficiency, not about the futility of action.

**dharma** (*Ch. 15, thread*) — In Buddhist Abhidharma, a basic momentary constituent of experience; in Indian ethics more broadly, duty, norm, or the ordering principle of a life.

**diachronic / synchronic** (*Ch. 17*) — Relating to change over time vs. a single time-slice. Identity across time is diachronic; the relation between mental and physical at a moment is synchronic.

**dialetheism** (*Ch. 33*) — Some contradictions are true, so some sentences are both true and false. Motivated by the Liar; the cost is a non-classical logic and a revised notion of negation.

**difference principle** (*Ch. 47*) — Rawls: social and economic inequalities are just only if they maximize the position of the least advantaged.

**direct realism** (*Ch. 5*) — Perception relates us to mind-independent objects directly, without mediating mental objects. Revived by → disjunctivism.

**direction of fit** (*Ch. 28*) — Belief aims to fit the world (mind-to-world); desire aims to make the world fit it (world-to-mind). Anscombe's image; the distinction is contested for emotions.

**disagreement, epistemic** (*Ch. 11*) — The problem of how to revise belief when an epistemic peer disagrees. Conciliationism (→ equal weight view) vs. steadfastness.

**disjunctivism** (*Ch. 5*) — Veridical perception and hallucination share no common experiential factor: the veridical case is a direct relation to the world, and the hallucinatory case is merely subjectively indistinguishable. Blocks the argument from hallucination.

**disposition** (*Ch. 18*) — A property manifesting in characteristic conditions: fragility, solubility. Dispositional essentialism holds that laws follow from dispositions. ***Misuse:*** "disposition" as laziness-proof synonym for "tendency"; dispositions are individuated by their manifestation conditions.

**dissolving** (a problem) (*Ch. 3*) — Showing that a question rests on a false presupposition, so that it need not be answered. Contrast solving. ***Misuse:*** announcing dissolution without having done the diagnostic work; the move is cheap and often a way of dodging a problem one cannot solve.

**Doomsday argument** (*Ch. 10, thread*) — Bostrom's anthropic argument that, on Bayesian assumptions about one's place among humans ever born, the expected future population is smaller than we might think.

**doxastic** (*Ch. 8*) — Concerning belief. Doxastic justification concerns how a belief is held (well-founded); propositional justification concerns whether one has good reasons available.

**dualism** (*Ch. 23*) — Mind and body are fundamentally distinct. Substance dualism posits two kinds of entity; property dualism holds that mental *properties* are irreducible though instantiated in physical things. Contrast → physicalism.

**dual-aspect monism** (*Ch. 20*) — One underlying reality with two irreducible aspects, mental and physical. Associated with Spinoza and with some readings of Russell's → neutral monism.

**Dutch book** (*Ch. 10*) — A set of bets guaranteed to lose. Dutch-book arguments are used to justify probabilism: credences that violate the probability axioms are vulnerable to such a set.

### E

**eliminativism** (*Ch. 23*) — Folk-psychological categories (belief, desire, pain as conceived) fail to refer, like phlogiston. Should be replaced by a mature neuroscience, not reduced to it.

**emergence** (*Ch. 21*) — Weak emergence: higher-level patterns are computationally irreducible but metaphysically derivative. Strong emergence: higher-level properties are genuinely novel and possess downward causal power. The latter is what the exclusion argument threatens.

**emotivism** (*Ch. 44*) — Ayer and Stevenson: moral utterances express attitudes rather than stating propositions. ***Misuse:*** treated as identical with → expressivism; emotivism is one species. Also not a description of psychological selfishness.

**empirical / empiricism** (*Ch. 9*) — Empirical: based on observation. Empiricism: the thesis that substantive knowledge derives ultimately from sensory experience. ***Misuse:*** "empiricism" as a synonym for "empirical"; and as "practical, no-nonsense" in lay usage.

**enactivism** (*Ch. 5, 30*) — Cognition is constituted by sensorimotor activity and its coupling with the environment, not by representations in a head.

**endurantism** (*Ch. 17*) — Ordinary things persist by existing wholly at each moment, enduring through change. Contrast → perdurantism.

**entrenchment** (*Ch. 10*) — Goodman's explanation of projectibility: predicates entrenched by past inductive use are the ones we can project. An account of practice, not a justification of it.

**epiphenomenalism** (*Ch. 23*) — Mental events are caused by physical events but cause nothing themselves. Preserves the closure of physics at the price of mental causation and the obviousness of mental efficacy.

**epistemic injustice** (*Ch. 11*) — Fricker: testimonial injustice is deflated credibility due to prejudice; hermeneutical injustice is a gap in shared interpretive resources that marginalizes experience.

**epistemic luck** (*Ch. 7*) — Warrant-undermining luck. Veritic luck: the truth of the belief is lucky given the method. Central to Gettier cases.

**epistemic virtue** (*Ch. 8*) — A character trait or competence that makes a person good inquirer: open-mindedness, intellectual courage, rigor, humility.

**epistemology naturalized** (*Ch. 52*) — Quine's programme: the theory of knowledge is a chapter of empirical psychology, not a first-philosophy foundation. The objection is that it changes the subject; the reply is that the old subject was incoherent.

**epoché** (*Ch. 6*) — Suspension of judgement (Sceptics) or the bracketing of the natural attitude (Husserl). Two very different notions sharing a name.

**equal weight view** (*Ch. 11*) — Conciliationism: with a known epistemic peer and no independent reason for asymmetry, one should give the peer's opinion equal weight and split the difference.

**equilibrium, reflective** (*Ch. 3, 45*) — Mutual adjustment of principles and particular judgements until coherence is attained. Wide vs. narrow depending on how much background theory is included.

**error theory** (*Ch. 44*) — Mackie: moral discourse is truth-apt but systematically false, since it presupposes objective prescriptive facts that do not exist. Contrast → non-cognitivism.

**essence** (*Ch. 16*) — What a thing is, in the order of explanation: the properties without which it could not be that thing. The post-Kripke revival treats essence as prior to and explanatory of necessity, not as defined by it.

**ethics, normative vs. meta** (*Ch. 44*) — Normative ethics asks what to do and why; metaethics asks what moral judgement is and whether it can be true. ***Misuse:*** conflating the two, as when a metaethical anti-realist is thought thereby to recommend immorality.

**eudaimonia** (*Ch. 48*) — Aristotelian flourishing: the excellent activity of a life, not a pleasant feeling. ***Misuse:*** translating as "happiness" in the contemporary sense, which reverses the direction of the argument.

**evidence** (*Ch. 10*) — That which supports or undermines a hypothesis. ***Misuse:*** restricting the term to sense-experience; testimony, proofs, and memories are evidence on most views.

**evidence, higher-order** (*Ch. 11*) — Evidence about the reliability or state of one's evidence and cognitive capacities: the fact that I have reasoned for a long time, or that I am tired, or that others disagree.

**evidentialism** (*Ch. 8*) — Belief is justified exactly when it fits one's evidence (Clifford, Feldman, Conee). Contrast pragmatic encroachment.

**evolutionary debunking argument** (*Ch. 44*) — Street's Darwinian dilemma: if moral beliefs were selected for reproductive fitness rather than truth, their reliability is threatened. The strength of the argument depends on the best account of moral truth.

**excluded middle** (*Ch. 35*) — The schema $p \lor \neg p$. See → bivalence for the distinction. Rejected by → intuitionistic logic.

**existence** (*Ch. 14*) — The question whether existence is a first-order property of things or captured by the quantifier. Kant's "existence is not a predicate" is the classic statement of the second view.

**existential import** (*Ch. 31*) — Whether a categorical statement presupposes the existence of its subject class. Aristotelian logic has it; modern predicate logic does not.

**explanandum / explanans** (*Ch. 38*) — The thing to be explained and the explaining content, respectively.

**explanatory gap** (*Ch. 24*) — Levine: no matter how complete the functional or neural account, an explanatory residue remains — why this physical process is *experienced as* something. Weaker than the → hard problem: it is epistemic.

**explanatory power** (*Ch. 4*) — A criterion of theory choice: scope, precision, unification, and the ability to render phenomena expected rather than coincidental.

**expressivism** (*Ch. 44*) — Ethical sentences express non-cognitive states (attitudes, plans, commitments) rather than beliefs. The central burden is explaining moral reasoning and moral disagreement in non-descriptive terms.

**extension / intension** (*Ch. 31*) — The set of things a term applies to vs. the condition by which it applies. Informative identity statements are explained by sense differing while reference is shared.

**externalism** (*Ch. 8, 25*) — In justification: warrant may depend on facts beyond the subject's access (reliability). In content: content may depend on the environment. ***Misuse:*** the two are independent theses and are often conflated under one word.

### F

**fact/value distinction** (*Ch. 44*) — Descriptive claims vs. evaluative claims, and whether the latter can be derived from the former (Hume's guillotine). ***Misuse:*** taking the distinction to prove moral non-realism; it establishes a logical gap at most, not a metaphysical verdict.

**fallibilism** (*Ch. 7*) — Knowledge does not require certainty or immunity from error. ***Misuse:*** conflated with → scepticism, which questions whether we know at all.

**fallacy** (*Ch. 4*) — A form of inference that tends to produce false conclusions from true premises. ***Misuse:*** "fallacy" as a label for any argument one dislikes; the same form may be legitimate in some contexts (e.g., appeals to authority in testimony-heavy domains).

**falsificationism** (*Ch. 40*) — Popper: scientific theories are those that forbid observable states of affairs, making them vulnerable to refutation. The problem is that theories are never tested in isolation.

**fatalism** (*Ch. 27*) — Whatever happens, happens regardless of what anyone does. Distinct from → determinism, and far less defensible.

**feminist epistemology** (*Ch. 11, thread*) — The study of how gender structures the production of knowledge, including credibility economies, the situatedness of knowers, and the politics of what counts as a question.

**fictionalism** (*Ch. 14*) — A discourse is useful though not literally true; assert only its "nominalistic paraphrase" in serious contexts. Applied to numbers, modality, possible worlds, and morality.

**fideism** (*Ch. 50*) — Religious commitment is grounded in faith rather than reason, and immune to evidential assessment. Its cost is that it severs religion from public argument.

**fine-tuning argument** (*Ch. 50*) — The physical constants appear constrained to life-permitting values; the theistic explanation is offered as the best account. The central disputes concern the reference class and the alternative "multiverse" hypotheses.

**fission** (*Ch. 26*) — Parfit's case: one person divides into two psychologically continuous successors. Standard psychological theories imply that identity cannot survive fission, which presses toward a reductionist view.

**foundationalism** (*Ch. 8*) — Justification terminates in non-inferentially justified beliefs. Objections: the Sellarsian dilemma for the Given, and the arbitrariness of the stopping point.

**Frankfurt case** (*Ch. 27*) — A person acts freely though could not have done otherwise, because an intervener would have prevented it. Used to rebut the principle of alternative possibilities.

**functionalism** (*Ch. 23*) — Mental states are defined by causal roles, hence multiply realizable. The central theory in cognitive science. ***Misuse:*** confused with biological function-talk or with the claim that everything has a purpose.

### G

**gender / sex** (*Ch. 47, thread*) — Analytic distinction between a social/ascriptive category and a biological one. The distinction is not itself a political position; how the categories relate is a substantive empirical and normative question.

**generality problem** (*Ch. 8*) — Reliabilism must say which type a belief-forming process belongs to, and the choice of typing changes the verdict. No fully satisfactory solution exists.

**generic** (*Ch. 37*) — A statement like "tigers are striped," which tolerates exceptions and is not a universal generalization. Generics structure default reasoning and much natural-language metaphysics.

**Gettier case** (*Ch. 7*) — A case in which a justified true belief is true only by luck and so is not knowledge. The lesson: justification must be connected to truth non-accidentally.

**Given, the** (*Ch. 8, 12*) — Supposedly non-conceptual, incorrigible sensory content that grounds knowledge. Sellars' critique: the "given" would have to stand in logical relations (to justify), and logical relations require conceptualization, so nothing can be both non-conceptual and justificatory.

**global workspace theory** (*Ch. 24*) — Consciousness is a functional bottleneck of information dissemination. Explains access consciousness well; contested for phenomenal consciousness.

**good, the** (*Ch. 44, 48*) — What is worth pursuing. ***Misuse:*** assuming value is one thing; pluralists distinguish at least the prudentially good, the morally good, the aesthetically good, and the simply choiceworthy.

**Goodman's paradox** (*Ch. 10*) — The predicate *grue* (green if examined before time $t$, blue otherwise) would be equally supported by the evidence, threatening the distinction between confirmable and unconfirmable predicates. The work done is by our practice of predicate entrenchment, not by any formal criterion.

**grounding** (*Ch. 22*) — The relation of metaphysical explanation: $A$ grounds $B$ when $A$ makes $B$ the case, in the order of being rather than of knowing. ***Misuse:*** conflated with causation and with epistemic reasons; grounding is supposed to be non-causal and objective.

**group belief** (*Ch. 11*) — Whether collectives can hold beliefs; summative (aggregated individual beliefs) vs. non-summative (emergent, irreducibly group-level) accounts.

**guillotine, Hume's** (*Ch. 44*) — The observation that no ought-conclusion follows from purely is-premises. Often confused with Moore's open-question argument, which is a different (and stronger) claim about definability.

---

### H

**haecceity** (*Ch. 16*) — A primitive "thisness" individuating a thing apart from any of its properties.

**hard problem of consciousness** (*Ch. 24*) — Chalmers: why is there subjective experience at all, given that the functional facts are (in principle) all settled? Distinct from the "easy problems" of access, integration, and report.

**harm principle** (*Ch. 47*) — Mill: coercion is justified only to prevent harm to others. The hard questions are what counts as harm and whether offensive conduct qualifies.

**hedonism** (*Ch. 48*) — Well-being consists in net pleasure over pain. ***Misuse:*** the lay sense of "hedonism" as dissolute behaviour; the philosophical thesis is about the structure of welfare, not a lifestyle.

**Hempel's ravens** (*Ch. 40*) — A green shirt confirms "all ravens are black" by confirming its contrapositive, much of which is remote from the hypothesis. Reveals that confirmation is not simply an instance relation.

**hermeneutics** (*Ch. 1, thread*) — The theory of interpretation; in philosophy, the claim that understanding is historically and linguistically situated. Gadamer's fusion of horizons.

**hinge proposition** (*Ch. 6*) — Wittgenstein: a proposition exempt from doubt because it functions as the scaffolding of inquiry ("there are hands here"). Doubt presupposes it rather than defeating it.

**historical injustice** (*Ch. 47*) — Injustice whose effects persist but whose perpetrators and victims are dead. The disputes concern whether the relevant obligations are inherited, and by whom.

**holism** (*Ch. 10, 31*) — Confirmation holism: hypotheses are tested in bundles, not singly. Meaning holism: the meaning of a term depends on its place in a wider web. ***Misuse:*** invoked loosely to mean "everything is connected," which licenses nothing.

**Humean theory of motivation** (*Ch. 46*) — Belief alone cannot motivate; desire (or a conative state) is necessary. Contrast the Kantian view that pure practical reason can determine the will.

**hylomorphism** (*Ch. 19*) — Objects are compounds of matter and form. Revived in contemporary metaphysics of biology and in neo-Aristotelian accounts of essence.

### I

**idealism** (*Ch. 20*) — Reality is fundamentally mental or mind-dependent. *Berkeleyan*: to be is to be perceived. *Transcendental*: the forms of experience are contributed by the mind. *Absolute*: reality is a single coherent whole. ***Misuse:*** the word is regularly confused with the ordinary sense of "idealistic" (having high aims), which is not a metaphysical thesis.

**identity, personal** (*Ch. 26*) — Numerical identity of a person across time, as opposed to mere similarity. The relation asked about by "will it be me who suffers?" → psychological continuity; → animalism.

**identity of indiscernibles** (*Ch. 15*) — Leibniz: things sharing all properties are identical. Its converse (the indiscernibility of identicals) is uncontroversial; the principle itself is not.

**identity theory** (*Ch. 23*) — Mental states are identical with brain states (type or token). Type identity faces → multiple realizability; token identity is compatible with non-reductive physicalism.

**ideology** (*Ch. 22*) — In Sider's technical sense: the primitive, indefinable notions a theory takes as basic (e.g., "part" or "ground"). ***Misuse:*** the political sense is unrelated; a theory's "ideology" in the metaphysical sense is not its politics.

**illocutionary act** (*Ch. 34*) — What one does *in* speaking: asserting, promising, ordering. Contrast locutionary (what is said) and perlocutionary (the effect produced).

**illusionism** (*Ch. 24*) — Frankish: phenomenal consciousness as naively conceived is an illusion; introspection misrepresents. The meta-problem — why it seems to us that there is a hard problem — becomes the explanatory target.

**illusion, argument from** (*Ch. 5*) — Since things appear otherwise than they are (bent sticks, after-images), perception involves something other than the object: a sense-datum. The premise of the classical phenomenalist programme.

**imagination** (*Ch. 30, 49*) — The capacity to represent without asserting. Central to pretence, fiction, modal epistemology, simulation-based mindreading, and the paradoxes of emotional response to fiction.

**implicature** (*Ch. 34*) — What a speaker conveys without literally saying, via the cooperative principle. Conversational vs. conventional. ***Misuse:*** conflated with entailment; implicatures are cancellable, entailments are not.

**incompatibilism** (*Ch. 27*) — Free will and moral responsibility are incompatible with determinism. Includes → libertarianism and hard determinism.

**indexical** (*Ch. 12*) — "I," "here," "now": terms whose reference depends on the context of utterance. Perry's "essential indexical": the content of "I am making a mess" is not capturable by any context-free proposition.

**indispensability argument** (*Ch. 43*) — Quine–Putnam: mathematics is indispensable to our best scientific theories, and we should be ontologically committed to whatever our best theories require; so abstract objects exist.

**induction** (*Ch. 10*) — Projecting observed regularities to unobserved cases. Hume's problem: no non-circular justification of the practice appears available.

**inductive risk** (*Ch. 39*) — The risk of accepting or rejecting a hypothesis erroneously; since the choice of evidential threshold has practical consequences, values enter scientific inference.

**inference** (*Ch. 2*) — The mental or logical passage from premises to conclusion. Deductive, inductive, abductive.

**inference to the best explanation** (*Ch. 10*) — Lipton: accept the hypothesis that would, if true, provide the loveliest explanation of the evidence. ***Misuse:*** used without specifying the explanatory virtues by which "best" is measured; the phrase then merely dresses a preference.

**infinite regress** (*Ch. 8*) — A series that cannot terminate and so cannot ground anything. Vicious when the regress is generated by the very demand for justification; benign when the regress is not required (some regresses are "harmless" because the derived items explain only derivatively).

**information, semantic** (*Ch. 25, 41*) — The notion used in naturalizing content: a state carries information in the Shannon sense when it correlates reliably with a condition. Insufficient for intentional content without teleology or function.

**inscrutability of reference** (*Ch. 31*) — Quine: rival manuals of translation can assign incompatible referents to a term while preserving all dispositions to behaviour; reference is therefore indeterminate.

**instrumentalism** (*Ch. 40*) — Theories are instruments for prediction, not descriptions of unobservable reality. ***Misuse:*** confused with "using instruments" and with pragmatism generally; instrumentalism is a specific semantic/ontological thesis.

**integrated information theory** (*Ch. 24*) — Tononi: consciousness is integrated information, $\Phi$. A bold identity claim with testable-ish consequences and serious conceptual objections.

**intentionalism (perception)** (*Ch. 5*) — Experience is a representation with representational content; there is no additional object of experience. ***Misuse:*** not to be confused with *intentionalism* in literary theory (meaning is determined by authorial intention) — a completely unrelated doctrine sharing a name.

**intentionality** (*Ch. 25*) — The aboutness of mental states. Brentano's mark of the mental; its naturalization is one of the central projects of the philosophy of mind.

**internalism** (*Ch. 8, 28*) — In justification: warrant depends only on what is accessible to the subject. In reasons: a normative reason for action must be capable of motivating the agent. ***Misuse:*** as with externalism, the justificatory and motivational theses are independent.

**interventionism** (*Ch. 18*) — Woodward: $X$ causes $Y$ if an intervention on $X$ changes $Y$ while holding other relevant variables fixed. Makes causation manipulable and testable, and connects to causal modelling.

**intrinsic / extrinsic** (*Ch. 22*) — Intrinsic properties a thing has in itself; extrinsic ones depend on relations to other things. Value theory uses the distinction heavily (intrinsic vs. instrumental value). ***Misuse:*** "intrinsic" as an intensifier ("intrinsically wrong" = very wrong).

**introspection** (*Ch. 25*) — The first-person access we have to our own mental states. ***Misuse:*** assuming introspection is a kind of inner perception of a private theatre; the nature of the access is exactly what is disputed.

**intuition** (*Ch. 9*) — A seeming: an apparent self-evidence attaching to a proposition or case, treated as defeasible evidence in philosophy. ***Misuse:*** "intuition" for a guess, a prejudice, or a gut feeling about practical matters; none of these is the methodological notion.

**is/ought** (*Ch. 44*) — See → guillotine, Hume's.

### J

**justice** (*Ch. 47*) — The virtue of institutions and of persons in distributing benefits and burdens according to what is due. Rawls's "first virtue of social institutions" is a substantive claim, not a definition.

**justification** (*Ch. 8*) — The property that makes a true belief non-accidentally true; also the activity of giving reasons. The ambiguity between the *state* and the *activity* generates much confusion in epistemology.

**just-so story** (*Ch. 38*) — An explanation with no independent evidence beyond the phenomenon it purports to explain: an adaptive tale fitted to the trait post hoc.

### K

**kalām** (*Ch. 50*) — Islamic scholastic theology; its cosmological argument (that the universe began and so needs a cause) is a distinct argument from Aquinas's.

**kinds, natural** (*Ch. 39*) — Groupings that carve nature at its joints and support induction. ***Misuse:*** assuming every category we use (race, gender, mental disorder, species) is a natural kind; some are social kinds, and the question is empirical and contested.

**knowledge argument** (*Ch. 24*) — Jackson's Mary: a scientist who knows all the physical facts about colour vision learns something new on seeing red. ※ The argument is contested at every premise, and Jackson himself later abandoned it.

**knowledge-first** (*Ch. 7*) — Williamson: knowledge is the most general factive mental state, not definable from belief plus conditions; it is the norm of assertion and of practical reasoning.

**knowledge how / knowledge that** (*Ch. 30*) — Ryle's distinction between a practical ability and a propositional truth. The intellectualist reply: knowing how is knowing that certain ways of acting satisfy a standard.

**KK principle** (*Ch. 7*) — If one knows that $p$, one knows that one knows that $p$. Rejected by most accounts that tolerate higher-order ignorance.

**Kuhn-loss** (*Ch. 40*) — The loss of explanatory or predictive power when one paradigm replaces another; the phenomenon that makes scientific progress non-cumulative in Kuhn's sense.

---

### L

**language of thought** (*Ch. 25*) — Fodor: thought has a compositional, sentence-like representational format realized in the brain; natural language acquisition is learning to express it.

**law of nature** (*Ch. 18*) — A true, lawlike generalization: universal, supporting counterfactuals, and explanatory. Humean (best-system) analyses vs. necessitarian and dispositionalist accounts. ***Misuse:*** "law" as in legal statute; the two are not even analogous in structure.

**libertarianism** (*Ch. 27 / Ch. 47*) — **Two unrelated uses.** Metaphysical: incompatibilist free will involving agent or event causation not determined by prior states. Political: the minimal-state view of justice associated with Nozick. ***Misuse:*** concluding that because someone is a metaphysical libertarian they hold the political view (and vice versa).

**liberty, negative / positive** (*Ch. 47*) — Freedom from interference vs. freedom to be one's own master (self-realization, participation). ***Misuse:*** assuming positive liberty is simply "more" liberty; the two can conflict, which is the whole point of the distinction.

**Liar paradox** (*Ch. 33*) — "This sentence is false." Produces contradiction under classical assumptions. Solutions: Tarski's hierarchy, Kripke's fixed points, contextualism, → dialetheism.

**logic, classical vs. non-classical** (*Ch. 35*) — Classical logic validates bivalence, excluded middle, and explosion. Intuitionistic, relevant, and paraconsistent logics each drop or restrict one of these, for principled reasons connected to truth, relevance, or inconsistency.

**logical form** (*Ch. 31, 35*) — The structure in virtue of which an argument is valid, as opposed to its surface grammar. The theory of descriptions is the paradigm case of revealing hidden logical form.

**logical positivism** (*Ch. 52*) — The Vienna Circle's programme: meaningful statements are either analytic or empirically verifiable; metaphysics is nonsense. Self-undermining on most readings, but its dissolution of pseudo-problems survives in more modest forms.

**lottery paradox** (*Ch. 10*) — Each ticket is probably not the winner; yet one knows some ticket will win. Pressures the "knowledge requires high probability" thesis.

**luminosity** (*Ch. 7*) — Williamson: a mental state is luminous if one is always in a position to know one has it. He argues no non-trivial state is luminous, which undermines many internalist programmes.

**luck egalitarianism** (*Ch. 47*) — Inequalities are just only when traceable to the agent's choices, not to brute luck.

---

### M

**many-worlds** (*Ch. 41*) — Everett: the wavefunction never collapses; all outcomes occur in decoherent branches. Removes the measurement postulate at the cost of an enormous ontology and a preferred basis problem.

**materialism / physicalism** (*Ch. 19, 23*) — Everything is physical, or supervenes on the physical. **Physicalism** is the preferred contemporary term because "physical" is not a fixed list of particles but whatever physics ultimately says. ***Misuse:*** treating physicalism as "scientism" or as the denial of experience; most physicalists claim to accommodate experience, contestedly.

**maximalism (truthmaker)** (*Ch. 22*) — Every truth has a truthmaker. Modest versions restrict the principle to positive truths, exempting negative existentials.

**meaning** (*Ch. 31 / Ch. 48*) — **Two unrelated uses.** Semantic: what an expression means. Existential: what makes a life worth living. ***Misuse:*** transferring questions and answers across the two, e.g., arguing that because words have meanings, lives must have a designer-supplied meaning.

**measurement problem** (*Ch. 41*) — The linear dynamics of quantum mechanics predicts superpositions, while measurement yields definite outcomes. Collapse, many-worlds, Bohmian mechanics, and QBism are competing responses.

**Meinongianism** (*Ch. 14*) — There are objects that do not exist (the golden mountain, the round square). Rejected by Russell's theory of descriptions, but with contemporary defenders.

**memory** (*Ch. 11, 26*) — A source of knowledge and of personal identity. The circularity problem: memory presupposes the identity of the rememberer, which identity theory then uses memory to explain.

**mental causation** (*Ch. 21, 23*) — Mental states cause physical events. Threat: if physical causes are sufficient, mental causes seem excluded. Responses: identity, interventionism, emergentism, or denial of closure.

**mereology** (*Ch. 14*) — The theory of parts and wholes: composition, overlap, atomism, gunk (everything divisible), junk (no maximal whole).

**meta-ethics** (*Ch. 44*) — The metaphysics, epistemology, and semantics of moral judgement.

**meta-philosophy** (*Ch. 1, 52*) — Reflection on the nature, methods, and limits of philosophy itself.

**methodism / particularism (Chisholm)** (*Ch. 13*) — Methodism: start from a criterion of knowledge and derive particular judgements. Particularism: start from particular cases of knowing and derive the criterion. Neither escapes the → criterion problem without cost.

**mind-body problem** (*Ch. 23*) — How mental phenomena relate to physical ones.

**modal epistemology** (*Ch. 16*) — How we know what is possible or necessary. Conceivability-based, essences-based, and counterfactual-based accounts compete.

**modal realism** (*Ch. 16*) — Lewis: possible worlds are concrete universes, spatiotemporally isolated from ours. The explanatory payoff is enormous; the ontological price is the objection.

**modality** (*Ch. 16*) — Possibility, necessity, contingency, impossibility. Modality in the *de dicto* and *de re* forms.

**model (scientific)** (*Ch. 39*) — A simplified representational structure used to reason about a target system. Models may be deliberately false; the question is how falsehood can afford understanding.

**monism** (*Ch. 20*) — Reality is one. **Substance** monism: one kind of thing. **Priority** monism (Schaffer): the whole is prior to the parts. ***Misuse:*** confused with monotony or uniformity; monism is a claim about fundamentality.

**moral responsibility** (*Ch. 27*) — Being appropriately subject to praise, blame, and the reactive attitudes. Its conditions (control, knowledge, capacity) and its compatibility with determinism are separate questions.

**motte-and-bailey** (*Ch. 4*) — A rhetorical structure: defend a modest claim (the motte) when attacked, but advance a controversial one (the bailey) when unchallenged.

**multiple realizability** (*Ch. 23*) — The same mental state can be realized in different physical substrates, so type identity fails. The standard reply: type identity can be relativized to a domain.

**mysterianism** (*Ch. 24*) — McGinn: the mind is cognitively closed to the solution of the consciousness problem, as a rat is closed to calculus. An empirical-cognitive claim, not a metaphysical one.

**myth of the given** (*Ch. 8*) — See → Given, the.

### N

**nativism** (*Ch. 31*) — Some concepts or structures are innate. The debate is about which and in what sense; "innate" does not mean "present at birth in full form."

**naturalism** (*Ch. 52*) — Philosophical inquiry is continuous with the sciences and posits nothing supernatural. ***Misuse:*** conflated with "scientism" (the view that science exhausts all value) and with the artistic sense of naturalism; and sometimes used as a mere label of approval.

**naturalistic fallacy** (*Ch. 44*) — Moore: identifying the good with any natural property commits an error, since the identification can always be intelligibly questioned. ***Misuse:*** regularly conflated with Hume's is/ought gap; they are distinct arguments with distinct targets, and Moore's is stronger.

**necessary a posteriori** (*Ch. 16*) — Kripke: propositions knowable only empirically that hold in all possible worlds ("water is H₂O"). Overturns the traditional equation of a priori with necessary.

**necessity** (*Ch. 16*) — Truth in all possible worlds: logical, metaphysical, nomological, and conceptual grades are distinguished.

**neutral monism** (*Ch. 20*) — One kind of stuff that is neither mental nor physical, with both aspects arising from its structure (Russell, James). Now revived as *Russellian monism* in the consciousness literature.

**no-miracles argument** (*Ch. 40*) — Putnam/Boyd: scientific realism is the only position under which the predictive success of science is not a miracle. The pessimistic meta-induction is the standard reply.

**no true Scotsman** (*Ch. 4*) — Redefining a category to exclude a counterexample. Named by Flew; a species of → ad hoc rescue.

**nominalism** (*Ch. 15*) — There are no universals; only particulars (and perhaps classes or resemblance relations). ***Misuse:*** "nominal" in ordinary usage (in name only, token) has no connection to the doctrine.

**non-cognitivism** (*Ch. 44*) — Moral judgements are not truth-apt beliefs but expressions of attitude or commitment. Contrast → error theory, which agrees about the metaphysics but not the semantics.

**non-identity problem** (*Ch. 51*) — Parfit: if a policy determines which people exist, we cannot say it harms those who exist because of it, since they would not otherwise exist. Threatens person-affecting accounts of intergenerational obligation.

**non-locality** (*Ch. 41*) — Quantum correlations violate Bell inequalities and cannot be explained by local hidden variables. Whether this implies action at a distance is interpretation-dependent; no-signalling holds.

**normative** (*Ch. 44*) — Concerning standards of correctness: what ought to be, what is licensed, what is good. ***Misuse:*** restricted to ethics; epistemology, logic, and semantics are equally normative domains.

**noumenon / phenomenon** (*Ch. 20*) — Kant: the thing in itself vs. the thing as it appears under our forms of intuition.

**no-self view** (*Ch. 26*) — There is no substantial, persisting self; "self" is a convenient fiction over a stream of experience (Hume, Parfit, Buddhist → anattā).

---

### O

**objective list theory** (*Ch. 48*) — Well-being consists in objective goods (knowledge, friendship, achievement), whether or not desired.

**objectivity** (*Ch. 13*) — Correctness independent of any particular perspective. ***Misuse:*** equated with "neutrality" or "having no values"; objectivity is a property of the *method*, not of the person.

**obligation** (*Ch. 45*) — What one is required to do, with a corresponding claim on one's action. Not identical to what is best; the gap generates the debate over → supererogation.

**occasionalism** (*Ch. 20*) — God is the sole cause; apparent natural causation is occasion for divine action. Malebranche and some kalām atomists.

**Occam's razor** (*Ch. 14, 40*) — Do not multiply entities without necessity. ***Misuse:*** "simpler is better" as an algorithm. Simplicity is multidimensional (ontological, ideological, computational), its weight against explanatory power is unclear, and it never decides alone.

**ontological argument** (*Ch. 50*) — From the concept of the greatest conceivable being to its existence. Anselm's version and its modal descendant (Plantinga) remain the most discussed; the standard objections target the move from concept to existence and the coherence of the concept.

**ontological commitment** (*Ch. 14*) — Quine: what a theory says exists is what its bound variables must range over in regimented form. A criterion of commitment, not a criterion of truth.

**ontology** (*Ch. 14*) — The inventory of what there is; also the subdiscipline that asks what the inventory should contain.

**open question argument** (*Ch. 44*) — Moore: for any natural property $N$, "this is $N$ but is it good?" remains an open, intelligible question; so good is not identical to $N$. Strong against definitional naturalism, weaker against revisionary or synthetic identifications.

**operationalism** (*Ch. 40*) — A concept is defined by the operations used to measure it. Abandoned in its strict form but still shapes measurement debates.

**ought implies can** (*Ch. 45*) — Obligation presupposes the capacity to comply. Its logical status (conceptual truth, pragmatic principle, or normative stipulation) is disputed.

**overdetermination** (*Ch. 18*) — Two or more independently sufficient causes of one event. Used against counterfactual theories of causation, which deny causation to redundant causes.

---

### P

**panpsychism** (*Ch. 19, 24*) — Mentality is fundamental and ubiquitous. The combination problem — how micro-experiences compose macro-experiences — is its main difficulty.

**paradigm** (*Ch. 40*) — Kuhn: an exemplary achievement that defines a research tradition, including standards, puzzles, and techniques. ***Misuse:*** "paradigm shift" as a synonym for "change" or "new idea," emptying the term of its structure.

**paradox** (*Ch. 33*) — An apparently valid argument from apparently true premises to an apparently false conclusion. Resolution must locate the false step; merely feeling puzzled is not a paradox.

**particularism, epistemic** (*Ch. 13*) — See → methodism/particularism. Distinct from → particularism, moral.

**particularism, moral** (*Ch. 45*) — Dancy: the moral relevance of a feature varies with context; there are no exceptionless moral principles. ***Misuse:*** no relation to the epistemic sense of the term, despite sharing a name.

**particulars** (*Ch. 15*) — Non-repeatable individuals, as opposed to → universals.

**paternalism** (*Ch. 51*) — Interference with a person for their own good. Soft paternalism restricts interference to cases of non-autonomous action; hard paternalism allows it for autonomous choices.

**perdurantism** (*Ch. 17*) — Objects persist by having temporal parts. Solves the problem of temporary intrinsics at the cost of a counterintuitive ontology.

**performative** (*Ch. 34*) — An utterance that does something rather than describing: "I promise." ***Misuse:*** the popular sense ("performative" = insincere or for show) is a different, non-Austinian usage; note it and do not equivocate.

**perlocutionary act** (*Ch. 34*) — The effect of an utterance on an audience: persuading, frightening, amusing.

**person** (*Ch. 26*) — A being with moral standing, typically characterized by self-consciousness and rational agency. The concept is normative as well as descriptive; "human being" and "person" are not interchangeable.

**phenomenal concepts** (*Ch. 24*) — Concepts of experience formed from the first-person perspective; the supposed vehicle for the knowledge argument's force.

**phenomenal consciousness** (*Ch. 24*) — What it is like to be in a state (Nagel). Contrast → access consciousness.

**phenomenalism** (*Ch. 5, 20*) — Physical objects are constructions out of actual and possible sensory experiences. ***Misuse:*** confused with → phenomenology. Phenomenalism is a reductionist metaphysics of the external world; phenomenology is a descriptive method. The words are alike; the doctrines are not.

**phenomenology** (*Ch. 3, 30*) — The descriptive study of the structures of experience as lived, bracketing metaphysical questions (Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, Heidegger).

**phronesis** (*Ch. 48*) — Practical wisdom: the capacity to perceive what a situation requires and to act well in it, not reducible to rule-following.

**physicalism** (*Ch. 23*) — See → materialism.

**platonism** (*Ch. 15, 43*) — Abstract objects exist independently of minds and language. ***Misuse:*** the term also names romantic doctrines and mathematical realism specifically; "Platonic" in ordinary speech (of love, forms) carries almost none of the philosophical content.

**pluralism, logical** (*Ch. 35*) — There are multiple equally correct logics, each correct for its domain or purpose (Beall, Restall). Challenges the idea of a single canonical logic.

**possible world** (*Ch. 16*) — A complete way things might have been. ***Misuse:*** in most philosophical usage, not a physically existing parallel universe; only → modal realism makes that claim.

**pragmatics** (*Ch. 34*) — Meaning in use: implicature, presupposition, speech acts. Contrast semantics.

**pragmatism** (*Ch. 32*) — Peirce, James, Dewey: the meaning and truth of a conception lie in its practical bearings and in the conduct of inquiry. ***Misuse:*** "pragmatic" as a synonym for "practical" or "expedient"; pragmatism is a theory of meaning and truth, and Peirce's maxim is a criterion of clarity, not a licence for expediency.

**presupposition** (*Ch. 34*) — Content taken for granted by an utterance, which survives negation ("the king of France is bald" presupposes there is a king). Presupposition failure threatens bivalence.

**prima facie** (*Ch. 45*) — At first sight, subject to defeat. Ross's prima facie duties are genuine duties that can be overridden. ***Misuse:*** "prima facie" for "superficial" or "apparent but false"; the term marks a defeasible standing, not an illusion.

**primary / secondary qualities** (*Ch. 5*) — Locke: primary qualities (extension, figure, motion) resemble their causes; secondary qualities (colour, taste) are powers to produce ideas in us. The distinction is not stable under modern physics.

**prioritarianism** (*Ch. 45*) — Improvements to worse-off people have greater moral weight, independently of total welfare.

**private language argument** (*Ch. 31*) — Wittgenstein: a language referring to essentially private sensations is impossible, since there could be no difference between following a rule and merely seeming to. The interpretation is contested; Kripke's reading is sceptical.

**privileged access** (*Ch. 25*) — First-person authority about one's own mental states. Its scope and explanation (constitutive, evidential, expressive) are disputed.

**probability, interpretations of** (*Ch. 10*) — Frequentist, propensity, subjective (Bayesian), logical/evidential. Each solves some problems and creates others; the choice has consequences for confirmation theory.

**projectibility** (*Ch. 10*) — The property of predicates that can be projected inductively (grue cannot). Goodman's problem is to characterize it without circularity.

**proper function** (*Ch. 8, 25*) — Plantinga: a belief is warranted when produced by cognitive faculties functioning properly according to a design plan aimed at truth.

**proposition** (*Ch. 31*) — The bearer of truth value; what a declarative sentence expresses. ***Misuse:*** used interchangeably with "sentence" and "statement"; propositions are usually taken to be abstract and language-independent, sentences are not.

**propositional attitude** (*Ch. 25*) — A mental state directed at a proposition: believing that $p$, desiring that $p$, fearing that $p$.

**prosententialism** (*Ch. 32*) — "It is true that $p$" is a device of anaphoric reference to a sentence's content, not an ascription of a property. A species of → deflationism.

**pseudo-problem** (*Ch. 52*) — A question that appears substantive but rests on a confusion of grammar or category. ***Misuse:*** labelling a difficult question a pseudo-problem to avoid answering it; the diagnosis must be earned by showing the confusion.

**pseudoscience** (*Ch. 40*) — A body of claims presented as science without meeting its evidential standards. The demarcation problem shows that no single criterion suffices; a cluster (testability, engagement with evidence, error-correction, institutional norms) is more defensible.

**psychologism** (*Ch. 36*) — The reduction of logical or mathematical truths to psychological facts. Frege's critique is the locus classicus; ***misuse:*** "psychologism" as a label for any psychological consideration in philosophy, which is not the doctrine.

**public reason** (*Ch. 47*) — Rawls: the standards of argument appropriate to constitutional essentials in a pluralistic society, restricted to reasons all reasonable citizens could accept.

**pramāṇa** (*Ch. 11, thread*) — In Indian epistemology, a valid means of knowledge: perception, inference, testimony, comparison, and others depending on school. The *pramāṇa* debates are a fully developed rival to Anglophone epistemology, with sophisticated treatments of testimony and error.

### Q

**qualia** (*Ch. 24*) — The intrinsic, subjective characters of experience. ***Misuse:*** used as a catch-all for "the mysterious part"; careful use distinguishes phenomenal character from relational and functional properties.

**quasi-realism** (*Ch. 44*) — Blackburn: an expressivist programme that earns the right to realist-seeming discourse (truth, fact, knowledge) by explaining it from expressive foundations.

**quietism** (*Ch. 52*) — Philosophy's task is to end philosophical confusion, not to answer its questions (Wittgenstein, McDowell). The objection: quietism must give an account of why the confusions arise, which is itself a substantive theory.

**quus** (*Ch. 31*) — Kripke's deviant function, differing from addition only for numbers never computed. The rule-following paradox: no fact about past usage distinguishes meaning plus from meaning quus.

### R

**rational reconstruction** (*Ch. 1*) — Recasting a body of practice or discourse in an idealized logical form to display its structure (Carnap, Lakatos). ***Misuse:*** confused with rationalization in the psychological sense; reconstruction is descriptive-idealizing, not self-serving.

**rationalism** (*Ch. 9*) — Substantive knowledge is possible through reason independent of experience. ***Misuse:*** "rationalism" for "being rational," and for the economic doctrine of rational choice.

**rationality** (*Ch. 28*) — Conformity to norms of theoretical and practical reason. Instrumental rationality concerns means-end coherence; substantive rationality concerns the ends themselves.

**reactive attitudes** (*Ch. 27*) — Strawson: resentment, gratitude, indignation, forgiveness — the interpersonal responses that constitute holding someone responsible. Responsibility is located in the practice, not in a metaphysical condition.

**realism** (*Ch. 22*) — **At least four distinct theses share the name:** (i) metaphysical realism: there is a mind-independent world; (ii) scientific realism: our best theories are approximately true; (iii) moral realism: there are objective moral facts; (iv) semantic realism: bivalence holds for a discourse. ***Misuse:*** arguing for one and concluding another. Always specify which.

**realism, moral** (*Ch. 44*) — There are objective moral truths, independent of anyone's attitudes. Robust (non-naturalist), Cornell (naturalist), and quietist versions differ sharply.

**realism, scientific** (*Ch. 40*) — Our best theories are approximately true and their unobservable posits exist. Contrast → instrumentalism and constructive empiricism.

**reason** (*Ch. 28*) — **Two uses to keep apart:** a *cause* of action (the psychological state that produces it) and a *normative reason* (the consideration that counts in favour of it). ***Misuse:*** sliding between explanatory and justifying senses within a single argument; Davidson's "reasons are causes" is precisely about how the two connect.

**reasons internalism / externalism** (*Ch. 28*) — Internalism: a normative reason for $A$ to $\phi$ must be able to motivate $A$ (Williams: reasons are internal to the agent's "subjective motivational set"). Externalism: reasons can exist independently of any motivating connection.

**reasons-responsive** (*Ch. 27*) — Fischer and Ravizza: an action is free when the mechanism producing it is responsive to reasons. A compatibilist condition that avoids the regress of higher-order desires.

**reduction** (*Ch. 21*) — Explaining a domain in terms of a more basic one: by identity, by derivation, by functionalization, or by mechanism. ***Misuse:*** "reductionism" as a global term of abuse; the question is always *which* reduction, of what, by which relation.

**reference** (*Ch. 31*) — The relation between a term and what it designates. Direct reference: the referent is not mediated by a descriptive sense (Kripke, Kaplan).

**reflective equilibrium** (*Ch. 3*) — See → equilibrium, reflective.

**regress** (*Ch. 8*) — See → infinite regress.

**relational egalitarianism** (*Ch. 47*) — Equality consists in relations of equal standing, not in equal distribution (Anderson, Scheffler).

**relativism** (*Ch. 13, 32*) — Truth or justification is relative to a framework, culture, or perspective. Distinguish *descriptive* (people differ), *metaethical/epistemic* (truth is relative), and *normative* (tolerance is required) theses. ***Misuse:*** treating relativism as itself entailing tolerance; that entailment requires a further normative premise, and relativism notoriously struggles to supply it.

**reliabilism** (*Ch. 8*) — A belief is justified when produced by a reliable process. Faces the → generality problem and the new evil demon objection.

**representation** (*Ch. 25*) — A state that stands for something and can be assessed for accuracy. The mark of the cognitive on many views; the dispute is whether all cognition is representational (→ enactivism says no).

**republicanism** (*Ch. 47*) — Pettit: freedom is non-domination — the absence of arbitrary power — not merely non-interference. ***Misuse:*** no connection to the American political party.

**rights** (*Ch. 47*) — Claims (or entitlements) that constrain others' treatment of the right-holder. Claim-rights vs. liberty-rights; whether rights are absolute, prima facie, or defeasible.

**rigid designation** (*Ch. 12*) — A term designates the same object in every possible world where it designates anything ("Aristotle" is rigid; "the teacher of Alexander" is not).

**rule-following** (*Ch. 31*) — Wittgenstein's problem: what makes an application of a rule correct, given that any finite history of use is compatible with deviant rules (→ quus)?

**rule utilitarianism** (*Ch. 45*) — The right act is the one conforming to rules whose general adoption maximizes welfare.

### S

**safety** (*Ch. 7*) — A belief is safe if it could not easily have been false (in nearby worlds where the subject forms the belief the same way). A leading anti-luck condition.

**scepticism** (*Ch. 6*) — The view that knowledge is impossible or that we cannot know the external world. ***Misuse:*** conflated with cynicism, or with cautious doubt in ordinary life; philosophical scepticism is a thesis about the possibility of knowledge.

**self-defeating** (*Ch. 13*) — An argument or position that undermines its own possibility. Two forms: logical (it entails its own negation) and pragmatic (asserting it requires what it denies). ***Misuse:*** declaring relativism self-refuting without constructing the argument; the charge is often made and rarely completed.

**self-evidence** (*Ch. 9*) — A proposition whose truth is grasped in the very understanding of it. ***Misuse:*** "self-evident" for "obvious to me"; self-evidence is a claim about the source of warrant, not about the strength of conviction.

**self, the** (*Ch. 26*) — See → no-self view, → identity, personal.

**semantics** (*Ch. 31*) — The theory of meaning and truth conditions. Contrast → pragmatics.

**sense / reference** (*Ch. 31*) — Frege: *Sinn* (mode of presentation) and *Bedeutung* (the designated object). Explains how "Hesperus is Phosphorus" can be informative despite identity of reference.

**sense-datum** (*Ch. 5*) — A private mental object of immediate awareness in perception. Its postulation generates the veil-of-perception problem and a regress of appearing.

**sensitivity** (*Ch. 7*) — Nozick: if $p$ were false, one would not believe $p$. Fails for beliefs about necessary truths and for knowledge of the actual world in certain inductive cases.

**simulation** (*Ch. 29, 6*) — **Two unrelated uses.** *Simulation theory* in mindreading: understanding others by running one's own cognitive processes offline. *Simulation hypothesis*: we may be in a computer simulation. ***Misuse:*** the shared word invites fallacious transfer of arguments; they are logically independent.

**situationism** (*Ch. 46*) — Doris, Harman: behavioural evidence suggests character traits are weak predictors relative to situations. Used to attack virtue ethics. ***Misuse:*** citing Milgram and Zimbardo as settled; their replications and ecological validity are seriously contested.

**skeptical theism** (*Ch. 50*) — The goods relevant to God's permission of evil may be beyond our cognitive reach, so the evidential argument from evil fails as an inference from what we see. Critics reply that this scepticism, if genuine, would undercut all moral knowledge.

**social epistemology** (*Ch. 11*) — The study of knowledge as a social achievement: testimony, institutions, division of epistemic labour, and the epistemics of expertise.

**solipsism** (*Ch. 6*) — Only I (or only my experiences) exist. Less a live position than the limit case that any account of other minds must show to be unmotivated.

**sortal** (*Ch. 26*) — A concept that supplies criteria of identity and individuation ("dog," "person"), as opposed to a mere characterization ("red").

**soundness** (*Ch. 2, 35*) — An argument is sound iff it is valid and all its premises are true. ***Misuse:*** "sound" used to mean "valid" (very common) or "convincing"; validity and truth of premises are separate, and soundness requires both.

**speech act** (*Ch. 34*) — An action performed in saying something: promising, warning, asserting, marrying.

**steel-manning** (*Ch. 2*) — Reconstructing an opponent's position in its strongest form before criticizing it.

**straw man** (*Ch. 4*) — Refuting a weakened version of an opponent's view and claiming victory over the original.

**structural realism** (*Ch. 40*) — Our theories capture structure, not intrinsic natures (Worrall). Offers a response to the pessimistic meta-induction; contested for whether it can state its own central claim without asserting knowledge of natures.

**structuralism** (*Ch. 43*) — In mathematics: numbers are positions in structures, not independently existing objects. ***Misuse:*** unrelated to structuralism in social theory, in literary criticism, or in philosophy of science about theories; specify the domain.

**sub specie aeternitatis** (*Ch. 54*) — "Under the aspect of eternity" (Spinoza): seeing things as they are independent of one's particular interests and position. Russell borrows it for his account of philosophy's value.

**substratum** (*Ch. 15*) — The supposed bare particular that bears properties without itself being a property. Contrast → bundle theory.

**supererogation** (*Ch. 45*) — Action beyond the call of duty: good but not required. Threatens act-consequentialism, which leaves no space for it.

**supervenience** (*Ch. 21*) — No difference in $A$ without a difference in $B$: the $A$-facts are fixed by the $B$-facts. ***Misuse:*** treated as a form of causation or explanation; supervenience is a covariation thesis and is compatible with epiphenomenalism.

**syllogism** (*Ch. 35*) — A categorical argument with two premises and a conclusion; the core of Aristotelian logic.

**śūnyatā** (*Ch. 15, thread*) — Emptiness: things lack intrinsic, independent existence and are constituted by relations (Nāgārjuna). ***Misuse:*** translating as "nothingness"; the doctrine is a middle way between eternalism and nihilism, not a denial that anything exists.

### T

**tabula rasa** (*Ch. 31*) — The blank-slate view of the mind; the empiricist counter to → nativism.

**teleology** (*Ch. 42*) — Explanation by purpose or function. ***Misuse:*** assuming any functional explanation implies a designer; etiological accounts of function are naturalistic.

**teleosemantics** (*Ch. 25, 42*) — Millikan, Dretske: representational content is fixed by the biological function of the representing state. Faces the indeterminacy of function ascription.

**testimony** (*Ch. 11*) — Knowledge acquired from others' say-so. Reductionism (Hume) requires independent grounds for trusting each source; anti-reductionism (Reid) grants a default entitlement.

**thought experiment** (*Ch. 3*) — An imagined case used to test a principle or elicit a judgement. ***Misuse:*** treating a vivid scenario as evidence without isolating the variable it is supposed to isolate; and ignoring that intuitions about far-fetched cases may be artefacts of the far-fetchedness.

**token / type** (*Ch. 23*) — A type is a kind; a token is an instance. Type identity: all pain is C-fibre firing. Token identity: each particular pain is some physical event. ***Misuse:*** unrelated to "token" in the sense of tokenism; the type/token distinction is a logical relation between kinds and instances.

**transcendental argument** (*Ch. 52*) — An argument that some feature of experience is necessary for the possibility of something we take for granted (Kant). ***Misuse:*** "transcendental" confused with "transcendent" (beyond experience) and with meditation practices; the philosophical term names a mode of argument, not a spiritual one.

**transcendental idealism** (*Ch. 20*) — Kant: space, time, and the categories are forms contributed by the subject; empirical realism holds within it.

**trolley problem** (*Ch. 45*) — A family of cases probing the difference between doing and allowing, intended and foreseen. ***Misuse:*** treating a survey result about a trolley as a datum about morality without asking what the case is a case *of*.

**trope** (*Ch. 15*) — A particular property-instance: this redness here, not redness as such. Trope theory offers a one-category ontology.

**truth** (*Ch. 32*) — The property (or device) in virtue of which true sentences are true. Correspondence, coherence, pragmatist, and deflationary accounts; truthmaker theory supplies the metaphysics of the correspondence intuition. ***Misuse:*** "my truth" is not a philosophical position but a rejection of the concept under discussion; if intended as relativism, it must be argued for.

**truth-apt** (*Ch. 44*) — Capable of being true or false. Non-cognitivists deny that moral sentences are truth-apt in the minimal sense, or explain their truth-aptness derivatively.

**truthmaker** (*Ch. 22*) — That in virtue of which a truth is true. The truthmaker principle: $p$ is true because something makes it so.

**Turing test** (*Ch. 29*) — A behavioural criterion of intelligence: indistinguishability from a human interlocutor. ***Misuse:*** treating it as a test of consciousness or as a definition of intelligence; Turing offered it as a replacement for the question, not an analysis of the mind.

**Twin Earth** (*Ch. 25*) — Putnam's scenario: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate world where "water" refers to XYZ. Shows that meanings are not in the head.

**two dogmas** (*Ch. 36*) — Quine's attack on the analytic/synthetic distinction and on reductionism, concluding with a holistic pragmatism about the web of belief.

### U

**ubuntu** (*Ch. 47, thread*) — Southern African conception of personhood as constituted by relations: a person is a person through other persons. A systematic alternative to atomistic accounts of the self.

**underdetermination** (*Ch. 40*) — Evidence is compatible with rival theories, so evidence alone cannot decide. ***Misuse:*** conflated with indeterminacy (there being no fact of the matter); underdetermination is an epistemic relation between theory and evidence.

**understanding** (*Ch. 38*) — Grasping why and how, as opposed to merely knowing that. Whether it is reducible to knowledge of explanations, and whether it is factive, are live questions.

**universal** (*Ch. 15*) — A repeatable property or relation, as opposed to a → particular. Realism, nominalism, and trope theories divide on whether universals exist.

**utilitarianism** (*Ch. 45*) — The right act maximizes aggregate welfare. Act vs. rule; hedonistic vs. preference; total vs. average vs. prioritarian.

### V

**vagueness** (*Ch. 33*) — Borderline cases with no sharp boundary (heap, bald). Theories: epistemicism (there is a boundary we cannot know), supervaluationism (truth is truth on all sharpenings), degree theories, contextualism. ***Misuse:*** confused with ambiguity (multiple meanings) and with generality (covering many cases); only vagueness generates the sorites.

**validity** (*Ch. 2, 35*) — An argument is valid iff the premises cannot be true while the conclusion is false. ***Misuse:*** "valid" for "true," "good," or "sound" — the single most common technical error in undergraduate writing. Validity is a property of argument *form*, and a valid argument with false premises is still valid.

**value** (*Ch. 44*) — Worth or goodness. Intrinsic vs. instrumental; final vs. contributory; prudential vs. moral vs. aesthetic.

**veil of perception** (*Ch. 20*) — The problem: if we are directly aware only of sense-data, how can we know anything about mind-independent objects?

**veridical** (*Ch. 5*) — Of a perception: accurately presenting its object. A veridical hallucination is a hallucination that happens to match reality.

**verificationism** (*Ch. 31, 52*) — A sentence is meaningful only if it is analytic or empirically verifiable. Self-undermining on its own criterion; survives in weakened forms as a constraint on empirical content.

**virtue epistemology** (*Ch. 8*) — Knowledge is true belief attributable to the knower's intellectual competence (Sosa, Zagzebski, Greco). Solves the value problem elegantly; faces the generality problem in a new form.

**virtue ethics** (*Ch. 45*) — The right action is what a virtuous person would do; the primary evaluative notions are character and flourishing. ***Misuse:*** thought to be action-guiding in the way a decision procedure is; most virtue ethicists deny that it should be.

### W

**warranted assertibility** (*Ch. 32*) — Dewey's replacement for truth: the status a claim earns through successful inquiry. Whether this is a theory of truth or a theory of justification is the standard objection.

**weakness of will** (*Ch. 28*) — See → akrasia.

**well-being** (*Ch. 48*) — What is good for a person: hedonistic, desire-based, objective-list, or hybrid accounts.

**will to believe** (*Ch. 50*) — James: where evidence is inconclusive and the choice is forced, live, and momentous, it can be rational to believe. Carefully limited by James; often over-extended in citation.

**wisdom** (*Ch. 54*) — *Sophia*: theoretical grasp of the deepest truths. *Phronesis*: practical judgement in particular situations. Philosophy's claim to promote wisdom rests more on the second than on the first.

**wu wei** (*Ch. 22, thread*) — Daoist non-action: acting without forcing, in accord with the grain of things. Not passivity; a distinctive account of effective agency.

---

### X

**x-phi (experimental philosophy)** (*Ch. 3, 9*) — The empirical study of philosophically relevant intuitions and their variation. Its findings bear on the evidential status of intuition, not directly on the truth of the theories intuitions were used to support.

---

### Y

**yi** (*Ch. 45, thread*) — Confucian fittingness or rightness: the appropriateness of an action to the situation and to one's relations, as distinct from what is merely profitable.

**Yogācāra** (*Ch. 20, thread*) — The Buddhist "consciousness-only" school: experience isstructured by consciousness, and the subject–object split is a construction. A sophisticated idealism whose arguments deserve a place beside Berkeley and Kant.

---

### Z

**ziran** (*Ch. 22, thread*) — Daoist spontaneity or self-so-ness: the way things are when unobstructed. Metaphysically, a rejection of imposed order; ethically, a model for action.

**zombie, philosophical** (*Ch. 24*) — A being physically identical to you with no conscious experience. Conceivability of zombies is used to argue for property dualism; the reply disputes whether the conceivability is genuine or only apparent.

## Appendix B.1 — The Ten Confusions Most Worth Memorizing

A summary for revision. Each entry names a pair that experienced teachers see confused more than any other.

1. **Validity vs. truth** — an argument can be valid with false premises; a true statement is not an argument at all. (*Ch. 2, 35*)
2. **Bivalence vs. excluded middle** — one is about truth-values, the other a logical schema. (*Ch. 35*)
3. **Determinism vs. fatalism** — the first implicates causes, the second denies the efficacy of action. (*Ch. 27*)
4. **Metaethics vs. normative ethics** — the metaethical anti-realist is not thereby recommending wickedness. (*Ch. 44*)
5. **Naturalistic fallacy vs. is/ought gap** — Moore's argument and Hume's are distinct, and Moore's is the stronger. (*Ch. 44*)
6. **Phenomenalism vs. phenomenology** — a reductionist metaphysics of objects vs. a descriptive method of experience. (*Ch. 5, 20*)
7. **Libertarianism (metaphysical) vs. libertarianism (political)** — shared word, unshared content. (*Ch. 27, 47*)
8. **Realism (four senses)** — metaphysical, scientific, moral, semantic: arguing for one does not establish another. (*Ch. 22*)
9. **Explanatory vs. normative "reason"** — the cause of an action and the justification of it. (*Ch. 28*)
10. **Simulation theory vs. simulation hypothesis; intentionalism in perception vs. in criticism** — pairs that share a name and nothing else. (*Ch. 5, 6, 29*)

## Appendix B.2 — Note on Scope and Choices

Three editorial decisions are worth flagging.

**First, contested terms are defined by their function, not by a verdict.** *Knowledge*, *cause*, *consciousness*, and *justice* each receive the sense in which the debate uses them; no entry settles the debate. Where a term has a dominant technical sense that differs from the popular one, the technical sense is given first and the divergence flagged.

**Second, non-Western terms are entered as theories, not as exotica.** *Pramāṇa*, *śūnyatā*, *anattā*, *ren*, *wu wei*, *ubuntu*, and *Yogācāra* are included because the book treats them as systematic positions with arguments, and because a glossary that silently equates "philosophy" with "the Anglophone canon" would falsify its own subject matter.

**Third, the glossary is a diagnostic instrument.** The ***Misuse*** flags are not pedantry: nearly every one identifies a slide that has done real damage in published work — the equation of supervenience with explanation, the transfer of arguments across the four realisms, the treatment of a vivid case as a decisive counterexample. Read the flags as traps marked on a map, not as rules of etiquette.
