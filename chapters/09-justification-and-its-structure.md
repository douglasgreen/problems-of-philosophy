# Chapter 8 — Justification and Its Structure

> “If we have justification for believing anything, we must have justification for believing something without justification.”
> — The structure of the regress argument, stated in the form that makes it look impossible

> “The whole is greater than the sum of its parts.”
> — A modern paraphrase of Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, Z.17 (H. 1045a10–13), on why the coherentist thinks justification is holistic

---

Chapter 7 left us with an unfinished account. Knowledge requires justification, or warrant, or an anti-luck condition — every analysis agreed on that much. But what *is* justification? What makes a belief justified? And how is a justification *structured*: does it rest on a foundation, or does it consist in a web, or does it depend on a process, or on a virtue, or on nothing at all?

These questions are the subject of this chapter. They are older than the Gettier problem, and in some ways they are deeper: the Gettier problem is about a *condition* on knowledge, but the structure of justification is about the *whole architecture* of epistemic life. The regress problem was stated by Agrippa two thousand years ago; the responses to it — foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism, reliabilism, virtue epistemology — are the grand strategies of modern epistemology. None has won. All have taught us something.

The chapter proceeds in eight movements. §1 states the regress problem and the trilemma it generates. §2 examines foundationalism, the oldest and most intuitive response, and the Sellarsian dilemma that threatens it. §3 examines coherentism, the holistic alternative, and the isolation objection that threatens it. §4 examines reliabilism, the naturalistic alternative, and the generality problem that threatens it. §5 examines the internalism/externalism debate, which cuts across the other three, along with the evidentialist conception of justification that most of the debate presupposes, the challenge of pragmatic encroachment, and the deontological conception of responsibility. §6 examines defeasibility, the logic of defeat, and the structure of epistemic reasons. §7 examines virtue epistemology, the most recent synthesis, and the responsibility condition. §8 gives an honest verdict on the state of the debate.

The connecting thread is a single question: **what is the relation between a belief and the reasons for it?** Every theory answers this question differently, and the answers determine what can be said about knowledge, skepticism, and the value of inquiry.

## 1. The Regress Problem

### 1.1 The structure of the problem

The regress problem is the problem of **justification's stopping point**. The structure is simple and apparently inescapable:

1. A belief is justified only if it is supported by a reason.
2. The reason is itself a belief (or a proposition), and it is justified only if it is supported by a reason.
3. And so on.

The regress is generated by the **principle of sufficient reason for belief**: a belief is justified only if there is a further belief (or proposition) that justifies it. If the principle holds universally *and the chain of supporting reasons must be completed*, then no belief is justified, because the chain of reasons would be infinite.

The **Agrippan trilemma** (Ch. 6, §3.2) states the available options:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
(\mathrm{Infinitism}) & \text{The chain of reasons is infinite.} \\
(\mathrm{Foundationalism}) & \text{The chain terminates in basic beliefs.} \\
(\mathrm{Coherentism}) & \text{The chain is circular — but the circle is virtuous.}
\end{array}
$$

The trilemma is a genuine *trilemma*, not a dilemma: the three horns are distinct, and none of them is simply a version of another. But for anyone who accepts the principle of sufficient reason for belief, the horns are not equally available — two of them (infinitism and coherentism) are ruled out by considerations the skeptic shares, and the third (foundationalism) is available only if the principle is relaxed at the base. Each option has a cost. The question is which cost is acceptable.

### 1.2 Infinitism

**Infinitism** is the view that the chain of reasons is infinite, and that this is not a defect. The view is associated with Peter Klein (1999, 2007) and, in a different form, with the Pyrrhonian skeptics.

**The argument.** The regress is not vicious if the requirement is not that the chain be *completed*, but that it be *available*. A belief is justified if, for every reason that supports it, there is a further reason available (not necessarily *had*). Justification is a **dispositional** property: the subject is justified if she *could* produce the reasons, not if she has actually produced them.

**The objection.** The view seems to require an infinite cognitive capacity. If justification requires an infinite chain of available reasons, no finite subject is ever justified. The infinitist's reply: the requirement is *potential*, not *actual*; the subject needs the *ability* to extend the chain, not the *actuality* of having extended it. Whether this is a coherent notion is contested. (See the exchange between Klein and his critics in *Philosophical Studies* 134 (2007), and the survey in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry on infinitism.)

**The verdict.** Infinitism is a minority position. It has the virtue of taking the regress seriously, and the vice of making justification depend on a capacity that no finite subject can exercise. Its most important contribution is negative: it shows that the other two options are not the only ones, and that the trilemma is not exhaustive.

### 1.3 The structure of the debate

The debate between foundationalism and coherentism has a characteristic shape, which is worth stating in advance so the reader can locate each theory.

```
                        THE REGRESS
                            |
              +-------------+-------------+
              |                           |
        FOUNDATIONALISM              COHERENTISM
              |                           |
    "The chain stops in              "The chain is a
     basic beliefs"                    circle, and
              |                        the circle is
              |                        virtuous"
              |                           |
              v                           v
     Basic beliefs are:            Justification is:
     - self-evident                - holistic
     - infallible                  - explanatory
     - non-doxastic                - systematic
              |                           |
              v                           v
     SELLARSIAN DILEMMA            ISOLATION OBJECTION
     (the given is conceptual       (coherence is not
     or non-conceptual)             truth-conducive)
```

The diagram captures the **symmetry** of the debate: each view has a characteristic solution and a characteristic problem. The foundationalist solves the regress by positing a stopping point; the coherentist solves it by making the stopping point unnecessary. The foundationalist's problem is that the stopping point is either unjustified (the given) or not a stopping point (it needs justification); the coherentist's problem is that the circle is either too permissive (any coherent system counts) or too demanding (no actual system is coherent enough).

### 1.4 The meta-question: what is the regress a regress *of*?

The regress problem is often stated as a problem about *beliefs*. But the debate has shown that the formulation is too narrow. The regress is a regress of **epistemic support**, and support can be borne by different kinds of things:

| Relatum | What supports the belief | Theory |
|---|---|---|
| **Beliefs** | Other beliefs | Doxastic foundationalism / coherentism |
| **Experiences** | Non-doxastic states | Experiential foundationalism |
| **Facts** | Truths that obtain | Factual foundationalism |
| **Reliabilities** | Processes that produce the belief | Reliabilism |
| **Virtues** | Competences of the agent | Virtue epistemology |
| **Evidence** | The subject’s total evidence | Evidentialism |

The choice of relatum determines the shape of the regress. If support is borne by beliefs, the regress is a regress of beliefs, and the question is whether it terminates. If support is borne by experiences, the regress terminates in the experience, and the question is whether the experience can justify a belief (the Sellarsian dilemma). If support is borne by reliabilities, the regress is not a regress at all: the reliability is a fact about the process, not a reason the subject must possess.

The **meta-question** is therefore: *what kind of thing is epistemic support?* The answer determines which of the trilemma's options is available, and which problems are urgent.

### 1.5 The principle of sufficient reason for belief

The regress argument depends on the principle that a belief is justified only if it is supported by a further reason. The principle is not obviously true, and its denial is the strategy of several responses.

**The denial.** A belief can be justified without being supported by a further reason, if it is **basic** — if it is the kind of belief that does not need support. The foundationalist's task is to say what makes a belief basic, and why basic beliefs are justified.

**The reformulation.** The principle can be weakened: a belief is justified if it is supported by a *sufficient* reason, not by a *further* reason. A sufficient reason may be a reason that does not itself require support. This is the foundationalist's strategy, and it is available only if there are reasons that are self-justifying or non-doxastic.

**The deflationary response.** The principle can be rejected as a **philosophical artifact**: ordinary epistemic practice does not require an infinite chain of reasons, and the demand for one is a demand generated by a theory, not by the practice. This is the Wittgensteinian response (Ch. 6, §6.5), and it is a form of quietism.

The debate between these responses is the subject of the rest of the chapter.

## 2. Foundationalism

### 2.1 The classical picture

**Foundationalism** is the view that justified beliefs rest on a foundation of **basic beliefs** — beliefs that are justified without being supported by other beliefs. The metaphor is architectural: the building (the edifice of knowledge) rests on a foundation (the basic beliefs), and the foundation rests on nothing (it is self-supporting).

The classical statement is Descartes’ *Meditations*: the *cogito* is the foundation, and the rest of knowledge is deduced from it (with the help of God). The classical **empiricist** statement is the sense-datum theory (Ch. 5, §3): the basic beliefs are beliefs about sense-data, and they are justified by the **given** — the immediate deliverances of experience.

The classical picture has three components:

1. **The base**: a set of basic beliefs, justified without inference.
2. **The superstructure**: the non-basic beliefs, justified by inference from the base.
3. **The relation**: the inference is deductive (in the classical picture) or inductive (in the modest picture).

The picture is intuitive, and it has a long history. Its problems are also long-standing.

### 2.2 Versions of foundationalism

The classical picture has been refined in response to its problems. The main versions:

| Version | What makes a belief basic | Proponents | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Classical (strong) foundationalism** | The belief is self-evident or infallible | Descartes; Chisholm (1966) | The class of basic beliefs is too narrow; the inference to the superstructure is too strong |
| **Modest foundationalism** | The belief is non-inferentially justified, but fallible | Chisholm (1977); Audi (1993) | The “non-inferential” justification is hard to characterize |
| **Experiential foundationalism** | The belief is caused by an experience, and the experience justifies it | Pollock (1986); Pryor (2000) | The Sellarsian dilemma (§2.4) |
| **Externalist foundationalism** | The belief is produced by a reliable process, and it is basic in the sense that it does not depend on other beliefs | Plantinga (1993); Bergmann (2006) | The generality problem (§4.3) |
| **Phenomenal conservatism** | The belief is justified by a *seeming* | Huemer (2001, 2005) | The permissiveness objection |

The versions differ on **what the foundation is** and on **how strong the justification is**. The classical version is strong and narrow; the modest version is weak and broad. The debate is about which combination is defensible.

### 2.3 The classical arguments

The classical foundationalist has three arguments for the existence of a foundation.

**(i) The regress argument.** If there is no foundation, the regress is infinite or circular, and neither is acceptable. Therefore there is a foundation. (This is the argument of §1.)

**(ii) The argument from the structure of knowledge.** Knowledge is a *system*: the justification of one belief depends on the justification of others, and the system must be *grounded* if it is to be justified. The ground is the foundation.

**(iii) The phenomenological argument.** Some beliefs are *self-evidently* justified: they carry their justification with them, and no further reason is needed. The belief that I am in pain, the belief that $2+2=4$, the belief that I seem to see a red patch — these are the paradigms.

The arguments are not decisive. The regress argument is only as strong as the denial of infinitism and coherentism; the structural argument is a metaphor, not an argument; the phenomenological argument is contestable (the self-evidence of the cogito is exactly what the skeptic denies).

### 2.4 The Sellarsian dilemma

The most important objection to foundationalism is **Sellars’s dilemma** (1956, §§1–4), and it is the objection that the classical picture has never fully answered.

**The dilemma.** The foundationalist claims that basic beliefs are justified by the **given** — the immediate deliverance of experience. But the given is either:

1. **Conceptual**: it has propositional content, and it can stand in logical relations. But then it is not *given*; it is already shaped by concepts, and it needs justification like any other belief.
2. **Non-conceptual**: it is a raw sensation, without propositional content. But then it cannot stand in logical relations, and it cannot justify a belief. A sensation can *cause* a belief, but causation is not justification.

**The argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{A basic belief is justified by the given.} \\
2. & \text{The given is either conceptual or non-conceptual.} \\
3. & \text{If conceptual, the given is not basic (it needs justification).} \\
4. & \text{If non-conceptual, the given cannot justify (causation is not justification).} \\
\hline
5. & \therefore \text{There are no basic beliefs in the classical sense.}
\end{array}
$$

**The conclusion.** The classical foundationalist project fails. The foundation is either not a foundation (it needs support) or not a justification (it is merely causal).

**The responses.** The dilemma has generated a large literature, and the responses are the main versions of foundationalism.

**(i) Deny the dichotomy.** The given is *conceptual* but *non-doxastic*: it has propositional content, but it is not a belief, and it does not need justification. This is the view of **experiential foundationalism** (Pollock 1986; Pryor 2000; Huemer 2001). The experience of a red patch has the content “there is a red patch,” and the content justifies the belief, without the experience being a belief.

**The objection.** If the experience has propositional content, it is in the space of reasons, and the question of its justification arises. If it does not, it cannot justify. The dichotomy seems to reassert itself at the level of content.

**(ii) Deny the “causation is not justification” premise.** The causal relation between experience and belief can *itself* be a justification relation, if the causation is of the right kind. This is the **externalist** response (Plantinga 1993; Bergmann 2006): the experience justifies the belief because it is a reliable indicator of the truth, not because the subject can *see* the connection.

**The objection.** This is a version of reliabilism (§4), and it faces the generality problem and the new evil demon problem.

**(iii) Deny the “logical relations” requirement.** Justification does not require the ability to *state* the logical relation; it requires only that the relation *obtain*. This is the **reliabilist** response, and it is the same as (ii).

**(iv) Deny that the given is needed.** The belief is justified by the *absence* of defeaters, not by a positive foundation. This is the **defeasibility** response (§6), and it is a form of **coherentism** (the justification is holistic, not foundational).

The dilemma is not resolved. It is the deepest problem in the theory of justification, and it is the reason the debate between foundationalism and coherentism has not converged.

### 2.5 Modest foundationalism

The most defensible version of foundationalism is **modest foundationalism**: the view that basic beliefs are non-inferentially justified, but *fallible* — they can be defeated by further evidence.

**The view.** A belief is basic if it is justified without being inferred from other beliefs. The justification is *prima facie*: it holds in the absence of defeaters, but it can be overridden. The foundation is not a set of indubitable beliefs; it is a set of *defeasibly* justified beliefs.

**The advantage.** Modest foundationalism avoids the classical problems: it does not require infallible basic beliefs, and it does not require a deductive inference to the superstructure. The foundation is a set of *starting points*, not a set of *certainties*.

**The problem.** If basic beliefs are fallible, what distinguishes them from non-basic beliefs? The answer: they are *non-inferentially* justified. But what is non-inferential justification? The answer is contested. The candidates:

- **Self-evidence**: the belief is justified by its own content (Audi 1993).
- **Experience**: the belief is justified by the experience that causes it (Pryor 2000).
- **Seeming**: the belief is justified by a *seeming* (Huemer 2001).
- **Reliability**: the belief is justified by the reliability of the process (Plantinga 1993).

The choice among these is the choice among the versions of §2.2, and the debate is unresolved.

### 2.6 The Reidian alternative

A distinct version of foundationalism, due to Thomas Reid (1764) and developed by Plantinga (1993, 2000), holds that the basic beliefs are the deliverances of **common sense** — the beliefs that are presupposed by all inquiry, and that are justified by the **proper functioning** of our cognitive faculties.

**The view.** The basic beliefs are not the narrow class of self-evident truths; they are the broad class of beliefs that human beings form spontaneously: the belief in an external world, the belief in other minds, the belief in the uniformity of nature, the belief in the reliability of memory. These beliefs are justified because they are the products of faculties that are *designed* (by God, or by evolution) to produce true beliefs in the environment for which they were designed.

**The argument.** The alternative — that these beliefs are not justified — is self-defeating, because the skeptic’s argument relies on the same faculties. If the faculties are unreliable, the skeptic’s argument is unreliable too.

**The objection.** The “proper function” condition is speculative: it requires a design plan, and the design plan is not empirically accessible. The evolutionary version (Plantinga’s later work) is even more speculative, and it is the subject of the “evolutionary argument against naturalism” (Ch. 3, §5.2), which is contested.

The Reidian alternative is important because it is the most *naturalistic* version of foundationalism, and because it connects the theory of justification to the theory of knowledge (Ch. 7) and to the epistemology of testimony (Ch. 11).

## 3. Coherentism

### 3.1 The coherentist picture

**Coherentism** is the view that a belief is justified by its *coherence* with the rest of the subject’s beliefs, not by a foundation. The metaphor is the **web** or the **raft**: the beliefs support one another, and the system as a whole is justified by its internal structure.

The classical statement is the **British idealists** (Bradley, Bosanquet), and the **logical positivists** (Neurath’s boat: “We are like sailors who must rebuild their ship on the open sea”). The contemporary statement is **BonJour** (1985) and **Lehrer** (1990).

The core claims:

1. **Holism**: justification is a property of the *system* of beliefs, not of individual beliefs.
2. **Mutual support**: beliefs justify one another by their relations of entailment, explanation, and coherence.
3. **No foundation**: there are no basic beliefs; the regress is stopped by the *circle* of mutual support.

The view is a response to the failure of foundationalism: if the foundation is either unjustified (the given) or not a foundation (it needs support), then the only alternative is a system of mutual support.

### 3.2 The coherence relation

The central question is: **what is coherence?** The candidates:

| Conception | What coherence is | Proponents | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Logical consistency** | The absence of contradiction | The minimal conception | Too weak: a consistent system can be arbitrary |
| **Entailment** | The beliefs entail one another | The strong conception | Too strong: most justified systems are not deductively closed |
| **Explanatory coherence** | The beliefs provide mutual explanations | BonJour (1985); Thagard (1989) | The notion of explanation is contested |
| **Probabilistic coherence** | The beliefs are mutually probable | The Bayesian conception | The problem of the priors (Ch. 10, §5) |
| **Systematic coherence** | The beliefs form a unified system | Rescher (1973) | The notion of “system” is vague |

The choice among these is the choice among the versions of coherentism, and the debate is unresolved. The most influential is BonJour’s **explanatory coherence**: a system is coherent to the extent that its members explain one another, and the explanation relation is the source of justification.

### 3.3 The isolation objection

The most important objection to coherentism is the **isolation objection**: coherence is a relation among beliefs, and it is *isolated* from the world. A system of beliefs can be perfectly coherent and entirely false. The coherentist has no way to ensure that the system is *about* the world, and therefore no way to ensure that it is justified.

**The objection stated.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Coherence is a relation among beliefs.} \\
2. & \text{A system of beliefs can be coherent and false.} \\
3. & \text{If a system is coherent and false, it is not justified (or at least not knowledge).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Coherence is not sufficient for justification.}
\end{array}
$$

**The responses.**

**(i) The “input” response** (BonJour 1985). The system is constrained by *experience*: the beliefs must be *responsive* to the input of experience, and the coherence must include the experiences themselves. BonJour’s later view (2004): the system is justified if it is coherent *and* it is “cognitively spontaneous” — it is formed by a process that is responsive to the world.

**The objection.** This is a version of foundationalism: the experience is the foundation, and the coherence is the superstructure. BonJour’s view is a *hybrid*, and the hybrid needs an account of how the experience justifies the belief (the Sellarsian dilemma again).

**(ii) The “externalist” response** (Lehrer 1990). The coherence is not merely among beliefs; it is among beliefs *and* the facts that cause them. The system is justified if it is coherent *and* it is *undefeated* by the subject’s evidence, including the evidence of experience.

**The objection.** This is a version of reliabilism, and it faces the generality problem.

**(iii) The “pragmatic” response.** The isolation objection assumes that justification must be *truth-conducive*. The coherentist can deny the assumption: justification is a *pragmatic* property, not a truth-conducive one. The system is justified if it is *useful*, not if it is *true*.

**The objection.** This is a form of pragmatism (Ch. 32, §4), and it is a change of subject: it gives up the claim that justification is about truth.

The isolation objection is not decisive, but it is serious. The coherentist needs an account of how the system is *connected* to the world, and the account is not easy to give without reintroducing a foundation.

### 3.4 The “no-contact” objection

A stronger version of the isolation objection: the coherentist cannot explain why a coherent system is *about* anything. If the system is a closed circle of beliefs, the question “what is it about?” has no answer. The system is a *fiction* that is internally consistent, like a well-written novel.

The coherentist’s response: the system is not closed; it is *caused* by experience, and the causal connection is what makes it about the world. But the causal connection is not itself a *justification*; it is a fact about the system’s etiology. (This is the same problem as the Sellarsian dilemma: causation is not justification.)

### 3.5 The “alternative coherent systems” objection

A third objection: there are many coherent systems, and they are incompatible. The coherentist has no way to choose among them, because the choice would require a standard that is not internal to the systems.

**The response.** The coherentist can appeal to **explanatory virtue**: the system that is *more* coherent, *more* explanatory, *simpler*, is preferable. But this is a version of the criteria of theory choice (Ch. 4, §3), and the criteria are contested.

**The objection.** The criteria are not *internal* to the system; they are *external* standards, and the coherentist has no way to justify them without appealing to something beyond coherence.

The objection is serious, and it is the reason coherentism is often combined with a **naturalistic** account of the standards (e.g., the standards are the ones that are *reliable* in practice; Ch. 4, §3.3).

### 3.6 The state of coherentism

Coherentism is a minority position, but it has had a large influence. Its central insight — that justification is **holistic**, and that the relations among beliefs matter — is accepted by most contemporary epistemologists, even those who reject the full coherentist picture.

The **hybrid** positions — **foundationalist coherentism** (the foundation is the input, the coherence is the structure), **reliabilist coherentism** (the coherence is a reliable process), **virtue coherentism** (the coherence is the exercise of a virtue) — are the most promising developments. The pure coherentist picture is widely thought to be untenable, because of the isolation objection; the hybrid pictures are the attempt to keep the insight while avoiding the objection.

## 4. Reliabilism and the Generality Problem

### 4.1 The reliabilist picture

**Reliabilism** is the view that a belief is justified if it is produced by a **reliable process** — a process that tends to produce true beliefs.

The canonical statement is **Goldman** (1979), and the view has been developed by **Sosa** (1991), **Plantinga** (1993), **Bergmann** (2006), and many others.

The core claims:

1. **Justification is a property of the process**, not of the belief’s content or the subject’s evidence.
2. **The process is reliable** if it produces a high proportion of true beliefs in the relevant environments.
3. **The subject need not have access** to the reliability; the justification is *external* to the subject’s perspective.

The view is *naturalistic*: it treats justification as a fact about the world (the reliability of the process), not as a fact about the subject’s reasons. It is also *externalist*: the subject need not be able to *state* the justification.

### 4.2 The arguments

**(i) The “truth-conduciveness” argument.** Justification is valuable because it is truth-conducive. The reliabilist identifies justification with the property that makes it truth-conducive: the reliability of the process. The view is therefore *theoretically* motivated: it explains why justification matters.

**(ii) The “naturalistic” argument.** Justification is a natural phenomenon: it is a property of cognitive systems, and it can be studied by the sciences. The reliabilist offers a naturalistic account of the property.

**(iii) The “anti-skeptical” argument.** The reliabilist can respond to the skeptic (Ch. 6, §6.3) by arguing that our perceptual processes are reliable, and that reliability is sufficient for justification. The response is not a *refutation* of the skeptic, but a *reconceptualization* of the problem.

### 4.3 The generality problem

The most important objection to reliabilism is the **generality problem** (Goldman 1979; Feldman 1985; Conee & Feldman 1998): every belief is produced by *many* processes, and some of them are reliable while others are not. The question is: *which process* is the relevant one?

**The problem stated.** Consider a belief formed by perception. The process can be described as:

- “Seeing” (reliable, in normal conditions)
- “Seeing in good light” (more reliable)
- “Seeing in conditions C” (reliable in C, unreliable in C′)
- “Forming a belief by a process that has produced this token belief” (trivially reliable)
- “Forming a belief by a process that is unreliable in this particular case” (trivially unreliable)

The description determines the verdict, and the description is **underdetermined** by the belief itself. The reliabilist needs a principled way to select the relevant description, and no such way has been found.

**The proposed solutions.**

**(i) The “natural” solution.** The relevant process is the one that is *psychologically real* — the one that is actually implemented in the cognitive system. The solution is naturalistic, but it is underdeveloped: the psychological reality of a process is a matter of empirical fact, and the fact is contested.

**(ii) The “contextualist” solution.** The relevant process is determined by the *context* of the attributor, like the standards of knowledge (Ch. 7, §6.1). The solution is a version of contextualism, and it inherits the problems of contextualism.

**(iii) The “dispositional” solution.** The relevant process is the *disposition* that the subject exercises — the general capacity, not the particular token. The solution is intuitive, but the notion of a “disposition” is vague, and the vagueness is the problem.

**(iv) The “no solution” response.** Some reliabilists treat the problem as a *research question* rather than a refutation: the theory is a *research program* (Goldman 1986), and the generality problem is one of its open tasks. The response is honest, but it concedes that the theory is incomplete.

The generality problem is the reason reliabilism is often combined with **virtue epistemology** (§7): the virtue is the stable disposition of the agent, and the disposition is the relevant process. Whether this solves the problem is contested.

### 4.4 The new evil demon

The second major objection to reliabilism is the **new evil demon** problem (Cohen 1984).

**The case.** A subject in a demon world has exactly the same internal states as a subject in the actual world — the same experiences, the same beliefs, the same reasoning. But the demon ensures that the beliefs are false. The subject’s processes are *unreliable* (they produce false beliefs), but the subject seems *justified* — she is as rational as we are.

**The argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The demon-world subject is internally identical to the actual-world subject.} \\
2. & \text{The actual-world subject is justified.} \\
3. & \text{If the subjects are internally identical, they have the same justification.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The demon-world subject is justified.} \\
5. & \text{But the demon-world subject’s processes are unreliable.} \\
\hline
6. & \therefore \text{Reliability is not necessary for justification.}
\end{array}
$$

**The responses.**

**(i) The “externalist” response** (Goldman 1988, 1993). The demon-world subject is *not* justified, in the relevant sense. She is *subjectively* justified (she is doing the best she can), but she is not *objectively* justified (her beliefs are not reliable). The distinction between subjective and objective justification is the reliabilist’s way of accommodating the intuition without giving up the theory.

**The objection.** The distinction is *ad hoc*: it is introduced to save the theory, not because it is independently motivated. And it is not clear that the demon-world subject is *less* justified in the relevant sense; she seems as justified as we are.

**(ii) The “proper function” response** (Plantinga 1993). The demon-world subject’s faculties are *not* functioning properly (they are designed for a world that the demon has replaced), so she is not justified. The response depends on the design plan, which is speculative.

**(iii) The “virtue” response** (Sosa 1991, 2007). The demon-world subject exercises the same *competences* as the actual-world subject, so she is justified *in the internal sense*. But she lacks the *external* condition (the competence is not successful), so she lacks knowledge. The response distinguishes *justification* (the exercise of competence) from *warrant* (the success of the competence), and it is the basis of the virtue-theoretic synthesis (§7).

The new evil demon is the most important objection to reliabilism, and the responses are the main versions of the theory. The debate is unresolved.

### 4.5 Reliabilism and the structure of justification

Reliabilism has a distinctive implication for the regress problem: the regress **does not arise**. Justification is a property of the process, not of the chain of reasons. The process is reliable or not, and the reliability is a fact about the world, not a reason the subject must possess.

The implication is both the theory’s strength and its weakness. It is a strength because it avoids the trilemma (Ch. 6, §3.2); it is a weakness because it *changes the subject*: the regress argument was a demand for *reasons*, and reliabilism answers a different question.

The debate between the **reasons** tradition (foundationalism, coherentism) and the **reliability** tradition is the deepest divide in contemporary epistemology, and it is the subject of §5.

## 5. Internalism, Externalism, Evidentialism, and Pragmatic Encroachment

### 5.1 The internalism/externalism debate

The debate is about **what makes a belief justified**, and the two positions are defined by their answers.

**Internalism.** Justification depends on factors that are **internal** to the subject’s perspective — factors that the subject can access by reflection. The paradigm is the subject’s **evidence**: the beliefs and experiences that the subject has, and that she can cite in support of her belief.

**Externalism.** Justification depends on factors that may be **external** to the subject’s perspective — facts about the reliability of the process, the proper functioning of the faculties, the causal history of the belief.

The debate is not about *whether* internal factors matter; it is about whether they are *sufficient*. The internalist says they are; the externalist says they are not.

The map:

| View | What justifies | Access required? | Paradigm |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Access internalism** | The subject’s evidence, accessible by reflection | Yes | Evidentialism (Conee & Feldman) |
| **Mentalism** | The subject’s mental states | No (but the states are internal) | Conee & Feldman (2001) |
| **Process reliabilism** | The reliability of the process | No | Goldman |
| **Proper functionalism** | The proper functioning of the faculties | No | Plantinga |
| **Virtue epistemology** | The exercise of competence | Partial | Sosa, Zagzebski |

The debate is important because it determines the response to the skeptic (Ch. 6, §6.3), the analysis of knowledge (Ch. 7, §1.4), and the theory of the value of knowledge (Ch. 7, §7).

### 5.2 Evidentialism

**Evidentialism** is the internalist view that a belief is justified if and only if it **fits** the subject’s evidence. The canonical statement is **Conee and Feldman** (1985; 2004): “Doxastic justification is a matter of fitting the evidence.”

**The view.**

1. **The evidence** is the set of the subject’s beliefs and experiences — the things she has to go on.
2. **Fitting** is a normative relation: the evidence supports the belief, or it does not.
3. **The subject’s perspective** is the standard: justification is relative to the evidence the subject has, not to the evidence she *should* have.

**The argument.** Evidentialism captures the intuition that justification is about *reasons*: a belief is justified if the subject has good reasons for it. The view is internalist because the reasons must be *accessible* to the subject; it is normative because the relation between evidence and belief is a relation of *support*, not of causation.

**The objection: the “evidence” is underdetermined.** What counts as evidence? The subject’s beliefs? Her experiences? Her seemings? The choice matters, and the evidentialist has no principled way to make it. (This is the Sellarsian dilemma in a new form.)

**The objection: the “fitting” relation is unexplained.** What is the relation of “fitting”? The evidentialist says it is a *sui generis* normative relation, but the claim is a label, not an explanation. (This is the *normativity* problem of metaethics; cf. Ch. 44, §1.2.)

### 5.3 Pragmatic encroachment

**Pragmatic encroachment** is the view that justification (and knowledge) can depend on **practical stakes** — the costs and benefits of being wrong (Ch. 7, §6.3).

The view challenges the evidentialist assumption that justification is a function of evidence alone. If the stakes can affect whether a belief is justified, then the evidentialist picture is incomplete.

**The argument.** The bank cases (Ch. 7, §6.1) suggest that the same evidence can justify a belief in a low-stakes context and fail to justify it in a high-stakes context. If the cases are genuine, then justification is *stake-sensitive*, and the evidentialist is wrong.

**The objection.** The cases may show that *knowledge* is stake-sensitive, not that *justification* is. The evidentialist can accept the knowledge data while maintaining that justification is a matter of evidence alone. The distinction between justification and knowledge (Ch. 7, §1) is the evidentialist’s escape.

Whether the escape works is contested. The debate is the newest front in the theory of justification, and it is unresolved.

### 5.4 The “new evil demon” and the internalism/externalism debate

The new evil demon (§4.4) is the test case for the debate.

- The **internalist** says the demon-world subject is justified, because her evidence is the same as ours.
- The **externalist** says she is not (or not in the same sense), because her processes are unreliable.

The debate is not about the *intuition* (both sides agree that the subject seems justified); it is about what the intuition *shows*. The internalist takes it as a datum; the externalist takes it as a *phenomenological* fact that does not settle the *normative* question.

The debate is unresolved, and it is the deepest divide in contemporary epistemology.

### 5.5 The “deontological” conception

A third position, which cuts across the debate: **epistemic deontology** — the view that justification is a matter of **responsibility**, of doing one’s epistemic duty.

**The view.** A belief is justified if the subject has *done her best* — if she has gathered the relevant evidence, reasoned carefully, and formed the belief on the basis of the evidence. The justification is a matter of *praise* and *blame*, not of reliability.

**The argument.** The deontological conception captures the intuition that justification is *normative*: it is about what the subject *ought* to believe, not about what is *reliable*.

**The objection: the “ought implies can” problem.** If justification requires doing one’s duty, and if the subject’s duty is to gather evidence she cannot gather, then no belief is justified. The deontological conception needs a *feasible* standard, and the standard is hard to state without circularity.

The deontological conception is the ancestor of **virtue epistemology** (§7), which replaces the language of duty with the language of *competence*.

## 6. Defeasibility and the Logic of Defeat

### 6.1 The defeasibility theory

**Defeasibility theory** is the view that a belief is justified if it is supported by the evidence *and* there are no **defeaters** — no considerations that would undermine the justification.

The theory is a response to the regress problem: the regress is stopped by the *absence of defeaters*, not by a foundation. A belief is justified if it is *undefeated*, and the absence of defeaters is a negative condition, not a positive foundation.

The canonical statement is **Pollock** (1986, 1995), and the theory has been developed by **Klein** (1971, 1981) and **Lehrer** (1990).

### 6.2 The taxonomy of defeaters

**Pollock’s taxonomy** is the standard, and it is the most important contribution of the theory. Pollock (1986, pp. 37–39) distinguishes two kinds of defeater.

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Rebutting defeater** | A reason to believe the negation of the belief | I believe the wall is red; I am told it is white |
| **Undercutting defeater** | A reason to doubt the *connection* between the evidence and the belief | I believe the wall is red (on the basis of the light); I am told the light is red, so the visual evidence is unreliable |

The distinction between rebutting and undercutting is crucial, and it is often overlooked. A rebutting defeater attacks the *truth* of the belief; an undercutting defeater attacks the *justification* of the belief. The two have different logical structures, and they require different responses.

Many writers add a third category — the **overriding defeater** (Plantinga 1993; Swinburne 2001) — for a defeater that defeats the *reason* itself, rather than the truth of the belief or the link between reason and belief. Note that a report that one’s own faculties are malfunctioning (I am told I have a brain lesion that causes red-seemings) is, on Pollock’s definition, an *undercutting* defeater: it attacks the connection between the evidence and the belief, not the truth of the belief.

### 6.3 The logic of defeat

**The formal structure.** The defeasibility theory uses a non-monotonic logic: the justification relation is *defeasible*, and the addition of new information can remove the justification.

The structure:

$$
(\mathrm{Justified}) \quad J(p) \wedge \neg D(p) \to J^*(p)
$$

where $J(p)$ is the prima facie justification, $D(p)$ is the presence of a defeater, and $J^*(p)$ is the all-things-considered justification.

**The defeater-of-defeater problem.** If a defeater is itself defeasible, then a defeater-of-defeater can restore the justification. The structure is *recursive*, and the recursion can be infinite.

The problem: does the recursion terminate? If it does, there is a *foundation* of defeaters (a defeater that cannot be defeated), which is a version of foundationalism. If it does not, the justification is never all-things-considered, which is a version of skepticism.

**The proposed solutions.**

**(i) The “pragmatic” solution.** The recursion terminates in *practice*: the subject has a finite cognitive capacity, and the defeaters that are actually considered are the ones that matter. The solution is pragmatic, and it is a version of the contextualist strategy.

**(ii) The “coherentist” solution.** The recursion terminates in the *coherence* of the system: the justification is holistic, and the defeaters are integrated into the system. The solution is coherentist, and it faces the isolation objection.

**(iii) The “default” solution.** The recursion terminates in a *default*: a belief is justified unless defeated, and the absence of a defeater is the default. The solution is a version of **phenomenal conservatism** (Huemer 2001), and it is the most influential contemporary version of the theory.

### 6.4 Defeasibility and the analysis of knowledge

The defeasibility theory is important for the analysis of knowledge (Ch. 7, §3.2), because it is one of the fourth conditions proposed to solve the Gettier problem. The **no-defeater** condition: $S$ knows that $p$ only if there is no true proposition $d$ such that, if $S$ were to believe $d$, $S$ would no longer be justified in believing $p$.

The condition is meant to exclude the Gettier cases (the defeater is the fact that the evidence is misleading), and it is meant to be *internal* to the subject’s epistemic situation.

The objection: the condition is too strong, because there is almost always *some* true proposition that would undermine the justification (the skeptical hypothesis, the possibility of error). The restriction of the condition to *relevant* defeaters is the standard reply, and the restriction is the same as the generality problem.

### 6.5 The “epistemic” conception of defeat

A recent development: the defeater is not a *proposition*, but a *fact about the subject’s epistemic situation* — a fact about what she *ought* to believe, given her evidence.

The **epistemic** conception (Bergmann 2006; Pryor 2000): the defeater is a *reason* that the subject has, not a proposition that is true. The reason can be a belief, an experience, or a seeming. The justification is defeated if the subject’s *total evidence* no longer supports the belief.

The conception is a version of evidentialism (§5.2), and it is the most natural way to integrate the defeasibility theory with the internalist tradition.

## 7. Virtue, Responsibility, and Epistemic Agency

### 7.1 The virtue turn

**Virtue epistemology** is the view that justification is a matter of the **exercise of intellectual virtue** — the competences, skills, and character traits that make a person good at inquiry.

The view is a synthesis of the traditions: it is **externalist** (the virtue is a reliable disposition), **internalist** (the virtue is an excellence of the agent), and **responsibilist** (the virtue is a matter of the agent’s agency).

The canonical statements are **Sosa** (1991, 2007, 2015), **Zagzebski** (1996), **Greco** (2010), and **Pritchard** (2012).

### 7.2 The two branches

The virtue tradition has two branches, which are often confused.

| Branch | What the virtue is | Paradigm | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reliabilist virtue epistemology** | A reliable cognitive faculty | Perception, memory, reason | The *faculty* |
| **Responsibilist virtue epistemology** | A character trait | Open-mindedness, intellectual courage, honesty | The *agent* |

The reliabilist branch is the descendant of Goldman’s reliabilism (§4): the virtue is the reliable process, and the justification is the output of the process. The responsibilist branch is the descendant of the **ethics of belief** (Ch. 4, §4.5): the virtue is a character trait, and the justification is the *credit* the agent deserves for the belief.

The two branches are not exclusive, but they have different implications. The reliabilist branch is *naturalistic* and *externalist*; the responsibilist branch is *normative* and *internalist*.

### 7.3 Sosa’s AAA structure

Sosa’s **AAA** structure (Ch. 7, §5.2) is the most influential version of the reliabilist branch.

| Level | Name | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Accuracy | The belief is true |
| **AA** | Adroitness | The belief is produced by competence |
| **AAA** | Aptness | The belief is true *because* it is produced by competence |

Justification, on Sosa’s view, is **adroit** belief: the belief is produced by competence. Knowledge is **apt** belief: the belief is true because it is produced by competence. The distinction between justification and knowledge is the distinction between AA and AAA.

The structure solves the Gettier problem (Ch. 7, §2) and the new evil demon (§4.4): the demon-world subject is adroit (she exercises the same competences) but not apt (the competences are not successful). The structure is the most elegant synthesis in the literature.

### 7.4 Zagzebski’s responsibilism

Zagzebski’s **responsibilism** (1996) is the most influential version of the responsibilist branch.

**The view.** Justification is a matter of the agent’s **credit**: the agent is justified if she deserves credit for the truth of her belief. The credit is earned by the exercise of intellectual virtue — the character traits that make a person good at inquiry.

**The argument.** The view captures the intuition that justification is *normative*: it is about what the agent *ought* to believe, and about what she *deserves* praise or blame for. The reliability of the process is not enough; the process must be *hers*, in the sense that it is an expression of her agency.

**The objection: the “credit” condition is too strong.** In cases of **testimonial knowledge** (Ch. 11, §1), the credit for the truth belongs to the testifier, not the believer. If credit is required, testimonial knowledge is not knowledge. (The reply: credit can be *distributed*; the believer is creditable for the *acceptance*, even if the testifier is creditable for the content.)

**The objection: the “character” condition is too demanding.** The responsibilist requires the agent to have *character traits*, which is a high standard. Most justified beliefs are formed automatically, without the exercise of character. (The reply: the traits can be *dispositional*, and the automatic beliefs can be expressions of the traits.)

### 7.5 The responsibility condition

The most important contribution of the virtue tradition is the **responsibility condition**: the agent is justified only if her belief is *hers* — if it is an expression of her agency, not merely a causal output of her cognitive machinery.

The condition is meant to capture the intuition that justification is **normative**: it is about what the agent *ought* to believe, and about what she *deserves* praise or blame for.

**The argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Justification is a normative property.} \\
2. & \text{A normative property must be grounded in the agent’s agency.} \\
3. & \text{The agent’s agency is exercised in the formation of the belief.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Justification requires the exercise of agency.}
\end{array}
$$

**The objection.** The argument is not valid: a normative property can be grounded in something other than agency (e.g., in the reliability of the process, or in the fittingness of the belief to the evidence). The responsibility condition is a *substantive* thesis, not a conceptual truth.

The condition is the most contested part of the virtue tradition, and it is the subject of the ongoing debate between the reliabilist and responsibilist branches.

### 7.6 The “epistemic agency” debate

The responsibility condition connects to a broader debate about **epistemic agency**: do we *choose* our beliefs, and are we *responsible* for them?

The debate is the subject of Ch. 4, §4.5 (the ethics of belief) and Ch. 28, §5 (practical and theoretical reason). The key question: is the *will* involved in belief-formation, or is belief-formation a *passive* process?

The **voluntarist** says belief is under our control, and we are responsible for it. The **involuntarist** says belief is not under our control, and the responsibility is *indirect* — we are responsible for the *processes* that shape our beliefs (what we read, whom we trust, how we inquire).

The virtue tradition presupposes the involuntarist picture: the virtues are *dispositions* that shape belief-formation, and the agent is responsible for cultivating them. The responsibility is *indirect*, and it is exercised over time, not in the moment of belief.

## 8. The State of the Debate

### 8.1 What is settled

- **The regress is real.** The Agrippan trilemma is a genuine problem, and no response has been universally accepted.
- **Foundationalism, coherentism, and reliabilism all face serious objections.** The classical versions are untenable; the hybrid versions are promising but incomplete.
- **The Sellarsian dilemma is the deepest problem.** The relation between experience and justification is unresolved, and it is the reason the debate has not converged.
- **The generality problem is unsolved.** Every reliabilist theory faces it, and no solution is universally accepted.
- **The internalism/externalism debate is unresolved.** The new evil demon is the test case, and neither side has a decisive argument.

### 8.2 What is open

- **The structure of justification.** Is it foundational, coherentist, reliabilist, or virtue-theoretic? Or is it a *plurality* of structures, with different structures in different domains?
- **The relation between justification and knowledge.** Is justification necessary for knowledge? Is it sufficient (with truth and the anti-luck condition)? The debate is unresolved (Ch. 7).
- **The normativity of justification.** Is justification a *natural* property (reliability) or a *normative* one (fittingness, credit)? The debate is the deepest divide in the field.
- **The role of agency.** Is justification a matter of the agent’s *responsibility*, or of the *reliability* of her processes? The virtue tradition is the most developed attempt to answer, and it is contested.
- **The relation between justification and truth.** Is justification *truth-conducive*? The swamping argument (Ch. 7, §7.2) suggests that the relation is more complex than the reliabilist assumes.

### 8.3 The methodological lesson

The chapter is a case study in the method of Part I.

- **Conceptual analysis** (§1 of Ch. 3) generated the classical theories and the objections to them.
- **Thought experiments** (§2 of Ch. 3) — the new evil demon, the clairvoyance cases, the bank cases — are the engine of the debate.
- **Formalization** (§3 of Ch. 3) — the non-monotonic logic of defeat — is essential for the defeasibility theory.
- **Reflective equilibrium** (§4 of Ch. 3) is the method by which the theories and the intuitions are adjusted.
- **The criteria of a good theory** (Ch. 4, §3) — simplicity, fertility, explanatory power — are the criteria by which the theories are assessed.
- **Negative capability** (§7 of Ch. 3) is the response of the skeptic: the problem may be a pseudo-problem, generated by a false assumption about the nature of justification.

The honest verdict: the theory of justification is a **field in transition**. The classical theories are untenable; the contemporary theories are promising but incomplete; the debate is unresolved. The unresolved character is *instructive*: it shows what philosophical progress looks like — not a settled answer, but a sharper understanding of what the question is, and of what would count as an answer.

### 8.4 The dialectical map

The chapter’s conclusion can be summarized in a table.

| Theory | Core idea | Solves the regress? | Main problem | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Foundationalism** | Basic beliefs | Yes (by fiat) | The Sellarsian dilemma | Live, in modest forms |
| **Coherentism** | Holistic mutual support | Yes (by circle) | The isolation objection | Live, in hybrid forms |
| **Infinitism** | Infinite chain | Yes (by availability) | The finite capacity | Minority |
| **Reliabilism** | Reliable process | Yes (by externalism) | The generality problem | Dominant, in virtue form |
| **Evidentialism** | Fitting the evidence | No (needs a structure) | The “fitting” relation | Live, in internalist form |
| **Defeasibility** | Absence of defeaters | Yes (by default) | The recursion | Live, in default form |
| **Virtue epistemology** | Exercise of competence | Yes (by agency) | The credit condition | Dominant, in reliabilist form |

The table is a map, not a verdict. The reader who wants to choose a theory must decide which cost is acceptable — and the decision is a philosophical argument.

## Optional threads

Cross-cutting material, for the reader who wants to go deeper.

**1. The Bayesian structure of justification.** The Bayesian framework (Ch. 10, §4) offers a *formal* account of justification: a belief is justified if it is the result of conditionalizing on the evidence. The account is *internalist* (the evidence is the subject’s) and *holistic* (the justification depends on the prior and the likelihood). The debate about the priors (Ch. 10, §5) is the Bayesian version of the regress.

**2. The “epistemic” conception of defeat and the “reasons” tradition.** The debate between the “reasons” tradition (internalism) and the “reliability” tradition (externalism) is the deepest divide in the field. The “epistemic” conception of defeat (Bergmann, Pryor) is the most promising attempt to bridge it.

**3. Epistemic injustice and the structure of justification.** Fricker (2007) argues that the theory of justification has neglected the *social* conditions of justification: whose evidence is *heard*, whose testimony is *believed*, whose competence is *recognized*. The virtue tradition has begun to incorporate this (Ch. 11, §4), but the theory of justification has not yet fully absorbed it.

**4. Non-Western epistemologies of justification.** The Indian *pramāṇa* tradition developed a sophisticated theory of justification that anticipates many of the contemporary debates: the *svataḥ prāmāṇya* (self-validity) debate about the default status of cognition, the *parataḥ prāmāṇya* (external validity) debate about the need for external confirmation, and the *khyāti* (error) theories. The debate between the *svataḥ* and *parataḥ* views is a version of the foundationalism/coherentism debate, and it is worth studying as a systematic alternative (see Matilal 1986; Ganeri 2001).

**5. The epistemology of the “extended mind.”** If cognition extends beyond the skull (Clark & Chalmers 1998; Ch. 30, §4), then the structure of justification may extend too: the notebook, the search engine, and the institutional archive are part of the subject’s epistemic system. The implication for the internalism/externalism debate is profound, and it is only beginning to be explored.

## Summary

- **The regress problem** is generated by the principle of sufficient reason for belief. The **Agrippan trilemma** — infinitism, foundationalism, coherentism — states the options. Each has a characteristic cost, and none has been universally accepted.
- **Foundationalism** posits basic beliefs that are justified without inference. The classical version (Descartes, sense-data) is untenable; the modest versions (Chisholm, Audi, Pryor, Huemer) are live but incomplete. The **Sellarsian dilemma** — the given is either conceptual (and needs justification) or non-conceptual (and cannot justify) — is the deepest problem.
- **Coherentism** holds that justification is holistic, a matter of the mutual support of beliefs. The **isolation objection** — a coherent system can be entirely false — is the central problem. The hybrid positions (foundationalist coherentism, reliabilist coherentism, virtue coherentism) are the most promising developments.
- **Reliabilism** holds that justification is a matter of the reliability of the process. The **generality problem** — which process is the relevant one? — is unsolved. The **new evil demon** — a subject with unreliable processes but the same internal states — is the central objection. The virtue-theoretic synthesis (§7) is the most influential response.
- **The internalism/externalism debate** is the deepest divide in the field. **Evidentialism** (internalist) and **reliabilism** (externalist) are the paradigms. **Pragmatic encroachment** challenges the evidentialist assumption that justification is a function of evidence alone.
- **Defeasibility theory** holds that a belief is justified if it is supported by the evidence and there are no defeaters. **Pollock’s taxonomy** — rebutting and undercutting — is the standard (later writers add the overriding defeater). The **recursion problem** — defeaters of defeaters — is the central difficulty, and the **default** solution (phenomenal conservatism) is the most influential response.
- **Virtue epistemology** is the most recent synthesis: justification is the exercise of intellectual virtue. The **reliabilist** and **responsibilist** branches differ on whether the virtue is a faculty or a character trait. **Sosa’s AAA structure** and **Zagzebski’s credit theory** are the most developed versions. The **responsibility condition** — the belief must be an expression of the agent’s agency — is the most contested part of the tradition.
- **The state of the debate**: the classical theories are untenable; the contemporary theories are promising but incomplete; the debate is unresolved. The unresolved character is *instructive*: it shows what philosophical progress looks like — not a settled answer, but a sharper understanding of what the question is, and of what would count as an answer.

## Study Questions

1. State the Agrippan trilemma. Is it exhaustive? If not, what is the fourth option?
2. Is the principle of sufficient reason for belief true? What would a *denial* of the principle look like, and is it defensible?
3. State the Sellarsian dilemma. Is there a way between the horns? If so, what is it? If not, what follows for foundationalism?
4. Is the isolation objection decisive against coherentism? What is the best coherentist response, and does it succeed?
5. State the generality problem. Is it a problem for reliabilism, or for any theory of justification? What is the best solution, and why is it not decisive?
6. Is the new evil demon a genuine counterexample to reliabilism? What does the answer depend on?
7. Is justification internal or external? State the strongest argument for each, and give your view with a calibrated confidence.
8. What is the difference between a rebutting and an undercutting defeater? Give an example of each, and explain why the distinction matters.
9. Is the recursion problem for defeasibility theory fatal? What is the best solution, and what does it presuppose?
10. Is the responsibility condition necessary for justification? If so, what follows for testimonial knowledge, and for the epistemology of artificial intelligence?
11. “The theory of justification is a field in transition.” Assess. What would count as progress in the field?
12. Construct a case in which the foundationalist, the coherentist, the reliabilist, and the virtue epistemologist give different verdicts. What does the case show about the state of the debate?

> *A closing note.* This chapter has followed the regress through its major stopping points — foundation, circle, process, default, virtue — and has arrived at a conclusion that is less a theory than a diagnosis: the theory of justification is the theory of *how beliefs answer to the world*, and the answer is not a single mechanism but a *structure* of relations among beliefs, experiences, processes, and agents. The structure is not a foundation, and it is not a circle; it is a *web* — but the web is not closed, and the way it is open to the world is the deepest question in the field.

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter turns to intuition and the a priori — the sources of justification that the theory of justification has presupposed. If justification is a matter of reasons, what are the reasons *made of*? The answer, for most of the tradition, is *intuition*: the intellectual seeming that a proposition is true. The next chapter examines intuition, the analytic, and the a priori, and asks whether the traditional answer can survive the challenges of the twentieth century. The connection is direct: every theory of justification is a theory of the *sources* of justification, and the sources are the subject of Chapter 9.
