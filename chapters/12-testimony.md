# Chapter 11 — Testimony, Disagreement, and the Social Life of Knowledge

> “The wise man proportions his belief to the evidence.”
> — Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, X.i

> “The wise and beneficent Author of nature … hath implanted in us a disposition to confide in the veracity of others, and to believe what they tell us.”
> — Thomas Reid, *An Inquiry into the Human Mind* (1764), VI.xxiv

> “Testimony is like an arrow shot from a long bow; the force of it depends on the strength of the hand that draws it. Argument is like an arrow from a cross-bow, which has equal force though shot by a child.”
> — Samuel Johnson, in Boswell’s *Life of Johnson* (1823), Vol. IV (a conversation of 30 May 1764); Johnson attributes the image to Bacon

---

Chapters 5–10 treated knowledge as though it were the achievement of a solitary mind. But we came into a world already full of speakers, and most of what we know we know because someone told us. The geography of continents, the dates of kings, the structure of the atom, the fact that the earth goes round the sun, the names of our grandparents — none of these are delivered by perception or by inference. They are delivered by **testimony**, and the epistemology of the solitary inquirer is, in consequence, a fiction.

This chapter is about the social life of knowledge. It has three layers. The first (§§1–2) is the **epistemology of testimony**: is it a basic source of knowledge, or is it derivative from perception and inference? The second (§§3–4) is the **epistemology of social epistemic relations**: disagreement, and the injustice that the distribution of credibility produces. The third (§§5–7) is the **epistemology of social epistemic systems**: groups, institutions, the internet, the archive.

The connecting thread is a single question, which is the social form of the question of Chapter 10: **when does the fact that someone said something give us reason to believe it?** The answer, we shall see, is not a single principle but a structure — and the structure is under strain in the conditions of the twenty-first century.

## 1. Testimony as a Source of Knowledge

### 1.1 What testimony is

**Testimony**, in the epistemological sense, is an **assertion offered as evidence** — a speech act whose function is to convey information from a speaker to a hearer. It includes:

- Ordinary statements of fact (“it is raining”).
- Expert reports (“the committee found no evidence of fraud”).
- Written records, books, and inscriptions.
- The content of a painting, a photograph, or a diagram (on some views).
- What is *not* testimony: silence, non-assertoric utterances, and commands. (The last is arguable: an order can carry information, but it does so indirectly.)

The scope matters. If everything that conveys information is testimony, then almost all of our knowledge is testimonial, and the question of the *justification* of testimony becomes the question of the justification of most of what we believe. If testimony is narrowed to *assertoric* speech, the question is narrower but still central.

**The standard definition** (Coady 1992, p. 38): testimony is the *assertion of a proposition* by a speaker who *intends* it to be believed, and who *gives the hearer to understand* that the proposition is true.

**A complication.** Writing and images are not *assertions* in the narrow speech-act sense. But they are *testimonial* in the broad sense: they purport to convey knowledge, and they are believed on the authority of their source. The epistemology of testimony must handle them, and the handling is not trivial.

### 1.2 The question

The epistemological question is not “is testimony sometimes reliable?” — it obviously is. The question is **whether testimony is a *basic* source of knowledge**, or whether it is *derivative* from other sources.

The distinction is the same one that structured Chapter 8. A source is **basic** if it can justify a belief *on its own*, without the justification depending on an inference from other sources. A source is **derivative** if its justificatory force depends on an inference — typically, an inference from the reliability of the source.

The positions:

| Position | Claim | Paradigm |
|---|---|---|
| **Reductionism** | Testimony is not basic; it is justified by an inference from perception, memory, and induction | Hume |
| **Anti-reductionism** | Testimony is basic; it has a default entitlement independent of inference | Reid, Coady, Burge |
| **Hybrid** | Testimony is basic, but the *entitlement* to accept it is *defeasible* and *monitored* (Lackey: some testimonial justification is basic, some is derived) | Fricker; Lackey (in different ways) |

### 1.3 Hume’s reductionism

Hume’s treatment is in the *Enquiry* X, and it is embedded in his argument against miracles — but its logic is general.

> “No testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish.” (*Enquiry* X.i)

**The structure of the argument.**

1. Testimony is an *empirical* phenomenon: it is a form of behavior, and its reliability is learned from experience.
2. The evidence for the reliability of testimony is *inductive*: we have observed that testifiers are usually truthful, and we project that regularity to the present case.
3. In any particular case, we must weigh the *probability* that the testimony is true against the *probability* of the event reported.
4. The weight of testimony is therefore *derivative*: it is a function of the observed reliability of testifiers, which is a matter of empirical generalization.

**The reductionist thesis.** Testimony is not a basic source. It is a *derivative* source whose justification is an inference from the reliability of testifiers, and the inference is *empirical* and *inductive*.

**The virtue.** The view is *naturalistic* and *demanding*: it tells us not to accept testimony *blindly*, but to evaluate the evidence for the reliability of the testifier. It is the epistemology of the *critical reader*, the *skeptical juror*, the *conscientious inquirer*.

**The vice.** It is *psychologically* and *epistemically* impossible. The evidence for the reliability of testifiers is *itself* largely testimonial. We learn that testifiers are reliable *from what we have been told*, and the *telling* is testimony. The reductionist’s inference is *circular*: it presupposes the reliability of the very source whose reliability it seeks to establish.

### 1.4 Coady’s circularity argument

Coady (*Testimony: A Philosophical Study*, 1992), Ch. 4, gives the most influential formulation of the circularity objection.

**The argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Reductionism requires that the justification of testimony be an inference from non-testimonial evidence.} \\
2. & \text{The non-testimonial evidence available to a finite subject is local: it concerns the subject's own observation and memory.} \\
3. & \text{A local evidence base is radically insufficient to ground the reliability of the whole system of testimony.} \\
4. & \text{The evidence we actually have for the reliability of testifiers is largely testimonial.} \\
\hline
5. & \therefore \text{The reductionist's inference is either insufficient (local) or circular (testimonial).}
\end{array}
$$

**The “token/type” distinction.** Coady distinguishes two kinds of reductionist claim (the distinction now standardly called *local* and *global* reductionism):

- **Token reductionism** (*local*): the *particular* testimony is justified by *particular* evidence about *this* testifier (his track record, his credentials, his incentives).
- **Type reductionism** (*global*): the *institution* of testimony is justified by *general* evidence about the reliability of testifiers *as a class*.

Coady’s claim: *token* reductionism is *possible* but *rarely* applied (we do not check the track record of our friends before believing them); *type* reductionism is *impossible* because the general evidence is itself testimonial.

**The reductionist’s objection.** Reid and the anti-reductionists are accused of an *over*-generalization: the fact that *some* testimony must be accepted in order to acquire the evidence for the reliability of *other* testimony does not entail that testimony is *basic*. (See §1.5.)

### 1.5 Reid and anti-reductionism

Reid (*Inquiry*, 1764, VI.xxiv) offers the classic anti-reductionist position.

**The argument.** The human mind is endowed by nature with two *principles*:

1. **The principle of veracity**: a disposition to speak the truth, and to communicate accurately.
2. **The principle of credulity**: a disposition to believe what one is told, in the absence of contrary evidence.

**The structure.** Testimony is a *first principle* of human knowledge — a source that is *constitutive* of our epistemic lives, not *derived* from other sources. It is *no more* in need of justification than perception or memory.

**The argument from design.** Reid’s full argument is *theological*: the Author of nature has implanted the principles, and they are *reliable* because the Author is *beneficent*. The argument is *not* available to the secular naturalist, and the anti-reductionist needs a *secular* version.

**The secular version** (Burge 1993; Coady 1992). The argument is *transcendental*: testimony is a *condition of the possibility* of the acquisition of language, and therefore of *thought*. A child learns language by *trusting* the testimony of her caregivers, and the trust is *not* justified by an inference (she has no evidence base to infer from). If the trust were not *default justified*, no one would ever acquire the concepts needed for the inference. Therefore, testimony must be basic.

**Burge’s “acceptance principle”** (1993, 1997). Burge proposes a principle that is *a priori* and *default*:

> **(AP)** A person is *a priori* entitled to accept as true something that is presented as true and that is intelligible to him, unless there are stronger reasons not to do so.

The principle is *a priori* in the sense that it does not depend on *empirical* evidence about the reliability of testifiers; it is *default* in the sense that it operates *unless defeated*. It is, in effect, the anti-reductionist’s central claim in a single formula.

**The objection.** The acceptance principle is *too permissive*: it licenses the acceptance of *any* intelligible testimony, including testimony from sources we have reason to distrust. (Burge’s reply: the principle is *defeasible*; the reasons to distrust are *defeaters*, and their presence is what the principle is qualified by.)

### 1.6 The Fricker alternative

Elizabeth Fricker (“Telling and Trusting,” 1995; “Against Gullibility,” 1994) offers a *critique of anti-reductionism* from within the anti-reductionist camp.

**The argument.** The anti-reductionist’s claim — that testimony is basic — is *not* the claim that the hearer should *believe everything she is told*. It is the claim that the hearer has a *default entitlement* to believe, *subject to* an *a posteriori* check on the testifier’s credibility.

**The “critical” element.** Fricker argues that the hearer has an *obligation* to *monitor* the testifier for signs of insincerity or incompetence. The monitoring is *automatic* and *non-inferential*: it is a *perceptual* sensitivity to the *testimonial* features of the speech act (the speaker’s *manner*, *confidence*, *consistency*). The monitoring is *not* a general inference from the reliability of testimony; it is a *local*, *situated* judgment.

**The “testimonial sensibility”** (Fricker 2007, Ch. 3). The hearer’s *sensibility* is the *capacity* to perceive the *credibility* of the speaker — and the sensibility can be *virtuous* (accurate) or *vicious* (biased). The virtuous hearer is *sensitive* to the *evidence* of credibility, and *resistant* to the *prejudices* that distort it.

**The position.** Fricker’s view is *neither* the reductionist’s (testimony is derivative) *nor* the anti-reductionist’s (testimony is basic). It is a *hybrid*: testimony is a *basic* source *with* a *critical* monitoring, and the monitoring is *itself* a form of *perception* (of the speaker’s credibility).

### 1.7 Transmission versus generation

A further distinction, due to Welbourne (1979, 1986) and developed by Lackey (1999, 2008).

**The transmission thesis.** Testimony *transmits* knowledge: the hearer can acquire knowledge *that* $p$ from a speaker only if the speaker *knows that* $p$. The hearer’s knowledge is *derived* from the speaker’s; the *epistemic warrant* is *transmitted*.

**The generation thesis.** Testimony can *generate* knowledge: the hearer can acquire knowledge *that* $p$ from a speaker even if the speaker does *not* know that $p$ — provided that the hearer has *adequate* grounds for accepting the testimony.

**Lackey’s counterexample.** A *creationist teacher* teaches evolution from a textbook. She does not *believe* the theory (she thinks it is false), but she *understands* it, and she *presents* it accurately. Her students *learn* evolution from her. The students have *knowledge* that $p$ (from the textbook), but the *teacher* does not *know* that $p$. The knowledge is *generated* by the testimony, not *transmitted*.

**The structure.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
(\mathrm{Transmission}) & K_S p \wedge \text{Testifies}_S(p) \wedge \text{Accepts}_H(p) \to K_H p \\
(\mathrm{Generation}) & \neg K_S p \wedge \text{Testifies}_S(p) \wedge \text{Accepts}_H(p) \wedge \text{Adequate}_H \to K_H p
\end{array}
$$

**The objections.**

**(i) The “knowledge” of the teacher.** The teacher *does* know the *content* of the textbook; the case is not one of *no* knowledge, but of *knowledge without belief* (Ch. 7, §1.2). If the transmission thesis is understood as requiring *belief* in the content, the case is a counterexample; if it is understood as requiring *knowledge of the content* (which the teacher has), it is not.

**(ii) The “epistemic warrant” of the hearer.** The students’ warrant is *not* the teacher’s warrant (she does not have any); it is the *textbook’s* warrant, which the teacher is *transmitting* without *endorsing*. The case shows that transmission is *not* a *simple* relation: the warrant can be *embedded* in a chain of sources, and the *speaker* need not *possess* it.

**(iii) The “testimony” requirement.** If the teacher is *not* testifying (she is *reading* the textbook, not *asserting* its content), the case is not a counterexample. The distinction between *reporting* and *asserting* is the crux.

### 1.8 The state of the testimony debate

| Position | Core claim | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reductionism** | Testimony is derivative | Naturalistic; critical | Circular; impossible |
| **Anti-reductionism** | Testimony is basic | Fits the child’s acquisition of language | Too permissive; ignores monitoring |
| **Hybrid** | Basic + critical monitoring | Fits the phenomenology; avoids both extremes | The “monitoring” is underdeveloped |
| **Transmission** | The speaker’s knowledge is necessary | Captures the “chain” of testimony | Counterexamples (Lackey) |
| **Generation** | The speaker’s knowledge is not necessary | Fits the textbook cases | Blurs the distinction between testimony and evidence |

**The verdict.** The anti-reductionist consensus is the *dominant* position in the contemporary literature, and it is *right* that testimony is a *basic* source. But the reductionist’s *concern* — that testimony can be *unreliable*, and that the hearer must be *vigilant* — is *not* answered by the default entitlement. The **hybrid** position is the most defensible: testimony is basic, *with* a critical monitoring, and the monitoring is a *perceptual* sensitivity to credibility.

## 2. Trust, Credibility, and the Ethics of Testimony

### 2.1 Trust versus reliance

A distinction from the ethics of trust (Baier 1986; Jones 1996; Hawley 2014).

| Concept | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Reliance** | A prediction about behavior | I rely on the bus to arrive at 8:00 |
| **Trust** | A *normative* expectation, with a *disposition* to be betrayed | I trust my friend to keep a secret |

**The structure of trust.** Trust involves (i) a *belief* that the trusted party will act in a certain way; (ii) a *normative* expectation that she *ought* to; (iii) a *vulnerability* to betrayal if she does not; and (iv) a *reactive attitude* (resentment, hurt) if she does not.

**The application to testimony.** When we believe what someone tells us, we are not merely *relying* on her behavior; we are *trusting* her *veracity*. The trust is *normative*: the speaker *ought* to tell the truth, and if she lies, the hearer is *betrayed*.

**The structure of the hearer’s position.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{H trusts } S \text{ to be veracious.} \\
2. & H \text{ believes } S \text{'s testimony.} \\
3. & H \text{'s belief is warranted if } S \text{ is trustworthy.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Trust is a condition of testimonial knowledge.}
\end{array}
$$

**The objection.** The trust is *not* sufficient: the speaker can be *sincere* and *mistaken*. The trust must be *in the speaker’s competence as well as her sincerity* — and the competence is *not* guaranteed by sincerity.

### 2.2 The ethics of testimony

**The norms of assertion.** The knowledge norm (Williamson 2000; Ch. 7, §4.2): one should assert $p$ only if one *knows* that $p$. The norm is *contested*, but it is the *default* in the literature, and it has a *direct* implication for the ethics of testimony: lying is a *violation* of the norm, and the violation is *not merely* a *moral* failing; it is an *epistemic* one.

**The taxonomy of epistemic vices in testimony.**

| Vice | Definition | Example | Frankfurt’s category (loosely) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lying** | Asserting what one believes to be false | “I did not have sexual relations with that woman” | The *intentional* violation |
| **Bullshit** | Asserting without *concern* for truth | “The vaccine causes autism” (said by a politician who does not care) | The *indifference* to truth |
| **Confabulation** | Asserting a *sincerely* held belief that is *unfounded* | The *false* memory of a witness | The *epistemic* failure |
| **Half-truth** | Asserting *technically true* content that *misleads* | “No *proven* link to cancer” | The *manipulation* of the *implicature* |
| **Mansplaining** | Asserting *unwarranted* authority | A layman explaining a physicist’s field to her | The *credibility* *injustice* |

**Frankfurt’s bullshit** (*On Bullshit*, 1986/2005). The bullshitter’s *defining* feature is *not* that she lies; it is that she is *indifferent* to the *truth* of what she says. She *aims* at *some other* effect — *persuasion*, *impression*, *manipulation* — and the *truth* is *irrelevant* to her. Frankfurt’s *thesis*: bullshit is *more dangerous* than lying, because the liar *acknowledges* the *authority* of truth (she *must* know the truth in order to *deny* it), whereas the bullshitter *undermines* the *authority* *itself*.

**The application to the modern environment.** The *bullshit* is *not* a *marginal* phenomenon; it is the *dominant* mode of *public* *discourse* (see §6.2). The *epistemic* damage is *structural*: the *norm* of *truthfulness* is *eroded*, and the *erosion* is *cumulative*.

### 2.3 The trustworthiness of the testifier

The ethics of testimony has a *structural* dimension: the *conditions* under which testimony is *trustworthy*.

**The conditions.**

1. **Sincerity**: the speaker believes what she asserts.
2. **Competence**: the speaker is in a position to know.
3. **Relevance**: the speaker’s testimony bears on the question.
4. **Independence**: the speaker’s testimony is not *merely* a *repetition* of another source.

The conditions are *ideal*. Actual testimony can fail any of them, and the *hearer*’s task is to *assess* the *likelihood* of failure.

**The “epistemic” *assessment*.** The hearer must *estimate* the *probability* that the *testimony* is *true*, given the *evidence* of the *speaker*’s *sincerity*, *competence*, and *independence*. Let $p$ be the *hypothesis* the speaker *asserts*. The *estimate* is *Bayesian* (Ch. 10, §4):

$$
P(p \mid \text{Testifies}(p)) = \frac{P(\text{Testifies}(p) \mid p) P(p)}{P(\text{Testifies}(p))}.
$$

The *likelihood* $P(\text{Testifies}(p) \mid p)$ is the *probability* that the *speaker* would *assert* $p$ if $p$ were *true*. The *posterior* is the *hearer’s* *credence*.

**The “credibility” *parameter*.** The *likelihood* is *not* a *fixed* *number*; it is a *function* of the *speaker*’s *credibility*. The *credibility* is the *hearer’s* *estimate* of the *probability* that the *speaker* is *sincere* and *competent*.

### 2.4 Moral testimony

A *special* case, with *philosophical* *significance*: can we *trust* a *moral* *expert*?

**The question.** If a *reliable* *moral* *authority* tells me that *eating* *meat* is *wrong*, can I *believe* her *on* her *authority*? Or must I *understand* the *reasons* *myself*?

**The positions** (Jones 1999; Hills 2009; McGrath 2009; Ch. 46, §8).

| Position | Claim | Proponents |
|---|---|---|
| **Moral testimony is possible** | We can acquire moral knowledge from testimony, as we do in other domains | Jones 1999; Sliwa 2012 |
| **Moral testimony is possible but limited** | We can acquire moral knowledge, but *not* moral *understanding* | Hills 2009 |
| **Moral testimony is impossible** | Moral knowledge requires *first-personal* *engagement*; testimony *cannot* deliver it | Wolff 1970; the “autonomy” tradition |

**The “autonomy” argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Moral knowledge requires moral understanding — the grasp of why the action is wrong.} \\
2. & \text{Understanding is not transmissible by testimony; it must be achieved by the agent.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{Moral knowledge cannot be acquired solely from testimony.}
\end{array}
$$

**The objection.** The *distinction* between *knowledge* and *understanding* is *contested* (Ch. 38, §6). If *testimonial* *knowledge* is *genuine* knowledge, then moral *testimony* can *deliver* it, and the *autonomy* argument *proves too much*.

The *debate* is *unresolved*, and it is *connected* to the *ethics* of *deference* (Ch. 46, §8).

### 2.5 The “duty to doubt”

A *neglected* *aspect* of the *ethics* of testimony: the *hearer*’s *duty* to *doubt*.

**The argument.** The *default* entitlement (Burge) is *not* a *license* for *credulity*. The *hearer* has a *duty* to *monitor* the *testifier* for *signs* of *unreliability*, and the *duty* is *not* *discharged* by *passive* *acceptance*.

**The structure.**

| Context | The *appropriate* *attitude* | The *duty* |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary conversation | Default *trust* | *Monitor* for *inconsistency* |
| Expert testimony | Deference to *expertise* | *Assess* the *expert*’s *credentials* and *incentives* |
| Anonymous / online | *Skepticism* | *Verify* the *source* |
| The *bullshitter* | *Active* *resistance* | *Refuse* to *engage* |

**The *epistemic* *virtue*.** The *virtue* is *not* *gullibility* (the *excess* of *trust*) and *not* *paranoia* (the *deficiency*). It is **epistemic *temperance***: the *disposition* to *trust* *appropriately*, *calibrated* to the *evidence* of *reliability*.

### 2.6 The “testimonial” *economy*

The *social* *structure* of *testimony* is an **economy**: a *distribution* of *credibility*, *attention*, and *authority*.

**The *elements*.**

- **Credibility**: the *hearer*’s *estimate* of the *speaker*’s *reliability*.
- **Attention**: the *hearer*’s *willingness* to *engage* with the *testimony*.
- **Authority**: the *speaker*’s *institutional* or *social* *standing*.
- **Access**: the *speaker*’s *ability* to *reach* an *audience*.

**The *inequality*.** The *distribution* is *not* *equal*, and the *inequality* is *not* *random*: it *tracks* *social* *identity* (race, gender, class, disability, accent). The *consequence* is *epistemic* *injustice* (§4).

## 3. The Epistemology of Disagreement

### 3.1 The question

The question is one of the *oldest* in philosophy, but it was *recast* by the *contemporary* *literature* (2005–present): **what should you do when an epistemic peer disagrees with you?**

**The scenario.** $S_1$ and $S_2$ are *epistemic peers*: they are *equally* *intelligent*, *equally* *informed*, *equally* *careful*, and they have *considered* the *same* *evidence*. They *disagree* about $p$. $S_1$ believes $p$; $S_2$ believes $\neg p$.

**The question.** Should $S_1$ *reduce* her *confidence* in $p$? Should she *retain* it? Is the *disagreement* itself *evidence*?

### 3.2 The three views

| View | Claim | Proponents |
|---|---|---|
| **Equal weight** | The disagreement *requires* *conciliation*: each should *reduce* confidence to the *average* | Christensen 2007; Feldman 2006; Elga 2007 |
| **Steadfast** | The disagreement *does not* require *reduction*: one may *retain* confidence if one has *independent* grounds | Kelly 2005; van Inwagen 1996; Sosa 2010 |
| **Total evidence** | The disagreement is *evidence*, but its *weight* depends on the *details* of the case | Lackey 2010; Kelly 2010 |

### 3.3 The equal-weight view

**The argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{If } S_1 \text{ and } S_2 \text{ are epistemic peers, their evidence} \text{ is the same}. \\
2. & \text{If their evidence is the same, their justified credences should be the same.} \\
3. & \text{They disagree; so at least one is wrong.} \\
4. & \text{Each should treat the disagreement as evidence that her own credence is mis-calibrated.} \\
\hline
5. & \therefore \text{Each should reduce her credence to the average (or split the difference).}
\end{array}
$$

**The formal rule.**

$$
c_{\text{new}}(p) = \frac{c_1(p) + c_2(p)}{2}.
$$

**The “conciliatory” intuition.** The view *captures* the *epistemic* *humility* that Ch. 4, §4.6 recommended. If someone as *informed* and as *careful* as I am *disagrees*, I should *take* it *seriously*, and the *taking* *seriously* is *conciliation*.

**The objection: “spinelessness.”** If I *conciliate* at *every* disagreement, I *lose* my *beliefs*. Elga (2007) calls this the *problem* of the *“spineless”* *conciliator*: the agent who *reduces* her *confidence* at *every* *disagreement* *ends* *up* with *no* *beliefs* at all. (The *reply*: the *reduction* is *only* *required* for *peer* disagreement, and *peer* disagreement is *rare*; the *reply* is *contested*.)

### 3.4 The steadfast view

**The argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The disagreement is evidence only if the disagreeing party has access to information the subject lacks, or is more reliable.} \\
2. & \text{If the peer is genuinely a peer, she has no such access, and is not more reliable.} \\
3. & \text{The disagreement is therefore not independent evidence; it is merely a restatement of the peer's assessment.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The subject may retain her confidence.}
\end{array}
$$

**The “symmetry breaker.”** Kelly’s (2005) *key* *idea*: the *subject* may *retain* her *confidence* if she has an *independent* *reason* — a *symmetry breaker* — to think that her *assessment* is *better* than the *peer’s*. The *reasons*:

- She has *more* *evidence*.
- She has *better* *reasoning* *skills*.
- She has *considered* the *peer’s* *objections* and *found* them *wanting*.

**The objection: “dogmatism.”** The *steadfast* view *licenses* *dogmatism*: the *subject* can *always* *claim* to have a *symmetry breaker*, and the *claim* is *self-serving*. (The *reply*: the *symmetry breaker* must be *independent* of the *first-order* *dispute*; it *cannot* be *merely* “I am *right*.” Whether the *reply* is *satisfactory* is *contested*.)

### 3.5 The total-evidence view

**The argument.** The *disagreement* is *evidence*, but its *weight* is *not* *fixed*; it *depends* on the *details* of the case.

**The structure** (Lackey 2010, 2018; Kelly 2010). The *subject* must *weigh* the *disagreement* against the *other* *evidence*. The *weight* depends on:

- The *independence* of the *peer*.
- The *competence* of the *peer*.
- The *reasonableness* of the *peer’s* *assessment*.
- The *stakes* of the *dispute*.

**The naming.** The *literature* usually *distinguishes* two *neighboring* *positions* here. The *justificationist* *view* (Lackey) holds that the *degree* of *antecedent* *justification* *governs* the *response*: if you were *highly* *justified* *before* the *disagreement* was *discovered*, little or no *conciliation* is *called for*. The *total-evidence* *view* (Kelly) holds that the *first-order* *evidence* must *also* *enter* the *calculation*, so that the *justified* *attitude* is *determined* by *all* the *evidence* and *not* by the *higher-order* *evidence* *alone*.

**The “justificationist” *picture*.** The *subject*’s *confidence* is *justified* if it is *proportionate* to the *total* *evidence*, and the *disagreement* is *part* of the *evidence*. The *conciliation* is *required* only when the *disagreement* is *sufficient* to *outweigh* the *other* *evidence*.

**The objection.** The *view* is *under-specified*: it *tells* us *that* the *weight* *depends* on the *details*, but it *does not* *tell* us *how* to *weigh* them. The *under-specification* is *not* a *defect* (the *details* *are* *complex*), but it *leaves* the *agent* *without* *guidance*.

### 3.6 Higher-order evidence

The *most important* *recent* *development*: the *distinction* between *first-order* and *higher-order* *evidence*.

| Kind | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **First-order** | Evidence *about* the *subject* *matter* | The *data* *supporting* the *theory* |
| **Higher-order** | Evidence *about* *the* *subject*’s *cognitive* *processes* | Evidence that I am *tired*, *biased*, or *mistaken* |

**The key claim** (Christensen 2010; Feldman 2009; Lasonen-Aarnio 2014). Higher-order evidence can *defeat* first-order *justification*, even if the first-order evidence is *unchanged*.

**The “conciliation” *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & S \text{ has first-order evidence } E \text{ that supports } p. \\
2. & S \text{ receives higher-order evidence } D \text{ that her assessment of } E \text{ is unreliable.} \\
3. & \text{If } D \text{ is undefeated, it undermines the justification from } E. \\
\hline
4. & \therefore S \text{ is not justified in believing } p.
\end{array}
$$

**The “level-splitting” *objection*** (Lasonen-Aarnio 2014). The *argument* *assumes* that the *higher-order* *evidence* *transmits* to the *first-order* *belief*. The *objection*: the *levels* are *separate*; the *first-order* *justification* is *intact*, and the *higher-order* *evidence* *affects* only the *second-order* *belief* (the *belief* about the *belief*). The *objection* is *technical*, and it is *contested*.

### 3.7 The application to philosophy

The *disagreement* *among* *philosophers* is the *paradigm* *case* (Ch. 9, §6.4).

**The “meta-induction”** (Chalmers 2015). The *history* of *philosophy* is a *history* of *unresolved* *disagreement*. The *induction*: *philosophers* have *disagreed* for *2,000* years; they *will* *probably* *continue* to *disagree*. The *conclusion*: *philosophical* *confidence* should be *low*.

**The *structure*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{If a discipline has unresolved disagreement for centuries, the disagreement is evidence that the questions are hard or the methods are weak.} \\
2. & \text{Philosophy has unresolved disagreement for centuries.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{Philosophical confidence should be calibrated to the disagreement.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *objection*.** The *meta-induction* *proves* *too* *much*: it would *undermine* the *confidence* in the *meta-induction* *itself* (Ch. 10, §5.5). (The *reply*: the *meta-induction* is *self-referential* but *not* *self-refuting*; the *confidence* in the *meta-induction* is *itself* *calibrated*.)

**The *practical* *upshot*.** The *philosophical* *diary* (Ch. 4, §5.1), the *steel* *man* (Ch. 2, §4.3), and the *calibrated* *confidence* (Ch. 4, §4.1) are the *technologies* for *managing* the *disagreement*. The *virtue* is *humility* — the *recognition* that the *disagreement* is *evidence*, and that the *confidence* is *calibrated*, not *absolute*.

## 4. Epistemic Injustice

### 4.1 Fricker’s taxonomy

Miranda Fricker’s *Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing* (2007) is the most influential work in *social* epistemology in the last two decades. The *central* *distinction* is between two *kinds* of *injustice*.

| Kind | What it is | Paradigm |
|---|---|---|
| **Testimonial injustice** | A *credibility* *deficit* *inflicted* on a *speaker* because of *prejudice* | The *police* *officer* who *does* *not* *believe* the *Black* *witness* (Fricker’s case of Duwayne Brooks) |
| **Hermeneutical injustice** | A *gap* in the *collective* *interpretive* *resources* that *prevents* a *subject* from *understanding* her *own* *experience* | The *woman* *who* *experiences* *sexual* *harassment* *before* the *concept* *existed* |

**The structure of testimonial injustice.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & S \text{ is entitled to credibility proportionate to her evidence.} \\
2. & S \text{ receives a credibility deficit — less than proportionate — because of a prejudice (race, gender, class, accent).} \\
3. & \text{The deficit is unjustified (it does not track the evidence).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore S \text{ is wronged epistemically — her knowledge is not recognized.}
\end{array}
$$

**The structure of hermeneutical injustice.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & S \text{ has an experience that lacks a collective concept.} \\
2. & \text{The lack of concept prevents } S \text{ from understanding the experience and from communicating it.} \\
3. & \text{The lack is not accidental; it reflects the marginalization of the group to which } S \text{ belongs.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore S \text{ is wronged epistemically — her experience is hermeneutically silenced.}
\end{array}
$$

**The paradigm.** Fricker’s *example*: the *woman* *in* the *1960s* who *experiences* *unwanted* *sexual* *attention* at *work*, but *has* *no* *concept* of *sexual* *harassment*. She *cannot* *understand* *what* is *happening* to her, and she *cannot* *communicate* it to *others*. The *concept* was *created* *collectively*, and *until* it *was*, the *experience* was *hermeneutically* *invisible*.

**The “collective” *dimension*.** Hermeneutical injustice is *not* an *individual* *failing*; it is a *structural* *feature* of the *collective* *conceptual* *resources*. The *resources* are *shaped* by *power*, and the *shaping* *systematically* *disadvantages* *marginalized* *groups*.

### 4.2 The *politics* of *credibility*

The *distribution* of *credibility* is *not* *neutral*; it *tracks* *social* *identity*.

**The *empirical* *evidence*.** The *social* *psychology* *literature* (Fiske et al. 2002; Greenwald & Krieger 2006) *documents* *systematic* *credibility* *deficits*. The *examples*:

- **Race**: Black *patients* *receive* *less* *pain* *treatment* (Hoffman et al. 2016); Black *students* *are* *less* *likely* to *be* *believed* when they *report* *racism* (the *experimental* *literature*).
- **Gender**: Women’s *testimony* *is* *less* *likely* to *be* *believed* in *sexual* *assault* *cases* (Fricker 2007); women’s *medical* *symptoms* *are* *more* *likely* to be *dismissed* as *psychosomatic*.
- **Class and accent**: *Working-class* and *non-native* *speakers* *face* *credibility* *deficits* (Fricker; the *linguistic* *literature*).
- **Disability**: *Disabled* *people*’s *testimony* *about* their *own* *needs* *is* *often* *discounted* (Carel & Kidd 2014).

**The *structural* *consequence*.** The *deficits* *compound*: the *subject* who *is* *not* *believed* *is* *less* *likely* to *report* *injustice* in *future*, and the *under-reporting* *reinforces* the *perception* that *injustice* is *rare*. The *result* is a *vicious* *cycle*.

### 4.3 The *concept* of *epistemic* *injustice* *and* *its* *reception*

The *concept* *has* *been* *widely* *adopted*, and *widely* *criticized*.

**The *supporters*.** The *concept* *captures* a *real* *phenomenon* — the *experience* of *not* *being* *heard* — and *connects* it to the *structural* *distribution* of *power*.

**The *critics*.**

- **The *conceptual* *critique*** (e.g., Medina 2011; Coady 2010). The *two* *kinds* Fricker *distinguishes* are *too* *narrow*: *Medina* argues that the *harm* is not the *deficit* *as* *such* but a *disproportionate* *credibility* *judgment* — and that the *excess* of *credibility* *conferred* on the *privileged* is the *mirror* *image* of the *deficit*; *Coady* argues that *epistemic* *injustice* covers *more* *than* *the* *two* *kinds*. (Fricker’s *reply*: the *harm* of the *deficit* is the *distinctive* *epistemic* *wrong*, and *excess* is a *different* *harm*.)
- **The *empirical* *critique*** (e.g., the *experimental* *literature*). The *psychology* of *credibility* is *more* *complex* than the *concept* *suggests*: the *effects* are *context-dependent*, and the *simple* *race/gender* *generalizations* *do* *not* *always* *hold*.
- **The *political* *critique*.** The *concept* *has* *been* *used* to *shut* *down* *debate*: the *label* “epistemic injustice” is *powerful*, and *sometimes* *used* to *avoid* *engagement* with *the* *argument*.

**The *defense*.** The *critics* *do* *not* *refute* the *concept*; they *refine* it. The *concept* *names* a *real* *phenomenon*, and the *refinements* (Fricker’s *later* *work*; *Medina* *2013*; *Dotson* *2011, 2014*) *make* it *more* *precise*.

### 4.4 Medina: *active* *ignorance* and *epistemic* *resistance*

José Medina’s *The Epistemology of Resistance* (2013) *extends* Fricker’s *analysis*.

**Active ignorance.** The *ignorance* *of* the *privileged* is *not* *passive* — it is *actively* *maintained*, *through* *habits* of *attention*, *affect*, and *social* *practice*. The *ignorance* is *not* a *lack* of *information*; it is a *structured* *resistance* to the *information*.

**The *structure*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The privileged agent is exposed to the evidence of injustice.} \\
2. & \text{The agent fails to register the evidence, or registers it without belief.} \\
3. & \text{The failure is not accidental; it is motivated by the desire to maintain the privileged position.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The ignorance is active, and the agent is responsible for it.}
\end{array}
$$

**Epistemic resistance.** The *marginalized* *agent* *develops* *cognitive* *resources* — *double* *consciousness*, *meta-lucidity*, *skepticism* toward the *dominant* *frameworks* — that *enable* *resistance* to the *active* *ignorance*.

**The *significance*.** The *concept* *of* *active* *ignorance* *changes* the *locus* of *responsibility*: the *problem* is *not* *only* the *credibility* *deficit* *inflicted* on the *speaker*; it is the *active* *ignorance* of the *hearer*, and the *ignorance* is *blameworthy*.

### 4.5 Dotson: *testimonial* *smothering* and *epistemic* *oppression*

Kristie Dotson (2011, 2012, 2014) *introduces* two *further* *concepts*.

**Testimonial smothering.** The *speaker* *truncates* or *withholds* her *testimony* *because* she *anticipates* that it *will* *not* be *received*. The *smothering* is a *response* to the *credibility* *deficit*, and it is a *form* of *self-censorship* *imposed* by the *epistemic* *environment*.

**Epistemic oppression.** The *structural* *condition* in which a *group*’s *epistemic* *resources* are *systematically* *undermined*. The *oppression* is *not* *reducible* to *individual* *acts* of *injustice*; it is a *feature* of the *system*.

**The *importance*.** The *concepts* *show* that the *epistemic* *injustice* *literature* *must* *move* *beyond* the *individual* *case* to the *structural* *conditions*. The *remedy* is *not* *only* the *virtue* of *testimonial* *justice* (Fricker); it is the *transformation* of the *institutions* and the *conceptual* *resources*.

### 4.6 The *virtue* of *testimonial* *justice*

Fricker’s *positive* *proposal*: the *virtue* of **testimonial justice**.

**The structure.** The *virtuous* *hearer* *corrects* for the *prejudice* in *her* *credibility* *judgments*. The *correction* is *not* a *matter* of *adding* a *fixed* *increment*; it is a *matter* of *refining* the *testimonial* *sensibility*.

**The *two* *forms*.**

| Form | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Reflexive** | The *hearer* *detects* the *prejudice* in *herself* and *corrects* for it | The *academic* who *notices* that she *discounts* the *female* *student* |
| **Naive** | The *hearer* *lacks* the *prejudice* to *begin* with | The *child* who *does* not *know* the *stereotypes* |

**The *objection*.** The *virtue* is *individualistic*: it *locates* the *remedy* in the *hearer*’s *character*, rather than in the *structural* *conditions*. (Fricker’s *reply*: the *virtue* is *necessary* but *not* *sufficient*; the *structural* *change* is *also* *required*.)

## 5. Knowledge in Groups

### 5.1 The question: can groups believe?

The *social* *epistemology* *literature* *distinguishes* two *questions*.

1. **The *aggregation* question**: how do *individual* *beliefs* *combine* into a *group* *belief*?
2. **The *ontology* question**: is the *group* *belief* *irreducible* to the *individual* *beliefs*, or is it *nothing* *over* *and* *above* them?

**The *positions*.**

| Position | Claim | Proponents |
|---|---|---|
| **Summative** | The *group* *believes* $p$ iff *all* (or *most*) *members* *believe* $p$ | Quinton 1975 |
| **Non-summative** | The *group* *believes* $p$ *independently* of *what* *members* *believe* | Gilbert 1989; Tuomela 1995; List & Pettit 2011 |
| **Eliminative** | *Groups* *do* *not* *believe*; the *talk* of *group* *belief* is *metaphorical* | the *individualist* *tradition* (Olson 2003) |

**The *non-summative* *case*.** The *group* *accepts* $p$ *as* a *body*, *through* a *procedure* (a *vote*, a *consensus* *process*, a *constitution*), and the *acceptance* *may* *diverge* from the *beliefs* of the *members*.

**The *example*.** A *committee* *votes* to *adopt* a *report* that *no* *individual* *member* *endorses* *in* *full*. The *group* *believes* the *report*, even though *none* of the *members* *does*.

**The *objection*.** The *group* *cannot* *have* *phenomenal* *states* (Ch. 24); *belief* *requires* *consciousness*; *therefore* *groups* *cannot* *believe*. (The *reply*: *belief* *is* *a* *functional* *state*, and the *group* *can* *realize* the *functional* *role*; the *phenomenal* *objection* *is* *a* *version* of the *“hard problem”* (Ch. 24, §2) *applied* to *groups*.)

### 5.2 The discursive dilemma

The *most* *important* *formal* *result* in *social* *epistemology*.

**The *set-up*.** A *group* of three *members* ($A$, $B$, $C$) *must* *decide* three *propositions*:

- $P$: The *defendant* is *liable*.
- $Q$: The *defendant* *intended* the *harm*.
- $R$: The *defendant* *caused* the *harm*.

The *group* *accepts* the *rule*: $P \leftrightarrow (Q \wedge R)$.

**The *individual* *judgments*.**

| Member | $Q$ | $R$ | $P$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $A$ | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| $B$ | Yes | No | No |
| $C$ | No | Yes | No |

**The *conclusion-based* *aggregation*.** Each *member* *votes* on $P$: $A$ *yes*, $B$ *no*, $C$ *no*. The *majority* *concludes* $\neg P$.

**The *premise-based* *aggregation*.** Each *member* *votes* on $Q$: $A$ *yes*, $B$ *yes*, $C$ *no* → *majority* $Q$. Each *votes* on $R$: $A$ *yes*, $B$ *no*, $C$ *yes* → *majority* $R$. The *conclusion*, *derived* from the *majorities*, is $P$.

**The *dilemma*.** The *two* *aggregation* *procedures* *yield* *opposite* *conclusions* ($\neg P$ and $P$). The *group* *cannot* *satisfy* *all* of the *following* *conditions*:

1. **Anonymity**: the *procedure* does not *privilege* any *member*.
2. **Systematicity**: the *procedure* *applies* the *same* *rule* to *all* *propositions*.
3. **Collective rationality**: the *group*’s *judgments* *satisfy* the *logical* *constraints*.

**The *significance*.** The *dilemma* *shows* that *group* *rationality* is *not* a *free* *lunch*: the *aggregation* of *consistent* *individuals* *can* *produce* an *inconsistent* *group*, and the *choice* of *aggregation* *procedure* *determines* the *outcome*.

### 5.3 The wisdom (and madness) of crowds

**The *Condorcet* *Jury* *Theorem* (1785).** If each *voter* has a *probability* $p > 0.5$ of *getting* the *answer* *right*, and the *votes* are *independent*, then the *probability* that the *majority* is *right* *increases* with the *number* of *voters*, and *approaches* 1 as $n \to \infty$:

$$
P_{\text{majority}} = \sum_{i = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor + 1}^{n} \binom{n}{i} p^i (1-p)^{n-i}.
$$

**The *conditions*.** The *theorem* *proper* *requires* only *two* *assumptions*:

1. **Competence**: each *voter* is *more* *likely* *than* *not* to be *right* ($p > 0.5$).
2. **Independence**: the *votes* are *not* *correlated*.

The *wider* *thesis* of the *wisdom* of *crowds* — that the *crowd* *outperforms* its *members* — *adds* two *further* *assumptions*:

3. **Sincerity**: the *voters* *report* their *beliefs* rather than *strategize*.
4. **Diversity**: the *voters* *have* *different* *information*.

**The *violations*.** The *theorem* *fails* when the *conditions* *are* *violated*:

- **Information cascades** (§6.1): the *independence* is *violated*.
- **Polarization**: the *independence* *is* *violated* — *members* *influence* *each other* — and the *group* *moves* *toward* *extremes* (Sunstein 2002).
- **Groupthink**: the *sincerity* is *violated*; the *members* *self-censor* (Janis 1972).

**The *lesson*.** The *wisdom* of *crowds* is *conditional*; the *conditions* are *fragile*; and the *violation* *produces* *madness*.

### 5.4 Institutional knowledge

**The *epistemic* *role* of *institutions*.** The *knowledge* *of* a *society* is *not* *the* *knowledge* *of* *individuals*; it is *embodied* in *institutions* — *universities*, *courts*, *scientific* *societies*, *archives*, *libraries*.

**The *structure*.** The *institution* *aggregates*, *stores*, *transmits*, and *validates* *knowledge*. The *validation* is *the* *crucial* *function*: the *institution* *certifies* *what* *counts* as *knowledge*, and the *certification* is *a* *social* *process*.

**The *examples*.**

| Institution | Epistemic function | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| **Science** | The *production* and *validation* of *empirical* *knowledge* | *Fraud*, *replication* *crisis*, *publication* *bias* |
| **The *court*** | The *adjudication* of *testimony* and *evidence* | *Wrongful* *conviction*, *prosecutorial* *misconduct* |
| **The *university*** | The *transmission* and *certification* of *knowledge* | *Credentialism*, *ideological* *capture* |
| **The *press*** | The *dissemination* and *verification* of *public* *knowledge* | *Sensationalism*, *partisanship*, *the* *collapse* of *gatekeeping* |

**The *epistemic* *virtues* of *institutions*.** The *institution* *can* *be* *epistemically* *virtuous* *only* if it *has*:

- **Transparency**: the *process* is *open* to *scrutiny*.
- **Accountability**: the *agents* are *responsible* for *errors*.
- **Diversity**: the *participants* *have* *different* *perspectives*.
- **Independence**: the *institution* is *not* *captured* by *external* *interests*.

**The *failure* *modes*.** The *institution* *can* *fail* in *all* of these *dimensions*, and the *failures* *are* *not* *independent*: the *capture* *undermines* the *diversity*, the *lack* of *transparency* *undermines* the *accountability*, and the *result* is *a* *system* that *produces* *false* *knowledge* *with* *the* *authority* *of* *truth*.

## 6. The Internet, AI, and the Crisis of Authority

### 6.1 Information cascades

**The *phenomenon*.** An **information cascade** (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch 1992) *occurs* when *agents* *ignore* their *private* *information* and *follow* the *observed* *behavior* of *others*.

**The *model*.** Each *agent* *has* a *private* *signal* (a *noisy* *indication* of the *truth*) and *observes* the *actions* of *previous* *agents*. The *agent* *updates* *Bayesianly* (Ch. 10, §4) on the *observed* *actions*, and *acts* on the *posterior*.

**The *result*.** After a *few* *agents*, the *public* *signal* *dominates* the *private* *signal*, and *all* *subsequent* *agents* *follow* the *crowd* — *even* *if* their *private* *signal* *says* *otherwise*.

**The *formal* *structure*.**

$$
P(H \mid a_1, \ldots, a_n) = \frac{P(a_1, \ldots, a_n \mid H) P(H)}{P(a_1, \ldots, a_n)}.
$$

The *likelihood* *depends* on the *actions*, and the *actions* *depend* on the *actions* of *others* — so the *likelihood* *becomes* *increasingly* *uninformative* about the *private* *signals*.

**The *consequence*.** The *cascade* is *rational* at each *step*, and *collectively* *irrational*: the *group* *converges* on a *belief* that *no* *member* *would* *hold* on *her* *private* *evidence*.

**The *application*.** The *cascade* *explains*: the *spread* of *rumors*, the *herding* of *financial* *markets*, the *polarization* of *political* *opinion*, and the *diffusion* of *misinformation* *on* *social* *media*.

### 6.2 Bullshit at scale

**The *Frankfurt* *point* (Ch. 11, §2.2) *applied* to the *internet*.**

**The *structural* *change*.** The *internet* *removes* the *gatekeepers* (the *editor*, the *publisher*, the *peer* *reviewer*) and *replaces* them with *algorithms* that *optimize* for *engagement*.

**The *consequence*.** The *incentive* *structure* *rewards* *attention*, not *truth*. The *result* is a *flood* of *content* that is *optimized* for *engagement* and *indifferent* to *truth* — *Frankfurt’s* *bullshit*, *at* *scale*.

**The *asymmetry*.** The *production* of *bullshit* is *cheap*; the *refutation* is *expensive*. The *asymmetry* *favors* the *bullshitter*, and the *advantage* is *structural*.

**The *AI* *dimension*.** Large *language* *models* *generate* *text* that is *fluent*, *plausible*, and *often* *false* — the *“hallucination”* *problem*. The *output* is *not* *lying* (the *model* *does* not *have* *intentions* *in* the *ordinary* *sense*), but it is *bullshit* in *Frankfurt’s* *sense*: *indifferent* to *truth*.

**The *epistemic* *consequence*.** The *AI-generated* *text* *floods* the *information* *environment*, and the *flood* *makes* it *harder* *to* *distinguish* *reliable* *from* *unreliable* *content*. The *verification* *cost* *rises*, and the *trust* *in* *all* *content* *falls*.

### 6.3 Deepfakes and the liar’s dividend

**The *phenomenon*.** A **deepfake** is a *synthetic* *image*, *audio*, or *video* that is *indistinguishable* from *authentic* *recordings*. The *technology* *is* *cheap*, *accessible*, and *improving*.

**The *epistemic* *consequence*.** The *deepfake* *undermines* the *evidential* *value* of *photographic* and *audio-visual* *records*. The *record* *was* the *paradigm* of *reliable* *evidence*; the *deepfake* *makes* it *unreliable*.

**The *“liar’s* *dividend”*** (Chesney & Citron 2019). The *deepfake* *does* not *only* *produce* *false* *evidence*; it *provides* a *pretext* for *denying* *authentic* *evidence*. The *liar* can *claim* that the *authentic* *recording* is a *deepfake*, and the *claim* is *plausible* *because* *deepfakes* *exist*.

**The *structure*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Authentic evidence } E \text{ is presented.} \\
2. & \text{The liar denies } E, \text{ claiming it is synthetic.} \\
3. & \text{The claim is plausible (deepfakes exist).} \\
4. & \text{The audience cannot verify the authenticity.} \\
\hline
5. & \therefore \text{The evidence loses its force — even though it is authentic.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *epistemic* *backstop*** (Rini 2020). The *loss* is *not* *only* the *specific* *evidence*; it is the *loss* of the *general* *trust* *in* *records*. The *backstop* — the *default* *assumption* that *photos* and *recordings* are *authentic* — *collapses*, and the *collapse* *affects* *all* *testimony*, not only the *specific* *cases*.

### 6.4 Echo chambers and filter bubbles

**The *concepts*.**

| Concept | What it is | The *problem* |
|---|---|---|
| **Filter bubble** (Pariser 2011) | The *algorithmic* *curation* of *content* to *match* the *user’s* *existing* *preferences* | The *user* *is* *exposed* only to *confirming* *information* |
| **Echo chamber** (Sunstein 2007) | A *social* *environment* in which *beliefs* are *reinforced* by *repetition* | The *beliefs* *become* *more* *extreme* and *more* *confident* |

**The *distinction*.** The *filter* *bubble* is *algorithmic*; the *echo* *chamber* is *social*. The *two* *interact*: the *algorithm* *selects* the *content*, and the *social* *group* *reinforces* it.

**The *empirical* *status*.** The *claims* are *contested*. Some *studies* (Flaxman, Goel & Rao 2016) *find* that *online* *exposure* is *more* *diverse* than *the* *offline* *exposure*; others (Bakshy et al. 2015) *find* that *the* *algorithms* *do* *reduce* *diversity*. The *effects* are *small*, *context-dependent*, and *hard* to *measure*.

**The *philosophical* *significance*.** Even if the *empirical* *effects* are *modest*, the *structural* *change* is *real*: the *conditions* of the *Condorcet* *theorem* (§5.3) are *violated*, and the *violation* *is* *designed* into the *system*.

### 6.5 The crisis of authority

**The *structure*.** The *traditional* *epistemic* *authority* was *institutional*: the *university*, the *press*, the *government*, the *church*. The *authority* was *not* *infallible*, but it was *identifiable*, and the *identification* *enabled* *trust*.

**The *change*.** The *internet* *decentralizes* the *production* and *distribution* of *information*. The *decentralization* is *democratizing* (anyone can *publish*), and it is *destabilizing* (the *old* *authorities* *lose* their *monopoly*, and the *new* *authorities* are *not* *established*).

**The *consequence*.** The *crisis* has *three* *dimensions*.

1. **Epistemic**: it is *harder* to *identify* *reliable* *sources*.
2. **Political**: the *loss* of *shared* *authority* *undermines* the *possibility* of *public* *deliberation* (Ch. 47, §6).
3. **Existential**: the *loss* of *trust* is *disorienting*; the *agent* *cannot* *know* *whom* to *believe*.

**The *responses*.** The *responses* *are* *institutional*, *individual*, and *structural*.

| Level | Response | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Institutional** | *Rebuild* the *gatekeepers*; *regulate* the *platforms*; *fund* *public* *media* | The *BBC*; the *EU* *Digital* *Services* *Act* |
| **Individual** | *Cultivate* the *epistemic* *virtues*; *verify* the *sources*; *diversify* the *inputs* | *Lateral* *reading* (Wineburg & McGrew 2019) |
| **Structural** | *Design* the *platforms* for *truth*, not *engagement*; *transparency* in *algorithms* | *Open* *source* *algorithms*; *audit* *requirements* |

**The *epistemic* *virtue*.** The *virtue* is *not* *skepticism* (which *paralyzes*) and *not* *credulity* (which *is* *exploited*). It is **epistemic *vigilance***: the *disposition* to *check*, *corroborate*, and *calibrate*, *combined* with the *willingness* to *trust* the *sources* that *have* *earned* it.

## 7. Testimony about the Past

### 7.1 History as testimony

**The *structure*.** The *historian* *works* with *testimony* — *documents*, *records*, *letters*, *memoirs*, *oral* *traditions*, *archaeological* *remains*. The *testimony* is *the* *evidence*, and the *historian’s* *task* is to *interpret* it.

**The *epistemic* *question*.** How can *testimony* about the *past* be *reliable*, given that the *past* *is* *not* *observable*?

**The *answer* (Coady 1992; Danto 1985).** The *historian* *uses* the *same* *canons* as the *testimony* *of* the *present*:

1. **The *source* *criticism*: who *produced* the *document*, and *why*?**
2. **The *corroboration*: is the *testimony* *consistent* with *other* *sources*?**
3. **The *causal* *plausibility*: does the *testimony* *fit* the *known* *causal* *structure* of the *period*?**

**The *difference*.** The *historian* *cannot* *cross-examine* the *witness*, and the *witness* *cannot* be *pressed* in *the* *way* a *witness* is *pressed* in *court*. The *absence* *makes* the *interpretation* *more* *uncertain*, and it *raises* the *stakes* of the *source* *criticism*.

### 7.2 The archive and its silences

**The *archive*.** The *archive* is *not* a *neutral* *repository*; it is a *selection*. What is *preserved* reflects *who* *had* the *power* to *write*, *to* *keep*, and *to* *transmit*.

**The *silences*** (Trouillot 1995). The *archive* *has* *silences*: the *voices* of the *marginalized* are *under-represented*, and the *under*-representation is *not* *accidental*.

**The *four* *moments* of *silencing* (Trouillot).** The *silence* *enters* at *four* *stages*:

1. **The *moment* of *fact-creation*: what *happens* is *already* *shaped* by *power*.**
2. **The *moment* of *fact-assembly*: what is *recorded* is *selected*.**
3. **The *moment* of *fact-retrieval*: what *survives* is *what* is *preserved*.**
4. **The *moment* of *retrospective* *significance*: what is *remembered* is *what* is *narrated*.**

**The *consequence*.** The *historian* who *relies* on the *archive* *uncritically* *reproduces* the *silences*. The *critical* *historian* *must* *read* *against* the *grain*, and *seek* the *sources* that *the* *archive* *omits*.

### 7.3 Memory as testimony

**The *parallel*.** The *memory* of the *individual* is a *form* of *testimony* — the *testimony* of the *past* *self* to the *present* *self* (Ch. 26, §§3.2 and 4.1).

**The *reliability* *question*.** The *memory* is *reconstructive*, not *photographic* (Bartlett 1932; Loftus 1979, 1996). The *reconstruction* is *shaped* by *schema*, *suggestion*, and *narrative*.

**The “false memory” debate.** The *recovered* *memory* *movement* (1980s–1990s) *claimed* that *traumatic* *memories* *could* *be* *repressed* and *recovered* in *therapy*. The *empirical* *evidence* is *mixed*, but it *undercuts* the *strongest* *versions* of the *claim*: in *Loftus* and *Ketcham*’s *experiment* (1994), some of the “recovered” *memories* of a *planted* *event* were *suggested* by the *experimenter*, and the *suggestion* *produces* *confabulated* *memories*. The *base* *rates* of *false* *recoveries* in *clinical* *practice* *remain* *contested*, and the *dispute* over the *movement* is *not* *closed*.

**The *epistemic* *lesson*.** The *memory* *is* *reliable* *enough* for *ordinary* *purposes*, but it is *not* *reliable* *enough* for *the* *high-stakes* *cases* (criminal *trials*, *historical* *atrocities*) *without* *corroboration*.

### 7.4 Testimony and atrocity

**The *question*.** How should we *treat* the *testimony* of *survivors* of *atrocity* — the *Holocaust*, the *genocides*, the *massacres*?

**The *tension*.** The *testimony* is *essential*: it is *the* *primary* *evidence* of the *atrocity*, and it is *the* *voice* of the *victims*. But it is *also* *fragile*: the *memory* *is* *traumatic*, the *trauma* *distorts*, and the *testimony* *is* *contested* by *deniers*.

**The *epistemic* *structure*.** The *testimony* *must* be *treated* *critically* *and* *respectfully*: *critically* (it is *evidence*, and it *must* be *assessed*), *respectfully* (the *speaker* *has* *authority* *over* her *own* *experience*, and the *dismissal* is a *form* of *testimonial* *injustice*).

**The *denial* *case*.** The *Holocaust* *denier* *uses* the *canons* of *evidence* to *dismiss* the *testimony*. The *denial* is *not* a *legitimate* *skepticism*; it is a *form* of *epistemic* *injustice* — the *testimony* is *dismissed* *not* because of *evidence*, but because of *ideology*.

**The *criterion*.** The *difference* between *legitimate* *criticism* and *denial* is the *willingness* to *engage* with the *evidence* and to *revise* the *belief* in the *light* of it. The *denier* is *not* *engaged*; she is *committed* to the *conclusion*, and the *conclusion* *determines* the *treatment* of the *evidence*.

## 8. The State of the Debate

### 8.1 What is settled

- **Testimony is a basic source.** The anti-reductionist consensus is *dominant*, and the *reductionist* *argument* *fails* *because* *of* *circularity*.
- **Disagreement is real and epistemically significant.** The *equal-weight* *view* is *too* *strong*, and the *steadfast* *view* is *too* *weak*; the *total-evidence* *view* is the *most* *defensible*.
- **Epistemic injustice is real.** The *concept* *captures* a *genuine* *phenomenon*, and the *empirical* *literature* *documents* the *credibility* *deficits* *that* *motivate* *it*.
- **Group knowledge is possible, but fragile.** The *discursive* *dilemma* *shows* that *aggregation* is *not* a *free* *lunch*, and the *Condorcet* *conditions* *are* *easily* *violated*.
- **The internet has changed the landscape.** The *crisis* of *authority* is *real*, and it is *structural*, *not* *incidental*.

### 8.2 What is open

- **The reductionism/anti-reductionism debate.** The *hybrid* *position* is *promising*, but the *relation* between *default* *entitlement* and *critical* *monitoring* is *underdeveloped*.
- **The disagreement debate.** The *total-evidence* *view* is *under-specified*, and the *weight* of *higher-order* *evidence* is *contested*.
- **The group-belief debate.** The *ontology* of *group* *belief* is *unresolved*, and the *aggregation* *procedures* are *contested*.
- **The AI debate.** The *epistemic* *consequences* of *AI-generated* *content* are *not* *yet* *understood*, and the *responses* are *speculative*.
- **The archive debate.** The *silences* are *acknowledged*, but the *methods* for *correcting* them are *underdeveloped*.

### 8.3 The methodological lesson

The chapter is a case study in the method of Part I.

- **Conceptual analysis** (§1 of Ch. 3) generated the *distinction* between the *basic* and the *derivative* sources.
- **Thought experiments** (§2 of Ch. 3) — the *creationist* *teacher*, the *peer* *disagreement* — are the *engine* of the debate.
- **Formalization** (§3 of Ch. 3) — the *Bayesian* *model*, the *Condorcet* *theorem*, the *discursive* *dilemma* — is essential for the social *epistemology*.
- **Reflective equilibrium** (§4 of Ch. 3) is the method by which the *intuitions* and the *theories* are adjusted.
- **Genealogy and debunking** (§5 of Ch. 3) — the *structural* *analysis* of *credibility* — is a *serious* *challenge* to the *individualist* *tradition*.
- **Negative capability** (§7 of Ch. 3) is the *response* to the *residue*: the *humility* to hold the *question* open.

The *honest* verdict: the social *epistemology* is a *field in transition*. The *individualist* *tradition* is *untenable*; the *social* *turn* is *promising* but *incomplete*; the *crisis* of *authority* is *real* and *unresolved*. The *unresolved* character is *instructive*: it shows what philosophical progress looks like — not a *settled answer*, but a *sharper understanding* of what the *question* is.

### 8.4 The dialectical map

| Position | Core claim | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reductionism** | Testimony is derivative | Naturalistic; critical | Circular; impossible |
| **Anti-reductionism** | Testimony is basic | Fits the acquisition of language | Too permissive |
| **Hybrid** | Basic + monitoring | Fits the phenomenology | Underdeveloped |
| **Equal weight** | Conciliation is required | Humble | Spineless |
| **Steadfast** | Conciliation is not required | Autonomous | Dogmatic |
| **Total evidence** | The weight depends on the details | Balanced | Under-specified |
| **Fricker** | Epistemic injustice is structural | Powerful | Individualistic remedy |
| **Medina** | Active ignorance | Changes the locus of responsibility | Hard to measure |
| **List & Pettit** | Group agency | Formal; rigorous | Contested ontology |
| **Sunstein** | The crisis of authority | Diagnoses the problem | The solutions are speculative |

The *table* is a *map*, not a *verdict*. The *reader* who wants to *choose* a *position* must decide which *cost* is *acceptable* — and the *decision* is a *philosophical* *argument*.

## Optional threads

Cross-cutting material, for the reader who wants to go deeper.

**1. The *Bayesian* *model* of *testimony*.** The *Bayesian* *framework* (Ch. 10, §4) *models* the *hearer*’s *credence* as a *function* of the *speaker’s* *credibility*. The *models* (Bovens & Hartmann 2003) *are* *formal* and *precise*, but they *idealize* the *hearer’s* *knowledge* of the *speaker’s* *reliability*, which is *usually* *unavailable*.

**2. The *epistemology* of *hearsay*.** The *legal* *treatment* of *hearsay* is a *case* *study* in the *epistemology* of *testimony*: the *rules* *exclude* *hearsay* *because* the *speaker* *cannot* be *cross-examined*, and the *exclusion* *encodes* a *theory* of *testimonial* *reliability*. The *theory* is *contested* (see the *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry, “The Legal Concept of Evidence”).

**3. Non-Western *epistemologies* of *testimony*.** The *Indian* *pramāṇa* *tradition* *treats* *śabda* (testimony) as a *distinct* *source* of *knowledge*, with *its* *own* *validity* *conditions*. The *debate* between the *Nyāya* *realists* and the *Buddhist* *nominalists* is a *version* of the *reductionism/anti-reductionism* *debate* (Matilal 1986; Ganeri 2001). The *African* *communalist* *epistemology* (Wiredu 1996; Oyowe 2013) *treats* *knowledge* as *social* *by* *nature*, and the *treatment* is a *systematic* *alternative* to the *individualist* *tradition*.

**4. The *epistemology* of *silence*.** The *silence* is *not* *merely* the *absence* of *testimony*; it is a *form* of *communication* (and of *power*). The *silence* of the *oppressed* (Dotson 2011) and the *silence* of the *archive* (Trouillot 1995) are *epistemic* *phenomena* with *distinct* *structures*.

**5. The *epistemology* of *AI* *testimony*.** If an *AI* *system* *produces* *testimony*, is it *testimony* *in* the *ordinary* *sense*? The *question* is *connected* to the *analysis* of *knowledge* (Ch. 7) and to the *nature* of *assertion* (Ch. 34). The *AI* *system* *does* not *have* *beliefs* (on *most* *views*), so the *knowledge* *norm* of *assertion* (Ch. 7, §4.2) *cannot* *apply*; and the *absence* of *the* *norm* *changes* the *epistemic* *status* of the *output*.

## Summary

- **Testimony** is the *social* *source* of *knowledge*. The *central* *question* is whether it is *basic* or *derivative*.
- **Reductionism** (Hume) holds that testimony is justified by an inference from the reliability of testifiers. The view is **circular** and **impossible**: the evidence for reliability is itself testimonial.
- **Anti-reductionism** (Reid, Coady, Burge) holds that testimony is a *basic* source with a *default* *entitlement*. The view is *dominant*, but it is *too permissive*, and it *neglects* the *hearer*’s *monitoring*.
- **The hybrid position** is the most defensible: testimony is *basic*, *with* a *critical* monitoring, and the monitoring is a *perceptual* sensitivity to the *speaker*’s credibility.
- **Transmission vs. generation**: testimony *transmits* knowledge (Welbourne) or *generates* it (Lackey). The *counterexamples* (the *creationist* *teacher*) *show* that the distinction is *not* *simple*.
- **Trust** is a *normative* relation, distinct from *reliance*. **Bullshit** (Frankfurt) is *more* *dangerous* than *lying*, because it *undermines* the *authority* of truth.
- **Disagreement**: the **equal-weight** view (conciliate) is *too strong*; the **steadfast** view (retain) is *too weak*; the **total-evidence** view is *most defensible*, but *under-specified*. The **higher-order** evidence debate is the *newest* front.
- **Epistemic injustice** (Fricker): **testimonial** injustice is a *credibility* *deficit*; **hermeneutical** injustice is a *gap* in the *collective* *interpretive* resources. **Medina** adds *active* *ignorance*; **Dotson** adds *testimonial* *smothering* and *epistemic* *oppression*.
- **Group knowledge**: the **discursive dilemma** (List & Pettit) shows that *aggregation* is *not* a *free* *lunch*. The **Condorcet Jury Theorem** is *conditional*: it *fails* when *competence* or *independence* *is* *violated*, and the *wider* *thesis* *fails* when *sincerity* or *diversity* *is* *violated*.
- **The internet** has *produced* a *crisis* of *authority*. **Information cascades**, **bullshit at scale**, **deepfakes** (and the *liar’s* *dividend*), and **echo chambers** are *structural* *features* of the *new* *environment*.
- **Testimony about the past**: the **archive** has *silences*, the **memory** is *reconstructive*, and the **testimony of atrocity** *must* be treated *critically* and *respectfully*.

The result is *not* a *solution* to the *problem* of *testimony*, and it is *not* a *refutation* of the *individualist* *tradition*. It is a *widening*: knowledge is *social* by *nature*, and the *epistemology* that *ignores* the *social* *conditions* *misses* the *phenomenon*.

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter turns to *acquaintance* and *description* — Russell’s *distinction*, which the *social* *turn* has *transformed* but *not* *replaced*. If *testimony* is *basic*, then *singular* *thought* is *not* the *monopoly* of *acquaintance*: we can *think* about *objects* we *know* only by *description*, and the *thoughts* are *about* the *objects* because the *descriptions* *succeed* in *picking* them out. The connection is direct: the *social* *epistemology* *complicates* the *distinction* between *knowing* *of* and *knowing* *that*, and the *next* *chapter* *examines* the *complication*.
