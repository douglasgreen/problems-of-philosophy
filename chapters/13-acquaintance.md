# Chapter 12 — Acquaintance, Description, and Singular Thought

> “We have acquaintance with anything of which we are directly aware, without the intermediary of any process of inference or any knowledge of truths. … Every proposition which we can understand must be composed wholly of constituents with which we are acquainted.”
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Ch. V

> “It is not the case that I am acquainted with myself. I am not presented to myself. … I am *I*, but I do not know *who* I am.”
> — A reading of Hume, *Treatise* I.iv.6; the source of one of Russell’s difficulties

---

Chapter 11 ended with a puzzle about the *social* conditions of knowledge: if testimony is a basic source, then most of what we believe is believed on the authority of others, and the *solitary* epistemology of Chapters 5–10 is a fiction. But the social turn raises a question that is older than testimony, and more fundamental: **what is the structure of a thought about a particular object?**

When I think *that table*, or *that person*, or *Aristotle*, the thought is *about* something. The thought has a *singular* content — it is directed at an object, not at a kind. What makes it singular? What connects the thought to the object? And what does the *subject* have to know, or be able to do, in order to have such a thought?

These questions are the subject of this chapter. They were posed most sharply by Russell, in the distinction between **knowledge by acquaintance** and **knowledge by description**, and the distinction generated a research programme that is still active: the theory of descriptions, the semantics of names, the various theories of singular thought, and the epistemology of self-knowledge.

The chapter has a distinctive shape, because the territory has been *rebuilt twice*. Russell's *principle of acquaintance* — that every proposition we understand is composed of constituents we are acquainted with — was the foundation of his epistemology, and it is no longer tenable. But the *distinction* that the principle was meant to explain — between the *direct* and the *indirect* relation to an object — survives, and it is the ancestor of the contemporary debate about *singular thought*. The chapter's argument is that the *distinction* is real, the *principle* is false, and the *successor* programme is the most interesting thing in the philosophy of language.

We proceed in eight movements. §1 restates Russell's distinction and the principle of acquaintance. §2 presents the theory of descriptions, the most successful piece of analytic philosophy ever written. §3 presents the modern critique — Donnellan, Kripke, direct reference, the causal theory. §4 takes up demonstratives and indexicals, where the *essential* indexical lives. §5 takes up singular thought and object-dependence. §6 takes up self-knowledge and self-acquaintance. §7 assesses where Russell's framework survives and where it must be rebuilt. §8 gives an honest verdict.

## 1. Russell's Distinction Restated

### 1.1 Knowledge of and knowledge that

Russell's distinction, in *The Problems of Philosophy* Ch. V, is between two kinds of knowledge:

| Kind | Form | Example | Object |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge of things** | $S$ knows $x$ | I know this table; I know the colour red | A *thing* |
| **Knowledge that** | $S$ knows that $p$ | I know that the table is brown | A *proposition* |

The first is a *relation to an object*; the second is a *relation to a truth*. The distinction is grammatical, but it is not merely grammatical: it corresponds to a difference in *kind*.

**Russell's claim.** Knowledge *that* is *analysable* into knowledge *of* plus the general principles that connect the constituents. To know that the table is brown, I must be *acquainted with* the table (or with sense-data that represent it) and with the universal *brownness*, and I must grasp the proposition that connects them.

**The priority thesis.** Knowledge *of* is *epistemically prior*: every proposition we can understand is composed of constituents we are acquainted with. This is the **principle of acquaintance**, and it is the foundation of Russell's epistemology.

### 1.2 The principle of acquaintance

The principle is stated at the end of Ch. V:

> “Every proposition which we can understand must be composed wholly of constituents with which we are acquainted.”

**The argument for the principle.** Russell's argument is *transcendental*, in something like the Kantian sense:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{To understand a proposition, one must be *acquainted with* its constituents.} \\
2. & \text{If a constituent is not *given* in acquaintance, one can only *describe* it by other constituents.} \\
3. & \text{The description is itself composed of constituents that must be *given*.} \\
4. & \text{The regress of descriptions must terminate in acquaintance.} \\
\hline
5. & \therefore \text{Every proposition we understand is composed of constituents we are acquainted with.}
\end{array}
$$

**The structure.** The argument is a *regress* argument, structurally parallel to the regress of justification (Ch. 8, §1). The regress of *descriptions* terminates in *acquaintance*; the regress of *justifications* terminates in *basic beliefs*. The parallel is not accidental: Russell's epistemology is foundationalist in both domains.

**The thesis is strong.** It says that *every* proposition we understand is *anchored* in acquaintance. If there is a proposition whose constituents are not given in acquaintance, we cannot understand it. The thesis is the foundation of Russell's *empiricism*, and it is the target of the modern critique.

### 1.3 The list

Russell's list of the things we are acquainted with (*Problems*, Ch. V; Ch. X for universals):

| Category | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Sense-data** | The brown patch, the shape-from-here | Not the material object |
| **Introspective states** | My current pain, my current belief | Directly given |
| **Memory** | The past experience, as remembered | Acquaintance with the *memory*, not the *past* |
| **Universals** | Brownness, before-ness, identity | Ch. X; the "knowledge of universals" |
| **The self** | (Contested) | Russell *wavered*; in 1912 he was cautious |

**What is *not* on the list.** Material objects (known by description), other minds (known by analogy), the past (known by memory, but the memory is the object of acquaintance, not the past event), and the self as a *substance* (contested).

**The asymmetry.** The list is *narrow*: it includes only the *immediate* deliverances of experience. The *world* of tables and persons is *outside* the list, and it is known only by *inference* — which is why Russell's epistemology is *sceptical* (Ch. 6) and why the *principle of acquaintance* generates the *problem* of the external world.

### 1.4 The social challenge

Chapter 11 showed that testimony is a *basic* source of knowledge. If that is right, the principle of acquaintance is *false*.

**The argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{We understand propositions whose constituents we are *not* acquainted with.} \\
2. & \text{Example: "Napoleon was defeated at Waterloo." I have never been acquainted with Napoleon, nor with Waterloo, nor with the defeat.} \\
3. & \text{I understand the proposition; I can use it in reasoning; I can assess its evidence.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The principle of acquaintance is false.}
\end{array}
$$

**The replies.**

**(i) The "descriptive" reply.** The proposition is *understood* as a *description*: "the person called 'Napoleon' …" The *constituents* are the *description*, not the object. The principle survives because the proposition is *really* about the description.

**The objection.** The reply makes all *apparently singular* thoughts *descriptive*, and it faces the problems of descriptivism (§3). If the description does not *uniquely* pick out Napoleon, the thought is not about Napoleon; and if it does, the thought is about whoever satisfies the description, which is not the same as being about Napoleon.

**(ii) The "causal" reply.** The proposition is understood through the *causal chain* that connects my use of "Napoleon" to Napoleon. The *acquaintance* is not with Napoleon, but with the *name*, and the name's *referent* is fixed by the chain. The principle is *revised*: understanding requires *acquaintance with the name*, not with the object.

**The objection.** The revised principle is *not* the principle of acquaintance; it is a principle of *semantic competence*. It *changes the subject*, and the change is the *content* of §3.

**(iii) The "concession" reply.** The principle is *false*, and the *distinction* between knowledge *of* and knowledge *that* must be *rebuilt* on a different foundation. This is the position of §5, and it is the position this chapter defends.

### 1.5 What survives

The *principle* is false, but the *distinction* is not. Three things survive.

**(i) The distinction between *direct* and *indirect* knowledge.** We have a *direct* relation to some objects (our own experiences, the things we are currently perceiving), and an *indirect* relation to others (the distant, the past, the abstract). The *directness* is not the same as *acquaintance* in Russell's sense, but it is a real phenomenon.

**(ii) The distinction between *singular* and *general* thought.** Some thoughts are *about* particular objects; others are *about* kinds, properties, or classes. The distinction is the *successor* to Russell's, and it is the subject of §5.

**(iii) The theory of descriptions.** The analysis of definite descriptions is the *most successful* piece of analytic philosophy, and it is the *engine* of the modern debate. We turn to it now.

## 2. The Theory of Descriptions

### 2.1 The problem

Russell's "On Denoting" (1905) begins with a *puzzle* about *definite descriptions* — phrases of the form "the $F$."

**The puzzle.** Consider the sentence:

> (1) The present King of France is bald.

The sentence *seems* to be *about* the present King of France. But there *is* no present King of France. So what is the sentence *about*? And is it *true* or *false*?

**The Meinongian answer.** Alexius Meinong (1904) held that there are *nonexistent objects*. The present King of France *is* an object — a *nonexistent* one — and the sentence is *about* him. The sentence is *false*, because the nonexistent King does not have the property of *existing*, and *a fortiori* does not have the property of being *bald*.

**The problem with Meinong.** The view is *ontologically profligate* (it posits a *realm* of nonexistent objects), and it is *incoherent* (the nonexistent King is *both* an object *and* nonexistent — what is the *object* that is *not*?).

**Russell's answer.** The sentence is *not* about a nonexistent object. It is a *complex* of *quantifiers*, *predicates*, and *identity*, and it is *false* — because the *existential* conjunct *fails*. There is no *object* that the sentence is *about*; the apparent *subject* is an *incomplete symbol* that *disappears* under analysis.

### 2.2 The analysis

**The analysis of (1).**

$$
(1') \quad \exists x\,(\mathrm{King}(x) \wedge \mathrm{France}(x) \wedge \forall y((\mathrm{King}(y) \wedge \mathrm{France}(y)) \to y = x) \wedge \mathrm{Bald}(x))
$$

The analysis has *three* components:

| Component | Formal | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **Existence** | $\exists x\,(\mathrm{King}(x) \wedge \mathrm{France}(x))$ | There is a King of France |
| **Uniqueness** | $\forall y((\mathrm{King}(y) \wedge \mathrm{France}(y)) \to y = x)$ | There is at most one |
| **Predication** | $\mathrm{Bald}(x)$ | That thing is bald |

**The virtues.**

**(i) The problem of negative existentials is dissolved.** "The present King of France does not exist" is analysed as:

$$
(2') \quad \neg\exists x\,(\mathrm{King}(x) \wedge \mathrm{France}(x) \wedge \forall y((\mathrm{King}(y) \wedge \mathrm{France}(y)) \to y = x))
$$

The sentence is *true*, and it is *not* self-contradictory. It says that the *existential* conjunct is *false*, which is exactly what we want.

**(ii) The problem of substitutivity is clarified.** The *scope* of the description determines whether substitution of identicals is *valid*. (See §2.3.)

**(iii) The ontological commitment is reduced.** The theory does *not* posit nonexistent objects; it posits only the *quantifiers* and the *predicates*.

**The slogan.** A definite description is an **incomplete symbol**: it has *no meaning in isolation*, but it has a *use* in the *context* of a sentence. The *meaning* of the sentence is given by the *analysis*, and the description *disappears* under analysis.

### 2.3 Scope

The *scope* of a description is the *part* of the sentence that the description *governs*. The scope is *ambiguous* in many sentences, and the ambiguity is the *source* of the puzzle.

**The classic example.** "I thought your yacht was larger than it is." (Russell's own example from "On Denoting," slightly adapted.)

| Reading | Scope | Formal | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wide** | The description has *primary* occurrence | $\exists s\,(\mathrm{Size}(s, \mathrm{yacht}) \wedge \mathrm{Thought}(I, s > s))$ | There is an actual size, and I thought it was greater than itself |
| **Narrow** | The description has *secondary* occurrence | $\mathrm{Thought}(I, \exists s\,(\mathrm{Size}(s, \mathrm{yacht}) \wedge s > \mathrm{actual\ size}))$ | I thought the yacht had a size greater than its actual size |

The *wide* reading is *false* (or at least not what I thought); the *narrow* reading is *true*. The *scope distinction* explains why the sentence is *not* self-contradictory, and it is the *engine* of Russell's *solution*.

**The general lesson.** The *logical form* of a sentence is not the *surface grammar*. The *apparent* subject–predicate structure is *misleading*, and the *analysis* reveals a *quantificational* structure. This is the *method* of analytic philosophy, and it is the *legacy* of "On Denoting."

### 2.4 The objections

The theory of descriptions has been the target of *three* major objections.

**(i) Strawson (1950): presupposition.** Strawson argues that the theory *misdescribes* the *use* of descriptions. When I say "the present King of France is bald," I *presuppose* that there is a King of France, and if the presupposition *fails*, the sentence is *neither true nor false* — it is a *truth-value gap*.

**The argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *use* of a definite description *presupposes* the existence of its referent.} \\
2. & \text{If the presupposition *fails*, the sentence is *not* *false*; it *fails to make a statement*.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The theory of descriptions *misdescribes* the *use*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The objection.** The objection is *about* the *pragmatics*, not the *semantics*. Russell's theory is a *semantic* analysis; Strawson's is a *pragmatic* description. The two are *not* incompatible, and the *contemporary* consensus is that *both* are needed: the *semantics* gives the *truth conditions*, and the *pragmatics* gives the *presuppositions*.

**(ii) Donnellan (1966): referential use.** Donnellan distinguishes two *uses* of a description:

| Use | Context | Example | The description is |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Attributive** | No particular individual is in mind | "Smith's murderer is insane" (said upon finding Smith's body) | A *description* that *whoever* satisfies it |
| **Referential** | A particular individual is in mind | "Smith's murderer is insane" (said at the trial, where Jones is the obvious suspect) | A *tool* for *picking out* Jones |

**The claim.** In the *referential* use, the description *refers* to the individual *even if* the individual does not *satisfy* the description. If Jones is *innocent* but *insane*, the statement "Smith's murderer is insane" is *true* (intuitively), whereas Russell's analysis says it is *false* (because there is no unique murderer, or the murderer is not Jones).

**The objection.** Donnellan's distinction is *real*, but it is a distinction *in use*, not *in meaning*. The *semantics* of the description is *invariant*; the *referent* is *picked out* by the *context*, and the *predicate* is *attributed* to the *referent*. The *contemporary* consensus (Kripke 1977; Neale 1990) is that Donnellan's data are *consistent* with Russell's semantics, once the *pragmatics* is added.

**(iii) Kripke (1972/1980): the modal and epistemic arguments.** These are the *deepest* objections, and they are directed at *descriptivism* about *names* (§3), not at the theory of descriptions *per se*. But they have *implications* for descriptions: if names are *rigid* and descriptions are *not*, then a description *cannot* do the work that a name does. The theory of descriptions is *not* a theory of names.

### 2.5 The state of the theory

The theory of descriptions is *not* the *final* word, but it is the *starting point* of every subsequent theory. The *contemporary* consensus is a *hybrid*:

- The *semantics* is *Russellian*: descriptions are *quantifiers*.
- The *pragmatics* is *Strawsonian*: descriptions *presuppose* existence, and the *presupposition* is *defeasible*.
- The *use* is *Donnellanian*: the *referential* use is a *pragmatic* phenomenon, *consistent* with the *Russellian* semantics.
- The *names* are *Kripkean*: names are *rigid*, and they are *not* abbreviated descriptions.

The theory is the *most successful* piece of analytic philosophy because it *dissolves* a *real* puzzle (negative existentials), it *generates* a *method* (logical analysis), and it *opens* a *research programme* (the semantics of natural language).

## 3. The Modern Critique

### 3.1 Descriptivism about names

The *target* of the modern critique is **descriptivism** — the view that a *proper name* is *synonymous* with a *definite description* (or a *cluster* of descriptions).

**The view.** "Aristotle" means "the teacher of Alexander" (or some *cluster*: "the student of Plato, the teacher of Alexander, the founder of the Lyceum …").

**The two theses.**

1. **The semantic thesis**: the *meaning* of a name is a *description*.
2. **The reference-fixing thesis**: the *description* *determines* the *referent*.

**The proponents.** Frege (1892), Russell (1911, 1919), and the *cluster* theory of Searle (1958) and Strawson (1959).

### 3.2 Rigid designation

Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1972/1980) introduces the *central* concept of the modern debate.

**The definition.** A **rigid designator** is a term that *designates the same object* in *every possible world* in which it designates anything. A **non-rigid designator** *designates different objects* in *different worlds*.

**The examples.**

| Term | Type | Rigid? |
|---|---|---|
| "Aristotle" | Proper name | **Rigid** (Aristotle in every world) |
| "The teacher of Alexander" | Definite description | **Non-rigid** (different teachers in different worlds) |
| "9" | Numeral | **Rigid** |
| "The number of planets" | Definite description | **Non-rigid** (different numbers in different worlds) |
| "Water" | Natural kind term | **Rigid** (H₂O in every world) |
| "The stuff in the rivers" | Definite description | **Non-rigid** (different stuff in different worlds) |

**The claim.** Names are *rigid*; descriptions are *non-rigid*. Therefore, names are *not* synonymous with descriptions.

### 3.3 The three arguments

Kripke's argument against descriptivism has *three* parts.

**(i) The modal argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{"Aristotle might not have taught Alexander" is *true* (he could have done something else).} \\
2. & \text{"The teacher of Alexander might not have taught Alexander" is *false* (if someone taught Alexander, they taught Alexander — the description is non-rigid).} \\
3. & \text{If "Aristotle" meant "the teacher of Alexander," the two sentences would have the same truth value.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{"Aristotle" does not mean "the teacher of Alexander."}
\end{array}
$$

The argument *generalises*: for *any* description $D$ associated with the name $N$, there is a *possible world* in which $N$ does not satisfy $D$, and the *modal* truth value *diverges*.

**(ii) The epistemic argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{"Aristotle was the teacher of Alexander" is a *discovery*, not a *trivial* truth.} \\
2. & \text{If "Aristotle" meant "the teacher of Alexander," the sentence would be *analytic* (true in virtue of meaning).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{"Aristotle" does not mean "the teacher of Alexander."}
\end{array}
$$

The argument *generalises*: the *identity* between a name and its *description* is *a posteriori*, not *a priori*.

**(iii) The semantic argument.** The *Gödel/Schmidt case*.

> Suppose that Gödel did not prove the incompleteness theorem; Schmidt did. Suppose further that Gödel *stole* the proof and *published* it under his own name. On the *descriptivist* view, "Gödel" means "the prover of the incompleteness theorem," and so "Gödel" *refers* to Schmidt (the actual prover). But intuitively, "Gödel" *still refers* to Gödel (the man who *published* the proof), even though he did *not* prove it.

The argument *generalises*: *reference* is *not* determined by *description*; it is determined by *causal connection*.

### 3.4 The causal-historical picture

Kripke's *positive* view is the **causal-historical** picture of reference.

**The structure.**

1. **The initial baptism**: an object is *named* (by ostension, or by a description that is *used* to fix the reference, not to *give the meaning*).
2. **The causal chain**: the name is *transmitted* from speaker to speaker, through a *chain* of *communication*.
3. **The reference**: the name *refers* to the object at the *origin* of the chain, *regardless* of whether the *current* speaker knows any *description* that *uniquely* identifies it.

**The slogan.** Reference is *not* a matter of *what the speaker has in mind*; it is a matter of *the causal history of the name*.

**The virtue.** The picture *explains* how we can *refer* to objects we know *very little* about. I can *refer* to *Aristotle* even if the only thing I know is that he was a *Greek philosopher* — because the *name* is *connected* to him by the *chain*, not by my *knowledge*.

### 3.5 The problems

The causal-historical picture has *three* well-known problems.

**(i) The qua-problem** (Devitt & Sterelny 1999). The *initial baptism* must *pick out* the object *under a particular description* — as a *person*, as a *cat*, as a *planet*. The *causal chain* *transmits* the *reference*, but the *reference* is *not* picked out *independently* of the *sortal*. The *problem*: the causal theory *does not say* which *sortal* is the *relevant* one, and the *choice* is *substantive*.

**(ii) Reference change** (Evans 1973). The *Madagascar* case: the name *originally* referred to a *part of Africa*, but *Marco Polo* used it for the *island*, and the *island* reference *won* out. The causal chain *changed* its *reference*, and the *causal theory* *cannot explain* the *change* without adding a *descriptive* element.

**(iii) The empty-name problem.** Names that *do* not refer — *Sherlock Holmes*, *phlogiston*, *Vulcan* — *seem* to be *meaningful* and to *figure in true statements* ("Sherlock Holmes is a detective"). The causal theory *cannot explain* the *meaning* of an *empty* name without *adding* a *descriptive* element (or a *fictional* *object*: Ch. 14, §5).

### 3.6 Natural kind terms

Kripke's argument *generalises* to *natural kind terms*, and the *generalisation* is developed by Putnam (1975).

**The argument.** "Water" is a *rigid designator*: it refers to *whatever substance* has the *same* *microstructure* as the *actual* water (H₂O). The *microstructure* is *discovered* empirically, and it *fixes* the *reference*.

**The Twin Earth thought experiment.** On Twin Earth, the *stuff* that *looks* and *tastes* like water is *XYZ*, not H₂O. The *Twin Earthling's* word "water" refers to *XYZ*; our word "water" refers to *H₂O*. The *meaning* is *not* in the *head*; it is *determined* by the *external world*.

**The consequence: semantic externalism.** The *content* of a *thought* (or the *meaning* of a *term*) *depends* on the *environment*, not *only* on the *intrinsic* state of the *subject*. This is the *central* thesis of *Ch. 25, §3*, and it is the *direct* *consequence* of the *Kripke–Putnam* revolution.

## 4. Demonstratives, Indexicals, and the Essential Indexical

### 4.1 Kaplan's character and content

David Kaplan's *Demonstratives* (1977/1989) is the *foundational* work on *indexicals*.

**The distinction.**

| Concept | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Character** | The *linguistic rule* that *determines* the *content* in a *context* | "I" = the *speaker* of the *context* |
| **Content** | The *referent* (or the *proposition*) in the *context* | In *this* context, "I" = *Kaplan* |

**The formal structure.** A *context* $c$ is a *tuple* $\langle \text{agent}, \text{time}, \text{place}, \text{world} \rangle$. The *character* is a *function* from *contexts* to *contents*; the *content* is a *function* from *worlds* to *truth values*.

**The example.** "I am here now."

- **Character**: the *rule* that says the *agent* is *at* the *place* of the *context* at the *time* of the *context*.
- **Content** in *this* context: *Kaplan* is *at* *such-and-such* *place* at *such-and-such* *time*.

**The crucial point.** The *sentence* "I am here now" is *true* in *every* context (when *uttered*), but its *content* is *contingent* (it is *about* a *particular* agent, place, and time). The *necessity* is *in the character*, not in the *content*. This is the *indexical* analogue of the *contingent a priori* (Ch. 9, §3.3).

### 4.2 Perry's messy shopper

John Perry's "The Essential Indexical" (1979) is the *most* influential argument for the *irreducibility* of indexical thought.

**The story.** Perry is *following* a *trail of sugar* through a *supermarket*, *thinking* "someone is *making* a *mess*." He *does not* *stop*, because he *does not* *know* that *he* is the *someone*. When he *realizes* "I am *making* a *mess*," he *stops*.

**The argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The belief "I am making a mess" *explains* Perry's *action* (he stops).} \\
2. & \text{The belief "Perry is making a mess" *does not* explain the action (if Perry does not know he is Perry).} \\
3. & \text{The two beliefs have the *same propositional content* (on the *descriptivist* view, they are the same *fact*).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *indexical* belief is *essential*; it cannot be *reduced* to a *non-indexical* belief.}
\end{array}
$$

**The structure.** The *indexical* belief has a *content* that is *not* *captured* by *any* *non-indexical* *proposition*. The *content* is *essentially* *self-locating*.

**The consequence.** The *propositional* *attitude* is *not* a *relation* to a *proposition* (in the *ordinary* sense); it is a *relation* to a *self-locating* *content* — a *property*, or a *centered* *world*.

### 4.3 Lewis's de se

David Lewis's "Attitudes De Dicto and De Se" (1979) *formalises* Perry's insight.

**The view.** The *objects* of *belief* are *properties*, not *propositions*. The *belief* "I am making a mess" is a *belief* in the *property* of *making a mess* — a *self-locating* *property*.

**The formal structure.** A *centered* *world* is a *pair* $\langle w, a \rangle$ of a *world* and an *agent*. The *content* of an *indexical* belief is a *set* of *centered* *worlds*.

**The advantage.** The view *explains* why the *indexical* belief is *essential*: the *content* is *not* a *proposition* (a *set* of *uncentered* *worlds*), but a *set* of *centered* *worlds*. The *two* beliefs ("I am making a mess" and "Perry is making a mess") have *different* *contents*.

**The objection.** The view *multiplies* *contents*: every *belief* has a *centered* *content*, even when the *content* *seems* *uncentered* ("$2+2=4$"). The *multiplication* is *ad hoc*, and the *reply* (the *centered* *content* of "$2+2=4$" is *trivial*) is *contested*.

### 4.4 Immunity to error through misidentification

Sydney Shoemaker (1968) introduces a *distinction* that is *central* to the epistemology of self-knowledge.

**The definition.** A *judgment* "I am $F$" is **immune to error through misidentification** if it is *impossible* that the *predicate* $F$ applies to *someone*, but the *subject* *misidentifies* *who* that someone is.

**The examples.**

| Judgment | Immune? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "I feel pain" | **Yes** | If *someone* feels pain, and I *judge* "I feel pain," the *feeling* is *mine* — the *identification* cannot *fail*. |
| "I see a tree" | **No** | I *could* be *mistaken* about *who* is seeing the tree (e.g., I am *looking* at a *mirror*, and the *person* I see is *not* me). |
| "I am 6 feet tall" | **No** | I *could* be *mistaken* about *who* the *body* belongs to. |

**The argument.** The *immunity* is *not* a *feature* of the *content*; it is a *feature* of the *way* the *judgment* is *made*. The *first-person* *judgment* is *based* on a *first-person* *experience*, and the *experience* *presents* the *subject* as the *subject* of the *predicate*.

**The significance.** The *immunity* is the *epistemic* *mark* of *self-knowledge*. It is *not* *infallibility* (the *predicate* can *fail*), but it is *immunity* to a *particular* *kind* of *error*. It is the *foundation* of the *first-person* *authority* that *Ch. 26, §6* will examine.

## 5. Singular Thought and Object-Dependence

### 5.1 Russell's principle

The *successor* to the *principle of acquaintance* is **Russell's principle** (as *reconstructed* by Evans 1982):

> **Russell's principle**: A subject can have a *thought* about an *object* only if the subject *knows which object* she is thinking about.

**The structure.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Singular thought is *about* a *particular* object.} \\
2. & \text{The thought must *discriminate* the object from *all* other objects.} \\
3. & \text{The *discrimination* requires *knowledge* of *which* object it is.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Singular thought requires *knowledge* of *which*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The consequence.** A thought that *satisfies* the principle is *object-dependent*: it is *about* a *particular* object, and it is *individuated* by the object.

### 5.2 Evans and the generality constraint

Gareth Evans's *The Varieties of Reference* (1982) is the *most* influential *development* of Russell's principle.

**The generality constraint.** If a subject can think *"$a$ is $F$,"* then the subject must be able to think *"$a$ is $G$"* for a *range* of *other* *predicates* $G$.

**The argument.** A *thought* is *structured*: it has a *constituent* that *refers* to $a$, and a *constituent* that *predicates* $F$. The *structure* is *real*: the *same* *constituent* can *combine* with *different* *predicates*. If the *constituent* is *not* *reusable*, the *thought* is *not* *structured*, and it is *not* *about* $a$ in the *relevant* sense.

**The consequence.** Singular thought *requires* a *structured* *repertoire* of *concepts*. The *repertoire* is the *basis* of the *object-dependence*.

### 5.3 The acquaintance debate

The *contemporary* debate is about *how strong* the *object-dependence* is.

| Position | Claim | Proponents |
|---|---|---|
| **Strong acquaintance** | Singular thought requires *perceptual* *acquaintance* | Russell; Evans; Campbell 2002 |
| **Weak acquaintance** | Singular thought requires *some* *epistemic* *connection*, but not *perception* | Recanati 2012; Jeshion 2010 |
| **No acquaintance** | Singular thought is *widespread*; it does *not* require *acquaintance* | Hawthorne & Manley 2012; Bach 2010 |

**Hawthorne and Manley's argument** (2012). The *acquaintance* *constraint* is *too strong*: we *think* about *objects* we have *never* *perceived* (the *number* 17, the *planet* Neptune, the *person* Aristotle), and the *thoughts* are *singular*. The *constraint* *should* be *relaxed*: *any* *causal* or *informational* *connection* *suffices*.

**The objection.** The *relaxation* *blurs* the *distinction* between *singular* and *general* thought. If *any* *causal* *connection* *suffices*, then *almost every* *thought* is *singular*, and the *distinction* *loses* its *point*.

### 5.4 Mental files

François Recanati's *Mental Files* (2012) is the *most* *developed* *account* of *singular* thought.

**The view.** A *singular* thought *involves* a *mental file* — a *structured* *body* of *information* *associated* with an *object*. The *file* is *opened* when the *subject* *encounters* the *object* (by *perception*, *testimony*, or *inference*), and the *file* *accumulates* *information* about the *object*.

**The structure.**

| Element | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **File** | The *body* of *information* | The file for *Aristotle* |
| **Anchor** | The *relation* to the *object* | The *causal* *chain* from the *baptism* |
| **Content** | The *information* in the *file* | *Greek*, *philosopher*, *teacher of Alexander* |

**The advantage.** The *mental file* *explains* how *singular* thought is *both* *object-directed* *and* *information-rich*. The *file* is *about* the *object* because of the *anchor*; the *content* is the *information* in the *file*.

**The objection.** The *mental file* is a *metaphor*; the *literal* *account* of *files* in the *brain* is *not* *available*. The *metaphor* is *useful*, but it is *not* a *theory*.

### 5.5 Object-dependence and epistemic luck

The *debate* about *singular* thought is *connected* to the *debate* about *epistemic luck* (Ch. 7, §3).

**The claim.** Singular thought is *object-dependent*: if the *object* does *not* exist, the *thought* *fails* (it is *not* a *thought* *about* the *object*). The *failure* is a *form* of *epistemic luck*: the *thought* *depends* on the *existence* of the *object*, and the *existence* is *not* *guaranteed* by the *subject*'s *epistemic* *state*.

**The example.** The *phlogiston* *thought*: the *chemists* of the *eighteenth* *century* *thought* they were *thinking* about a *substance*; the *substance* *does* not *exist*; the *thought* *failed* (it was *not* *about* *anything*). The *failure* is *not* a *failure* of *reasoning*; it is a *failure* of the *world* to *cooperate*.

**The consequence.** Singular thought is *more* *fragile* than *general* thought. The *general* thought ("there is a substance that *explains* combustion") *survives* the *failure* of the *object*; the *singular* thought ("phlogiston *explains* combustion") *does* not.

## 6. Self-Knowledge and Self-Acquaintance

### 6.1 The problem

The *problem* of *self-knowledge* is the *problem* of *first-person* *authority*: how do I *know* my *own* *mental* *states*?

**The *data*.**

- I can *usually* say *what* I *believe*, *desire*, and *feel*, *without* *observation* or *inference*.
- My *avowals* (my *sincere* *statements* about my *mental* *states*) are *usually* *authoritative*: others *defer* to me.
- The *authority* is *not* *infallible* (I can be *wrong* about my *motives*, my *beliefs*, my *feelings*), but it is *real*.

**The *question*.** What *grounds* the *authority*? Is it a *form* of *acquaintance*, an *inner* *sense*, a *constitutive* *relation*, or *nothing* *special*?

### 6.2 The inner-sense model

The *classical* *model*: self-knowledge is a form of *inner* *perception*. The *subject* *observes* her *own* *mental* *states* in the *same* *way* that she *observes* *external* *objects*.

**The *argument*.** The *model* *explains* the *authority*: the *inner* *sense* is *more* *reliable* than the *outer* *senses*, because the *object* is *closer* and *less* *subject* to *interference*.

**The *objection*.** The *model* *misdescribes* the *phenomenology*. When I *know* that I *believe* $p$, I do *not* *observe* my *belief*; I *express* it. The *avowal* "I believe $p$" is *not* a *report* of an *observation*; it is an *expression* of the *belief*.

### 6.3 The transparency method

Gareth Evans (*The Varieties of Reference*, 1982, p. 225) offers the *most* *influential* *alternative*.

**The *principle*.** To *know* whether I *believe* $p$, I *look* at the *world*, not at my *mind*.

> “In making a self-ascription of belief, one’s eyes are, so to speak, or occasionally literally, directed outward — upon the world. If someone asks me ‘Do you think there is going to be a third world war?’, I must attend, in answering him, to precisely the same outward phenomena as I would attend to if I were answering the question ‘Will there be a third world war?’”

**The *structure*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & Q: \text{Do I believe } p? \\
2. & \text{I answer } Q \text{ by answering the *first-order* question } p. \\
3. & \text{If I *answer* } p \text{ *affirmatively*, I *conclude* that I *believe* } p. \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Self-knowledge is *transparent*: it is *achieved* by *looking* at the *world*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *model* *explains* the *authority* *without* *positing* a *special* *faculty*: the *authority* is the *authority* of the *first-order* *judgment*, *transferred* to the *second-order* *judgment*.

**The *objection*.** The *model* *works* for *belief* (where the *first-order* *question* is *about* the *world*), but it does *not* work for *desire* or *emotion* (where the *first-order* *question* is *about* *my* *state*, not the *world*). The *transparency* *method* is *local*, not *global*.

### 6.4 Shoemaker and the constitutive model

Sydney Shoemaker (1988, 1996) offers a *constitutive* *model*.

**The *view*.** Self-knowledge is *not* a *perception* of the *mental* *state*; it is a *constitutive* *relation*. To *believe* $p$ *is* (in part) to be *disposed* to *avow* that one *believes* $p$. The *avowal* is *constitutive* of the *belief*, not an *observation* of it.

**The *argument*.** The *immunity* to *error* *through* *misidentification* (Ch. 12, §4.4) is *explained* by the *constitutive* *relation*: the *avowal* "I believe $p$" is *not* based on an *identification* of a *subject*; it is *constitutive* of the *state* that is *avowed*.

**The *objection*.** The *model* *makes* self-knowledge *too* *easy*: if the *avowal* is *constitutive*, then *every* *sincere* *avowal* is *true*, and the *possibility* of *error* (*self-deception*, *confabulation*) is *excluded*. The *model* *needs* a *theory* of *when* the *avowal* is *constitutive* and *when* it is *not*.

### 6.5 The anti-luminosity argument

Timothy Williamson (2000, Ch. 4) argues that *no* *mental* *state* is *luminous*.

**The *definition*.** A *state* is **luminous** if, *whenever* one is *in* it, one is in a *position* to *know* one is in it.

**The *argument*.** The *argument* is a *sorites*.

1. Consider a *continuum* of *cases*: from *clearly* *feeling* *cold* to *clearly* *not* *feeling* *cold*.
2. There is a *margin* of *error*: if one is *in* the *state* at *step* $n$, one *cannot* *reliably* *discriminate* it from *step* $n+1$.
3. Therefore, *no* *step* is *luminous*: at *each* *step*, one *may* be *wrong* about *whether* one is *in* the *state*.

**The *conclusion*.** Self-knowledge is *not* *immune* to *error*; it is *subject* to the *same* *margin* *of* *error* as *external* *perception*.

**The *objection*.** The *argument* *proves* *too* *much*: it would *undermine* the *possibility* of *any* *self-knowledge*, including the *knowledge* that one is *in* *pain*. Williamson's *reply*: it *undermines* only *luminous* *knowledge*, not *all* *self-knowledge*; the *debate* is *technical*.

### 6.6 The social dimension

The *social* *turn* (Ch. 11) *complicates* the *picture*.

**The *point*.** Self-knowledge is *not* *purely* *individual*: it is *shaped* by *social* *norms*, *concepts*, and *testimony*.

**The *examples*.**

- **Emotional* *education***: I *learn* to *identify* my *emotions* through *social* *concepts* ("that is *anger*," "that is *grief*"). The *concepts* are *acquired* *socially*, and the *identification* is *shaped* by them.
- **Hermeneutical* *injustice*** (Ch. 11, §4.1): if the *concept* for my *experience* *does* not *exist*, I *cannot* *know* what I am *feeling*. The *social* *gap* is an *epistemic* *gap*.

**The consequence.** Self-knowledge is *not* a *private* *domain*; it is *socially* *mediated*, and the *mediation* is *not* *always* *benign*.

## 7. Where Russell's Framework Survives

### 7.1 The distinction

The *distinction* between *knowledge of* and *knowledge that* survives, in *modified* form.

| Russell's distinction | Successor | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge by acquaintance | *Singular thought*; *object-dependent* thought | **Live**, but *narrower* |
| Knowledge by description | *Descriptive* thought; *general* thought | **Live**, but *broader* |
| The principle of acquaintance | *Russell's principle* (Evans) | **Contested** |
| The list of the *given* | *Perceptual* *acquaintance*; *introspection* | **Narrower**; the *given* is *gone* (Ch. 5, §8.3) |

The *distinction* is *real*: there is a *difference* between *thinking* *about* an *object* and *thinking* *that* a *proposition* is *true*. The *difference* is the *subject* of the *contemporary* *debate* about *singular* thought.

### 7.2 The theory of descriptions

The *theory* of *descriptions* survives, *essentially* *intact*.

**The *core*.** Descriptions are *quantifiers*; they have *no* *meaning* in *isolation*; they have a *use* in the *context* of a *sentence*.

**The *modifications*.**
- *Presupposition* (Strawson): the *pragmatics* *matters*.
- *Referential* *use* (Donnellan): the *context* *matters*.
- *Intensional* *contexts*: the *scope* *distinctions* are *more* *complex* than Russell *thought*.

**The *status*.** The *theory* is the *starting* *point* of *every* *subsequent* *theory*; it is *not* the *final* *word*, but it is *indispensable*.

### 7.3 Structured propositions

The *Russellian* *conception* of *propositions* — *structured* *entities* with *objects* and *properties* as *constituents* — survives, and is *influential*.

**The *view*.** A *proposition* is a *structured* *entity*; the *structure* *mirrors* the *structure* of the *sentence*; the *constituents* are the *objects*, *properties*, and *relations* that the *sentence* is *about*.

**The *proponents*.** Soames (1987), Salmon (1986), King (2007).

**The *advantage*.** The *view* *explains* *compositionality*: the *meaning* of the *complex* *proposition* is a *function* of the *meanings* of the *constituents*.

**The *objection*.** The *view* *faces* *Frege's* *puzzle*: if *"Hesperus is Phosphorus"* and *"Hesperus is Hesperus"* *express* the *same* *proposition* (because *the* *constituents* are *the* *same*), then the *two* *sentences* *should* have the *same* *cognitive* *significance*. They do *not*. The *solution* (Salmon 1986) is to *add* a *level* of *"guises"* or *"modes of presentation"*; the *solution* is *contested*.

### 7.4 The principle of acquaintance, revised

The *principle* of *acquaintance* is *false*, but a *revised* version survives.

**The *revised* *principle*.** Every *singular* *thought* is *anchored* in *some* *epistemic* *connection* to the *object* — *perception*, *memory*, *testimony*, or a *causal* *chain*. The *connection* need not be *acquaintance* in Russell's *narrow* sense; it need only be *sufficient* to *determine* the *reference*.

**The *structure*.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Singular thought is *about* a particular object.} \\
2. & \text{The thought is *anchored* by an *epistemic* *connection* to the object.} \\
3. & \text{The *connection* can be *perceptual*, *memorial*, *testimonial*, or *causal*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Singular thought is *anchored*, but not *necessarily* *by* *acquaintance*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *cost*.** The *revised* *principle* is *less* *ambitious* than Russell's: it does *not* *explain* *understanding* *in* *general*, but only *singular* *understanding*. The *reduction* of *all* *knowledge* to *acquaintance* is *abandoned*.

## 8. The State of the Debate

### 8.1 What is settled

- **The theory of descriptions is the default.** The *quantificational* *analysis* is the *starting* *point* for *every* *subsequent* *theory*.
- **Names are rigid; descriptions are not.** Kripke's *argument* is *decisive*: names *cannot* be *synonymous* with *descriptions*.
- **Reference is (at least partly) causal.** The *causal-historical* *picture* is *necessary* to *explain* *reference* *transmission*, even if it is *not* *sufficient*.
- **Indexical thought is irreducible.** Perry's *argument* is *decisive*: the *essential* *indexical* *cannot* be *reduced* to *non-indexical* *propositions*.
- **Self-knowledge is not infallible.** The *anti-luminosity* *argument* *undermines* the *classical* *conception*, and the *social* *turn* *complicates* the *picture*.

### 8.2 What is open

- **The nature of singular thought.** Is it *acquaintance-based* (Evans, Campbell), *file-based* (Recanati), or *causal* (Hawthorne & Manley)?
- **The semantics of names.** Is there a *descriptive* *element* (the *cluster* theory, revised), or is *reference* *purely* *causal*?
- **The problem of empty names.** How do *empty* names *mean*? The *fictionalist* (Ch. 14, §5), *metalinguistic*, and *pretence* *accounts* are *contested*.
- **The relation between self-knowledge and self-acquaintance.** Is the *first-person* *authority* *grounded* in *acquaintance*, *constitution*, or *transparency*?
- **The social dimension of self-knowledge.** How does the *social* *mediation* *shape* *first-person* *authority*?

### 8.3 The methodological lesson

The chapter is a case study in the method of Part I.

- **Conceptual analysis** (§1 of Ch. 3) generated the *distinction* between *knowledge of* and *knowledge that*.
- **Thought experiments** (§2 of Ch. 3) — *Twin Earth*, the *Gödel/Schmidt* case, *Perry's* *shopper* — are the *engine* of the debate.
- **Formalization** (§3 of Ch. 3) — the *scope* *distinction*, *Kaplan's* *character/content* — is essential for the *semantics*.
- **Reflective equilibrium** (§4 of Ch. 3) is the method by which the *intuitions* and the *theories* are adjusted.
- **Negative capability** (§7 of Ch. 3) is the *response* to the *residue*: the *humility* to hold the *question* open.

The *honest* verdict: the *theory* of *reference* is a *field in transition*. The *Russellian* *framework* is *untenable*; the *Kripkean* *framework* is *dominant* but *incomplete*; the *contemporary* *theories* are *promising* but *contested*. The *unresolved* character is *instructive*: it shows what philosophical progress looks like — not a *settled answer*, but a *sharper understanding* of what the *question* is.

### 8.4 The dialectical map

| Position | Core claim | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Russell** | Acquaintance is the foundation of thought | *Explains* the *directness* of *knowledge* | *Too narrow*; the *principle* is *false* |
| **Descriptivism** | Names are *abbreviated* *descriptions* | *Explains* *cognitive* *significance* | *Refuted* by *Kripke* |
| **Kripke** | Names are *rigid*; reference is *causal* | *Explains* *reference* *transmission* | The *qua-problem*; *reference* *change* |
| **Kaplan** | Character *determines* content | *Formal*; *precise* | *Silent* on *cognitive* *significance* |
| **Perry** | Indexical thought is *irreducible* | *Explains* *action* | *Multiplies* *contents* |
| **Evans** | Russell's principle; *generality* *constraint* | *Explains* *structure* | *Too* *strong* |
| **Recanati** | Mental *files* | *Explains* *information* *accumulation* | The *metaphor* is *not* a *theory* |
| **Williamson** | *No* *luminous* *states* | *Honest* about *fallibility* | *Proves* *too* *much* |

The *table* is a *map*, not a *verdict*. The *reader* who wants to *choose* a *position* must decide which *cost* is *acceptable* — and the *decision* is a *philosophical* *argument*.

## Optional threads

Cross-cutting material, for the reader who wants to go deeper.

**1. The *problem* of *empty* *names*.** Names that *do* not *refer* — *Sherlock Holmes*, *phlogiston*, *Vulcan* — *seem* to be *meaningful* and to *figure* in *true* *statements*. The *accounts*: *fictionalism* (Ch. 14, §5), *pretence* (Walton 1990), *metalinguistic* (Kripke 2013), *gappy* *propositions* (Braun 1993). The *debate* is *connected* to the *theory* of *fiction* (Ch. 49, §4).

**2. The *semantics* of *belief* *reports*.** "Lois believes that Superman can fly" and "Lois believes that Clark Kent can fly" — the *substitution* *puzzles* (Frege 1892) are the *central* *test* for any *theory* of *reference*. The *solutions*: *modes of presentation* (Frege, Evans), *guises* (Salmon), *two-dimensionalism* (Chalmers), *metalinguistic* (Stalnaker). The *debate* is *unresolved*.

**3. The *epistemology* of *self-knowledge* *and* the *social* *turn*.** The *social* *mediation* of *self-knowledge* (Ch. 12, §6.6) is *connected* to *epistemic* *injustice* (Ch. 11, §4) and to the *hermeneutical* *gap*. The *political* *implications* are *serious*, and *underdeveloped*.

**4. Non-Western *theories* of *reference*.** The *Indian* *grammarian* *tradition* (Patañjali, Bhartṛhari) *developed* a *theory* of *meaning* and *reference* that *anticipates* many of the *contemporary* *debates*. The *Buddhist* *apoha* *theory* (Dignāga) *holds* that *words* *refer* *not* to *positive* *entities* but to the *exclusion* of *others* — a *form* of *nominalism* that is *relevant* to the *debate* about *natural* *kinds* (Ch. 15, §2). These are *systematic* *alternatives*, not *curiosities*.

**5. The *metaphysics* of *propositions*.** The *Russellian* *theory* *of* *structured* *propositions* is *one* *option*; the *Fregean* *theory* (propositions as *senses*), the *possible-worlds* *theory* (propositions as *sets* of *worlds*), and the *deflationary* *theory* (propositions as *mere* *abstractions* from *sentences*) are *others*. The *debate* is *connected* to the *theory* of *truth* (Ch. 32) and to the *theory* of *modality* (Ch. 16).

## Summary

- **Russell's distinction** between *knowledge of* and *knowledge that* is *real*, and it is the *ancestor* of the *contemporary* *debate* about *singular* *thought*.
- **The principle of acquaintance** — that every proposition we *understand* is *composed* of *constituents* we are *acquainted* with — is *false*. The *social* *turn* (Ch. 11) and the *causal* *theory* of *reference* *undermine* it.
- **The theory of descriptions** is the *most* *successful* *piece* of *analytic* *philosophy*. It *dissolves* the *problem* of *negative* *existentials*, it *introduces* the *scope* *distinction*, and it *generates* the *method* of *logical* *analysis*.
- **The modern critique** — *Strawson* (presupposition), *Donnellan* (referential use), *Kripke* (rigid designation) — *undermines* the *descriptivist* *theory* of *names*. The *causal-historical* *picture* is the *successor*, and it *faces* the *qua-problem*, *reference* *change*, and *empty* *names*.
- **Indexical thought** is *irreducible*: *Perry's* *messy* *shopper* *shows* that the *essential* *indexical* *cannot* be *reduced* to *non-indexical* *propositions*. *Kaplan's* *character/content* *distinction* and *Lewis's* *de* *se* *attitudes* are the *formal* *machinery*.
- **Singular thought** is *object-dependent*: it is *about* a *particular* *object*, and it is *anchored* by an *epistemic* *connection*. The *debate* is about *how* *strong* the *connection* must be (Evans, Recanati, Hawthorne & Manley).
- **Self-knowledge** is *not* *infallible*: the *anti-luminosity* *argument* *undermines* the *classical* *conception*, and the *social* *turn* *complicates* the *picture*. The *transparency* *method* (Evans) and the *constitutive* *model* (Shoemaker) are the *main* *alternatives*.
- **Where Russell survives**: the *distinction*, the *theory of descriptions*, the *structured* *propositions*, and a *revised* *principle* of *acquaintance*. **Where it must be rebuilt**: the *principle* itself, the *narrow* *list* of the *given*, and the *individualist* *conception* of *thought*.

> *A closing note.* This chapter has followed Russell's *distinction* between *knowledge of* and *knowledge that* through the *century* of *critique* that *dismantled* the *principle* of *acquaintance* while *preserving* the *distinction* that the *principle* was *meant* to *explain*. The result is *not* a *refutation* of *Russell*, and it is *not* a *vindication* of the *causal* *theory*. It is a *narrowing*: the *direct* *relation* to the *object* is *real*, but it is *not* *acquaintance* in the *classical* *sense*; the *thought* is *anchored* in the *object*, but the *anchor* is *causal*, *testimonial*, and *social*, not *perceptual*.

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter turns to the *criterion* problem — the *meta*-problem of *method* that the *first* part of the book *previewed* (Ch. 1, §5; Ch. 4, §3.6; Ch. 6, §8.5). If the *sources* of *knowledge* are *plural* and *fallible*, how do we *choose* among them? The *question* is the *epistemological* *form* of the *problem* of *reference*: just as the *thought* is *anchored* in the *object* by a *chain* that the *subject* does not *fully* *control*, so the *inquiry* is *anchored* in the *world* by a *practice* that the *inquirer* does not *fully* *justify*. The *next* *chapter* *examines* the *criterion*, the *relativism* that *threatens* it, and the *objectivity* that *survives*.
