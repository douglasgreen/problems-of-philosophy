# Chapter 6 — The Existence of the External World: Skepticism Ancient and Modern

> “I shall suppose … that some evil demon, extremely powerful and cunning, has employed all his energies in deceiving me.”
> — Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*, I (1641)

> “If you tried to doubt everything you would not get as far as doubting anything. The game of doubting itself presupposes certainty.”
> — Wittgenstein, *On Certainty*, §115

---

Chapter 5 followed the table into the theory of perception. This chapter follows it into the abyss. If what we are immediately aware of is not the table but an appearance, a sense-datum, or a representation, then the question arises: **how do we know that there is a table at all?** Russell, having argued in Chapter I that the table is known only by inference, spends Chapters II–III explaining why the inference is worth making. The explanation is not altogether convincing — Russell himself admits as much — and the twentieth century has not improved its quality.

The problem of the external world is the oldest problem in epistemology, and it is the one most likely to be dismissed by students as a game. That reaction is worth taking seriously. The problem *looks* like a game because it is generated by a move that ordinary thought never makes — the move of stepping outside one’s beliefs and asking, all at once, whether they are true. The sceptic’s question is not “is this particular belief justified?” but “is the whole framework of beliefs justified?” And that question, as we shall see, may be unanswerable in the form in which it is asked — which is not the same as being unanswerable, and not the same as being a pseudo-question.

We proceed in seven movements. §§1–2 state the problem and give Descartes’ formulation of it. §3 recovers the ancient sources, which are more subtle than the Cartesian version and which provide the vocabulary the modern debate still uses. §4 examines the brain-in-a-vat scenario and Putnam’s semantic argument against it. §5 sets out the epistemic machinery — closure, relevant alternatives, sensitivity, safety — that has structured the debate since 1970. §6 surveys the responses. §7 examines the new scepticisms of the digital age. §8 asks what progress in this area could possibly look like, and gives an honest verdict.

## 1. The Shape of the Problem

### 1.1 Varieties of scepticism

“Scepticism” names a family of positions, not a single one. The taxonomy matters because different responses are appropriate to different members.

| Variety | Target | Claim | Canonical source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Academic scepticism** | The possibility of knowledge | Nothing can be known (or: nothing can be known with certainty) | Arcesilaus, Carneades; Cicero, *Academica* |
| **Pyrrhonian scepticism** | The possibility of rational belief | For every argument there is an equal and opposite argument; suspension of judgment follows | Pyrrho; Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* |
| **Cartesian scepticism** | The external world | The senses may be systematically deceived; no belief about the world is indubitable | Descartes, *Meditations* I |
| **Humean scepticism** | Induction and causation | Reason cannot justify the inference from observed to unobserved | Hume, *Enquiry* VI–VII, XII |
| **Semantic scepticism** | Meaning and rule-following | There is no fact of the matter about what a rule requires | Kripke, *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982) |
| **Moral scepticism** | Moral facts | There are no moral facts (error theory) | Mackie, *Ethics* (1977) |
| **External-world scepticism** | Knowledge of the external world | We do not know (or cannot know) that there is a world outside our minds | The focus of this chapter |

The subject of this chapter is the last of these, but its arguments draw on the first three. The **global** sceptic denies that we know anything; the **local** external-world sceptic denies only that we know about the world. The distinction matters because the global sceptic faces an obvious self-refutation problem (§3.3) that the local sceptic does not.

A further distinction, equally important: **scepticism as a thesis** versus **scepticism as a practice**. The thesis is a claim about the limits of knowledge. The practice is a way of inquiring — the deliberate suspension of assent, the refusal to accept a proposition until the case for it has been made. The two are connected but not identical: one can practise scepticism without holding the sceptical thesis (Descartes), and one can hold the thesis without practising the corresponding discipline (many a dogmatic sceptic).

### 1.2 The sceptical argument

The modern debate has a canonical structure, which we can state with the apparatus of Chapter 2.

Let $H$ = “I have hands.” Let $\text{BIV}$ = “I am a brain in a vat, being fed simulated experiences by a computer.” The sceptic’s argument relies on the **closure principle** for knowledge:

$$
(\text{Closure}) \quad [K(p) \wedge K(p \to q)] \to K(q)
$$

In words: if you know that $p$, and you know that $p$ entails $q$, then you know that $q$.

The sceptical argument then runs:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \neg K(\neg \text{BIV}) \quad \text{(I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat.)} \\
2. & K(H \to \neg \text{BIV}) \quad \text{(I know that having hands entails not being a brain in a vat.)} \\
3. & K(H) \to K(\neg \text{BIV}) \quad \text{(by Closure)} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \neg K(H) \quad \text{(I do not know that I have hands.)}
\end{array}
$$

Premise 1 is supposed to be self-evident: the BIV scenario is constructed so as to be experientially indistinguishable from the real world, so no evidence available to me could rule it out. Premise 2 is a conceptual truth: brains in vats do not have hands (they have no bodies). The inference is an instance of *modus tollens* on the closure principle. The conclusion generalises: since $H$ was arbitrary, no belief about the external world is knowledge.

The argument is valid. The philosophical work, as always, is in the premises — and, in this case, in the closure principle itself.

### 1.3 The sceptical paradox

There is a second, more revealing way to state the problem. DeRose (1995) formulates it as a paradox: three principles, each highly plausible, which cannot all be true.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
(\text{A}) & \text{I know that I have hands.} \\
(\text{B}) & \text{I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat.} \\
(\text{C}) & \text{If I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat, then I do not know that I have hands.}
\end{array}
$$

(A) is the deliverance of common sense. (B) is the deliverance of the sceptical scenario. (C) is the closure principle, contraposed. Any two are compatible; all three are not. The entire debate, from Moore to the present, is a dispute about which one to give up.

This is a useful formulation because it makes the **cost** of each response explicit. Every response to scepticism is a response to the paradox, and every response pays a price.

| Response | Principle denied | Price |
|---|---|---|
| **Mooreanism** | (B) | Implausible confidence; seems to beg the question |
| **Scepticism** | (A) | Denies common sense; makes knowledge vanish |
| **Deny closure** | (C) | Gives up a principle that seems constitutive of rationality |
| **Contextualism** | None (relativised) | Requires a semantics of “knows” that many find ad hoc |
| **Hinge epistemology** | None (bracketed) | Requires a distinction between knowledge and framework certainty |
| **Externalism** | (B) | Requires an externalist account of knowledge (Ch. 8, §§4–5) |
| **Disjunctivism** | (B) | Requires a disjunctivist account of perceptual evidence (Ch. 5, §6) |

The table is the map of the chapter. We shall visit each row.

## 2. Descartes: The Three Waves of Doubt

### 2.1 The methodological setting

Descartes’ doubt is not the doubt of the sceptic. The sceptic doubts in order to achieve tranquillity (Pyrrho) or to refute the dogmatists (Arcesilaus). Descartes doubts in order to **find a foundation**. The *Meditations* are a work of construction, and the doubt is the demolition that precedes it. This is why Descartes is often called the first modern philosopher: he makes the *subject* — the thinking thing — the starting point, and he makes *certainty* the criterion of knowledge.

The structure of the First Meditation is a sequence of three waves, each more radical than the last. Each wave is designed to undermine a class of beliefs that survived the previous wave.

### 2.2 First wave: the senses

> “Whatever I have up till now accepted as most true I have acquired either from the senses or through the senses. But from time to time I have found that the senses deceive, and it is prudent never to trust completely those who have deceived us even once.” (*Med.* I)

The first wave is the argument from **sensory error**. The senses sometimes deceive — the stick looks bent, the square tower looks round; the mirage in the desert looks like water. Therefore the senses are not entirely trustworthy.

The wave is weak, and Descartes knows it. It undermines only beliefs about **small or distant things**, not beliefs about the immediate environment. “That I am here, sitting by the fire, wearing a dressing gown” — the sceptic’s first wave does not touch this. So Descartes needs a second wave.

### 2.3 Second wave: the dream

> “I see so plainly that there are no definitive signs by which to distinguish being awake from being asleep.” (*Med.* I)

The dream argument is stronger. It does not rely on occasional error; it relies on the possibility of **systematic** error. Dreams can be vivid, coherent, and indistinguishable from waking experience. If I cannot rule out, at any given moment, that I am dreaming, then I cannot know that any given experience is veridical.

The argument’s structure:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{For all I know, I may be dreaming now.} \\
2. & \text{If I may be dreaming, then my perceptual beliefs are not certain.} \\
3. & \text{Knowledge requires certainty (Descartes’ assumption).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{My perceptual beliefs are not knowledge.}
\end{array}
$$

**Assessment.** The argument is valid *given Descartes’ conception of knowledge as certainty*. Premise 1 is the crux. Two observations:

- The premise is **epistemic**, not metaphysical. It says: I have no way of telling. It does not say: I am dreaming. The gap between “I cannot rule it out” and “it might be true” is the gap that the entire subsequent debate tries to bridge.
- The premise is **self-limiting**. Even if I am dreaming, some truths survive: the dream has colours, shapes, numbers, extension. “For whether I am awake or asleep, two and three added together are five, and a square has no more than four sides” (*Med.* I). The dream argument leaves the **simple natures** intact, which is why Descartes needs a third wave.

### 2.4 Third wave: the evil demon

> “I shall suppose … that some evil demon, extremely powerful and cunning, has employed all his energies in deceiving me.” (*Med.* I)

The third wave is the most radical, and it is the one that generates the modern problem. The demon can deceive me about *everything*, including the simple natures: perhaps $2+3$ only *seems* to be $5$; perhaps the domain of mathematics is an illusion woven by the deceiver.

The demon is the ancestor of the **brain in a vat**, the **simulation**, and the **Matrix**. Its logical role is precise: it is designed to be a hypothesis under which *all* my experiences are as they are, but none of them is veridical. The demon’s existence is not asserted — it is *entertained as a possibility*, and the mere possibility is sufficient to undermine certainty.

### 2.5 The *cogito* and the circle

The First Meditation ends in universal doubt. The Second begins with the escape:

> “I have convinced myself that there is absolutely nothing in the world, no sky, no earth, no minds, no bodies. Does it now follow that I too do not exist? No: if I convinced myself of something then I certainly existed. … So after considering everything very thoroughly, I must finally conclude that this proposition, *I am, I exist*, is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by me or conceived in my mind.” (*Med.* II)

The *cogito* — “I think, therefore I am” — is the foundation. But it is a narrow foundation: it establishes only that I exist *as a thinking thing*, at the moment I think. It does not establish that I have a body, that there is a world, or that my thoughts are true.

The road from the *cogito* to the external world runs through **God**. Descartes argues that the idea of a perfect being could not have been produced by an imperfect being like himself, so God exists; that a perfect being would not deceive; and therefore that whatever I clearly and distinctly perceive is true. The argument is the archetype of modern foundationalism, and it is also its most famous failure. The **Cartesian circle** is the objection: the proof of God’s existence relies on the reliability of clear and distinct perception, which is precisely what the proof is supposed to establish. Descartes’ replies — that even a deceiver cannot falsify what is *present to the mind* at the time of perception, as against what is merely remembered — have never fully satisfied critics.

The circle matters for this chapter because it shows the **structural temptation** of the foundationalist response to scepticism: to find a premise that the sceptic cannot doubt, and to build the world on it. The history of epistemology since Descartes is, in large part, a history of attempts to find a better foundation — and of the recognition that the foundation is never quite as secure as it needs to be (Ch. 8, §2).

## 3. The Ancient Sources

### 3.1 Why the ancients matter

The modern debate tends to treat scepticism as a problem about *evidence*: how can my evidence for the external world be sufficient? The ancient debate treated it as a problem about **argument**: for every argument there is an equal and opposite argument, and the rational response is suspension of judgment (*epochē*).

The difference is important, because the ancient tradition contains resources that the modern one has largely forgotten — and because the ancient sceptics were, in a sense, *practitioners* of a form of life, not merely theorists of a limit.

### 3.2 Pyrrho and Sextus

**Pyrrho of Elis** (c. 360–270 BCE) founded the school; **Sextus Empiricus** (c. 160–210 CE) is our main source. The *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* presents scepticism as a **discipline** with a therapeutic aim: to cure the dogmatist of the disease of rash assent, and thereby to achieve *ataraxia* — tranquillity.

The core argumentative device is the **modes** (*tropoi*). Sextus records two sets.

**The Ten Modes of Aenesidemus** (PH I.36–163) are arguments from the variability of perception:

1. **Differences in animals**: what is pleasant to a dog is not pleasant to a human.
2. **Differences in humans**: different constitutions perceive differently.
3. **Differences in the senses**: honey looks different from how it tastes.
4. **Differences in circumstances**: the same object looks different in different conditions.
5. **Differences in position, distance, place**: the tower looks round from afar, square from near.
6. **Differences in admixture**: the same thing appears different when mixed with others.
7. **Differences in quantity and constitution**: a small amount of wine strengthens, a large amount weakens.
8. **Relativity**: all things are relative to the perceiver and the conditions.
9. **Differences in frequency**: the same thing can be familiar or novel.
10. **Differences in custom, law, myth**: different cultures have different beliefs about the same thing.

The conclusion of each mode is **equipollence** (*isostheneia*): the evidence for the appearance is balanced by the evidence against it, so judgment must be suspended.

**The Five Modes of Agrippa** (PH I.164–169) are more formal, and they are the ones that matter for the modern debate:

1. **Regress**: every proof requires a further proof; the regress is infinite.
2. **Hypothesis**: at some point, a premise must be assumed without proof.
3. **Reciprocity (circularity)**: the proof of $p$ depends on $q$, and the proof of $q$ depends on $p$.
4. **The criterion**: how do we decide which criterion of truth to use, without presupposing a criterion? (Ch. 13, §1)
5. **Relativity**: everything is relative to the perceiver and the conditions.

Agrippa’s modes are the **trilemma** of justification, stated 1,800 years before the modern debate (Ch. 8, §1). The sceptic’s conclusion is that every attempt to justify a belief either (i) regresses infinitely, (ii) assumes a premise dogmatically, or (iii) argues in a circle. The **Pyrrhonian** conclusion is suspension; the **modern** conclusion is that justification is *defeasible*, *context-dependent*, or *external* — responses that the sceptic anticipates and argues against.

### 3.3 The self-refutation objection

The ancient sceptic’s position invites an obvious objection: **is the sceptical thesis itself a piece of knowledge?** If the sceptic claims to *know* that nothing can be known, she is caught in a performative contradiction. If she claims only to *believe* it, then the belief is either unjustified (by her own standards) or, if justified, then something can be known.

The Pyrrhonian response is subtle and instructive. Sextus distinguishes between the **dogmatic** sceptic (who asserts a negative doctrine) and the **Pyrrhonian** (who does not). The Pyrrhonian’s scepticism is not a doctrine but a **capacity** — an ability to produce arguments on both sides. The sceptic’s own utterances are not assertions but **reports** of her state, like a patient describing a symptom. The sceptic does not say “nothing can be known”; she says “it seems to me that I cannot decide.” The move is not entirely satisfactory (it threatens to make scepticism ineffable), but it is clever, and it anticipates the quietist responses of §6.5.

The **modern** point is the one to take away: the self-refutation objection is a *local* weapon. It refutes the *dogmatic* sceptic. It does not refute the *methodological* sceptic (Descartes), the *therapeutic* sceptic (Sextus), or the *practical* sceptic (the person who simply withholds assent). The modern debate is largely about whether there is a form of scepticism that neither asserts nor denies the possibility of knowledge, and that is nonetheless intellectually serious.

## 4. The Brain in a Vat and Putnam’s Semantic Argument

### 4.1 The scenario

The BIV scenario is the modern heir to the evil demon. A brain is kept alive in a vat of nutrients, connected to a computer that feeds it electrical impulses indistinguishable from the impulses it would receive if it were in a body. The brain’s experiences are exactly like ours. The brain is *not* in a body; it does *not* have hands; it is *not* on Earth.

The scenario is not offered as a serious hypothesis. It is offered as a **possibility** — a way of making vivid the claim that our evidence underdetermines our beliefs. If the BIV scenario is possible, then our evidence is consistent with a world in which we are brains in vats, and our ordinary beliefs are not certain.

### 4.2 Putnam’s argument

Hilary Putnam, in *Reason, Truth and History* (1981), Ch. 1 — one of the most celebrated arguments in twentieth-century philosophy — argues that the BIV hypothesis is **self-refuting**.

The argument rests on a **causal-informational theory of reference**: a term refers to whatever is (causally, informationally) responsible for the competent speaker’s use of the term. Putnam’s famous example: an ant walking in the sand traces a line that looks like a caricature of Churchill. The line *represents* Churchill only if it was produced in the right kind of way; the ant’s trace does not represent anyone (Putnam 1981, p. 1).

Now consider the BIV’s word “vat.” The BIV has never seen a vat. Its word “vat” is produced by images of vats — images generated by the computer. On the causal theory, the BIV’s “vat” does not refer to vats; it refers to *vat-images*, or to the computational states that produce them. Similarly, its word “brain” refers, not to brains, but to the images of brains in the simulation.

So when the BIV utters the sentence “I am a brain in a vat,” what it means is something like: “I am an image of a brain in an image of a vat.” That sentence is false, or at least it does not express the sceptical hypothesis. The BIV cannot *say* that it is a brain in a vat, because its words do not have the right referents.

The argument:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Either I am a BIV or I am not.} \\
2. & \text{If I am not a BIV, then “I am a BIV” is false.} \\
3. & \text{If I am a BIV, then my word “vat” refers to vat-images, and “I am a BIV” is false.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{“I am a BIV” is false in either case.}
\end{array}
$$

The conclusion is strong: it is not that we *know* we are not BIVs; it is that the sentence “we are BIVs” *cannot be true*. The hypothesis is, in Putnam’s phrase, self-refuting.

### 4.3 Assessment

Putnam’s argument has been enormously influential, and it has been subjected to four lines of objection and one general limitation.

**(i) The dependence on externalism.** The argument requires the causal theory of reference, which is a contested semantic thesis (Ch. 12, §3; Ch. 31, §2). If reference is determined by *description* rather than by causal connection, then the BIV’s word “vat” could refer to the vat-in-the-computer’s-program, or to the abstract type *vat*, and the argument collapses. Putnam is aware of this: the argument is a *conditional* — if externalism is true, then the BIV hypothesis is self-refuting. But the conditional is not nothing: it shows that the BIV scenario is not semantically innocent.

**(ii) The “unsayable” objection.** The argument shows at most that we cannot *say* (or *think*) that we are BIVs. It does not show that we are not. A BIV could be a BIV while being unable to formulate the hypothesis. This is the most serious objection, and it is instructive: it shows that the argument is *transcendental* in form (an argument from the conditions of the possibility of meaning) rather than *empirical*. It establishes a **limit on what can be meaningfully said**, not a **fact about the world**.

**(iii) The reductionism objection.** The BIV’s word “brain” — on Putnam’s view — refers to brain-images. But the vat contains a *real* brain, and the real brain is causally responsible for the BIV’s experiences (via the computer). Why does the causal chain not run from the real brain to the BIV’s word? Putnam’s answer: the causal connection is not of the right kind — it is mediated by the computer in a way that breaks the reference relation. This answer is contested (see, e.g., the discussion in Chalmers 1996, Ch. 5, “The argument from reference”; and the literature on “deviant causal chains,” Ch. 8, §4).

**(iv) The scope limitation.** Even if Putnam’s argument works, it defuses only *one* sceptical scenario. The dream, the demon, and the simulation are not addressed, because they do not (necessarily) involve the systematic illusion of *reference* that the BIV scenario does. The dream argument, in particular, is untouched: the dreamer’s words refer normally (she is asleep in a real bed, and her words are about the real world), but her *experiences* are not veridical. Putnam’s argument shows that the BIV cannot *mean* what it seems to mean; it does not show that we are not dreaming.

The consensus is that Putnam’s argument is a **partial** victory: it shows that one powerful formulation of scepticism is semantically unstable, but it does not refute external-world scepticism in general. What it does do — and this is its enduring contribution — is to force the debate to be **semantic** as well as **epistemic**. The question “could I be a BIV?” is not only a question about evidence; it is a question about what “I” and “vat” and “brain” mean.

## 5. The Epistemic Machinery

### 5.1 Closure

The **closure principle** was stated in §1.2. It descends from a long tradition (Moore, Wittgenstein, Dretske, Nozick), and its precise formulation is contested. Three versions:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
(\text{C}_1) & [K(p) \wedge K(p \to q)] \to K(q) \quad \text{(simple closure)} \\
(\text{C}_2) & [K(p) \wedge \text{competently deduces } q \text{ from } p \text{ and believes } q] \to K(q) \quad \text{(competent deduction)} \\
(\text{C}_3) & \text{If } S \text{ knows that } p, \text{ and } p \text{ entails } q, \text{ then } S \text{ is in a position to know that } q \quad \text{(knowledge-closure)}
\end{array}
$$

Closure is attractive because it seems constitutive of rationality: if you know $p$, and you know that $p$ requires $q$, how can you fail to know $q$? But it is also the principle that generates the sceptical argument, and its denial is the price of several responses (§6).

### 5.2 Relevant alternatives

**Dretske** (1970) proposed a way to keep closure while blocking the sceptical inference. The idea: knowledge is relative to a set of **relevant alternatives**. To know that $H$ (that I have hands), I must be able to rule out the *relevant* alternatives to $H$ — that I have no hands, that my hands are prosthetic, that I am hallucinating. I need not be able to rule out *irrelevant* alternatives — that I am a BIV, that I am dreaming, that I am a butterfly dreaming I am a man.

The view is intuitive: in ordinary contexts, the BIV scenario is not a relevant alternative, so the inability to rule it out does not undermine knowledge. In philosophical contexts, it becomes relevant, and knowledge is lost. (This is the seed of contextualism, §6.4.)

The problem: the criterion of relevance. If the BIV scenario is not relevant, what makes it irrelevant? Dretske’s answer — that it is not a *perceptual* alternative, or not a *practical* alternative — has been criticised as ad hoc. And the view has the consequence that closure fails for knowledge (since one can know $H$ without knowing $\neg \text{BIV}$), which many find unacceptable.

### 5.3 Sensitivity

**Nozick** (1981) proposed a tracking account of knowledge that is designed to yield *some* knowledge while conceding the sceptical result about the BIV:

$$
S \text{ knows } p \text{ iff } (i)\ p \text{ is true}; (ii)\ S \text{ believes } p; (iii)\ \text{if } p \text{ were false, } S \text{ would not believe } p; (iv)\ \text{if } p \text{ were true, } S \text{ would believe } p.
$$

Clause (iii) is **sensitivity**: the belief tracks the truth. Nozick’s insight: the belief “I have hands” is sensitive — if I had no hands, I would (in the nearest world) know it and not believe I had hands. The belief “I am not a BIV” is *not* sensitive — if I were a BIV, I would still believe I was not. So Nozick’s account yields:

- $K(H)$ — I know I have hands (sensitivity holds).
- $\neg K(\neg \text{BIV})$ — I do not know I am not a BIV (sensitivity fails).
- Closure fails — because $H \to \neg \text{BIV}$, and I know $H$ but not $\neg \text{BIV}$.

The account gives the sceptic the victory on the philosophical question while preserving ordinary knowledge. It is an elegant position, and it has been widely discussed.

**Objections.** (i) **The proximity problem**: sensitivity is a counterfactual, and the evaluation of counterfactuals requires a similarity ordering on worlds (Ch. 37, §3), which is itself contested. (ii) **The method problem**: sensitivity is sensitive to *how* the belief is held (Nozick’s “method” relativisation), and the account of methods is underdeveloped. (iii) **The conjunction problem**: sensitivity fails for conjunctions of sensitive beliefs, which is counterintuitive. (iv) **The sceptical residue**: it is not clear that the position is stable — if I cannot know I am not a BIV, can I really know I have hands? (This is the intuition behind closure.)

### 5.4 Safety

**Sosa** (1999, 2007) and **Pritchard** (2005, 2012) propose **safety** as the anti-luck condition:

$$
S \text{'s belief that } p \text{ is safe iff, in all (or most) nearby worlds in which } S \text{ believes } p, \ p \text{ is true.}
$$

Safety is a *modal* condition: it is not about what would happen if $p$ were false, but about what happens in worlds *near* the actual world. The key question for scepticism is whether the BIV world is *near*.

- If the BIV world is near, then “I have hands” is unsafe (in nearby worlds I believe I have hands and I do not), and scepticism follows.
- If the BIV world is far, then “I have hands” is safe, and knowledge is preserved.

Pritchard’s **safety-based** response to scepticism is subtle, and it turns on which of the two beliefs is safe. His view: the belief “I have hands” is **safe** (in nearby worlds where I believe I have hands, I do have them), but the belief “I am not a BIV” is **unsafe** (in nearby worlds where I believe I am not a BIV, I am — because the BIV world is near enough). The distance of the BIV world is therefore assessed relative to each belief, and the two assessments come apart. Pritchard then argues that closure fails: knowledge is not closed under known entailment, because the safety condition is not preserved under entailment. This is the **“safety-based” denial of closure**, and it is one of the most discussed positions in the contemporary literature.

The objection to Pritchard is the same as the objection to Nozick: the denial of closure is counterintuitive, and the claim that the BIV world is “near” (for the purposes of safety) is difficult to justify without circularity.

### 5.5 The dialectical situation

The machinery of §5 has not resolved the debate, but it has clarified it. The question is no longer “can we refute the sceptic?” but “which of the three principles of the paradox (§1.3) should we give up, and at what cost?” The answer depends on a substantive theory of knowledge — and that theory is the subject of Chapter 7.

## 6. The Responses

### 6.1 Moore’s proof

G. E. Moore, in “A Defence of Common Sense” (1925) and “Proof of an External World” (1939), offered the most direct response to the sceptic:

> “I can prove now, for instance, that two human hands exist. How? By holding up my two hands, and saying, as I make a certain gesture with the right hand, ‘Here is one hand,’ and adding, as I make a certain gesture with the left, ‘and here is another.’” (Moore 1939, p. 146)

The argument:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Here is one hand.} \\
2. & \text{Here is another.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{Two hands exist.} \\
4. & \therefore \text{An external world exists.}
\end{array}
$$

Moore’s point is that the premises are more certain than any philosophical argument against them. If the sceptic’s argument implies that I do not know that I have hands, then the sceptic’s argument is unsound, because I *do* know that I have hands — with a certainty greater than any premise of the sceptical argument.

**Assessment.** Moore’s proof is either a triumphant refutation or a spectacular *petitio principii*, depending on one’s view of the dialectic.

- **The charge of begging the question**: the sceptic’s whole point is that the premise “here is one hand” is exactly what is in doubt. Moore asserts it without argument. The proof therefore assumes what it is supposed to prove, and is useless against the sceptic.
- **Moore’s reply**: the charge of begging the question presupposes that the sceptic’s doubt is legitimate. But Moore’s claim is that the *certainty* of “here is one hand” is greater than the certainty of any premise in the sceptical argument. In a conflict of certainties, the more certain wins. The proof is not question-begging; it is a *refusal to accept the sceptic’s framework*, which is itself a substantive epistemic stance.

**Wittgenstein’s response** (in *On Certainty*) is the most interesting development of Moore’s position. Wittgenstein agrees with Moore that “here is one hand” is certain, but he denies that it is *knowledge* in the ordinary sense. It is a **hinge** — a proposition that is exempt from doubt because it is the framework within which doubt and inquiry take place. (See §6.5.)

### 6.2 Ordinary-language dissolution

The ordinary-language response, associated with Austin (1946, 1962) and the later Wittgenstein, is not a refutation of the sceptic but a **diagnosis** of the sceptical question.

Austin’s *Sense and Sensibilia* (1962) argues that the argument from illusion trades on a systematic misuse of the vocabulary of perception. The words “looks,” “seems,” “appears,” “is” encode distinctions that the sceptic ignores. When we say “the stick looks bent,” we are not reporting a private experience; we are making a claim about how the stick *appears in certain conditions*, and the claim is assessable by ordinary standards. The sceptic’s move — from “it looks bent” to “I am directly aware of a bent sense-datum” — is a **philosophical** inference, not a description of the phenomenology, and it is the inference that needs justification.

The dissolution: the sceptical question (“how do you know there is an external world?”) is not a question that arises within ordinary practice. It is generated by a philosophical theory (the theory of ideas, or the sense-datum theory) that is itself unsupported. Once the theory is abandoned, the question dissolves. (This is the technique of Ch. 3, §7.)

**Assessment.** The dissolution is powerful against the *classical* formulation of scepticism, and it has the virtue of explaining why the question seems compelling. But it has two weaknesses. First, it is not clear that the dissolution is *stable*: the sceptic can re-ask the question in ordinary language (“how do you know that the stick is really straight, and not just that it looks straight?”), and the ordinary-language response has no further resources. Second, the dissolution is a form of **quietism** (§6.5), and quietism has its own costs: it gives up the project of answering the sceptic, and it is not clear that giving up is a form of progress.

### 6.3 Epistemic externalism

**Externalism** is the view that the justification of a belief need not be accessible to the believer (Ch. 8, §§4–5). **Reliabilism** is the most influential externalist theory: a belief is justified if it is produced by a reliable process (Goldman 1979).

The externalist response to scepticism is direct:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Our perceptual beliefs are produced by a reliable process (perception).} \\
2. & \text{Reliable processes produce knowledge.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{Our perceptual beliefs are (mostly) knowledge.}
\end{array}
$$

And against the sceptical premise “I do not know that I am not a BIV”: the externalist says that I *do* know it, because my belief that I am not a BIV is produced by the same reliable process (perception, memory, testimony) that produces my ordinary beliefs. The BIV’s belief is not knowledge, because the BIV’s process is unreliable. The asymmetry between me and the BIV is exactly what the externalist needs.

**Objections.** (i) **The question-begging charge**: the externalist assumes that our processes are reliable, which is precisely what the sceptic denies. (ii) **The “conditional” problem**: externalism gives a *conditional* response — if we are not BIVs, we know; if we are, we do not. It does not establish *that* we are not BIVs. (iii) **The “new evil demon” problem** (Cohen 1984): a subject in a demon world has exactly the same internal states as a subject in the real world; if justification is external, the demon-world subject is unjustified, which is counterintuitive. (This is the strongest objection, and it is the subject of Ch. 8, §5.)

The externalist response is not a refutation of scepticism; it is a **reconceptualisation** of the problem. The sceptic asks for a justification that is accessible from the first-person perspective. The externalist says that knowledge does not require such a justification. Whether this is a solution or a change of subject is the central question of the debate.

### 6.4 Contextualism

**Contextualism** is the view that the truth conditions of knowledge attributions (“S knows that $p$”) are sensitive to the context of the attributor. In ordinary contexts, the standards are low; in philosophical contexts, they are high. The sceptic is right *in her context*; we are right in ours.

The canonical statements are Cohen (1988), DeRose (1995), and Lewis (1996). Lewis’s version is particularly elegant: the context determines which possibilities are **relevant** (the “rule of attention” — a possibility that is attended to is *ipso facto* relevant), and knowledge requires the elimination of all relevant possibilities. In ordinary conversation, the BIV scenario is not attended to, so it is not relevant, and we know. In the philosophy seminar, it is attended to, so it becomes relevant, and we do not know.

The **sceptical paradox** is solved by relativising (A) and (B) to different contexts:

| Context | (A) I know I have hands | (B) I don’t know I’m not a BIV |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary | True | (not evaluated; not relevant) |
| Philosophical | False | True |

**Objections.** (i) **The “ad hoc” charge**: the contextualist posits a context-sensitivity in “knows” that is not evident in ordinary usage. (ii) **The “semantic” charge**: the contextualist is making a claim about the semantics of “knows,” and the evidence for it is contested (see the experimental literature on the “Epistemic Side-Effect Effect,” Ch. 3, §6.3). (iii) **The “dissolution” charge**: contextualism does not answer the sceptic; it *explains away* the sceptical question as a context-shift. Whether that is a solution or an evasion is contested. (iv) **The “stability” charge**: if the sceptical context is always available (as Lewis’s rule of attention suggests), then the sceptic can always re-enter, and the ordinary knowledge is always vulnerable. (This is the “fragility” objection.)

Contextualism is, at present, the most widely defended response to scepticism in the analytic literature. It is not the most satisfying.

### 6.5 Hinge epistemology

**Hinge epistemology** is the Wittgensteinian response, developed in *On Certainty* (1969) and in the contemporary literature by Moyal-Sharrock (2004), Coliva (2010, 2015), and Pritchard (2016).

The core idea: there are propositions that are **exempt from doubt** — not because they have been proved, but because they are the **hinges** on which inquiry turns. “Here is one hand,” “the world has existed for more than five minutes,” “I am not a brain in a vat” — these are not beliefs that are justified or unjustified; they are the framework within which justification takes place.

Wittgenstein’s metaphors:

> “The questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn.” (*OC* §341)

> “If I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put.” (*OC* §343)

> “It is so difficult to find the beginning. Or, better: it is difficult to begin at the beginning. And not try to go further back.” (*OC* §471)

The response to scepticism: the sceptic’s question (“how do you know there is an external world?”) presupposes that the external world is something that could be *known* or *doubted* in the ordinary way. But the existence of the external world is a hinge — it is not a proposition that is known; it is a proposition that is *presupposed* by every knowledge claim. The sceptic’s demand for a justification is therefore not a demand that can be met; it is a demand that misunderstands the structure of inquiry.

**Pritchard’s development** is the most systematic. He distinguishes:

- **Hinge commitments**: framework propositions that are not beliefs, but are the “background” against which beliefs are formed.
- **Hinge *certainty***: the non-epistemic assurance that the hinges are in place.
- **The “biscopic” proposal**: a two-pronged response that combines **hinge epistemology** (for the closure-based sceptical paradox) with **epistemological disjunctivism** (for the dream and the virtual-qualia problem). The sceptic’s difficulty is not one problem but two, and any adequate solution must treat both.

The **self-refutation problem** returns here: is the hinge epistemologist *certain* that the hinges are exempt from doubt? If yes, she has a doctrine, and the sceptic can ask for its justification. If no, the position is unstable. Pritchard’s answer is that hinge commitments are not *propositional* knowledge, so they are not subject to the demand for justification — but this is a delicate position, and it is contested.

### 6.6 The disjunctivist response

**Disjunctivism** (Ch. 5, §6) offers a distinctive response to scepticism. The disjunctivist denies that veridical perception and hallucination are experiences of the same kind, and therefore denies that the sceptical scenario (a world in which all my experiences are hallucinatory) is epistemically on a par with the actual world.

McDowell (1982, 1994) argues that the sceptic’s argument relies on the **highest common factor** assumption: that veridical perception and hallucination share a common epistemic core. If that assumption is false, the sceptical argument collapses, because the veridical case is *epistemically different* from the hallucinatory case — it is a case of *direct awareness* of the object, not a case of inference from an appearance.

**Objections.** (i) The disjunctivist has to explain why the two cases are indistinguishable from the inside, and the explanation is difficult. (ii) The response is *externalist* in spirit, and it faces the same question-begging charge as reliabilism. (iii) The response is *local*: it applies to perceptual knowledge, not to knowledge of the past, of other minds, or of the future.

## 7. The New Scepticisms

### 7.1 Simulation

**Bostrom’s simulation argument** (2003) is the most discussed new sceptical scenario. It is a trilemma:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
(1) & \text{It is very likely that civilizations go extinct before reaching a “posthuman” stage.} \\
(2) & \text{Any posthuman civilization is extremely unlikely to run simulations of their ancestors.} \\
(3) & \text{We are almost certainly living in a computer simulation.}
\end{array}
$$

At least one of the three must be true (assuming the relevant probabilities are well-defined). Bostrom does not claim that (3) is true; he claims that the disjunction is, and that we cannot assign a low probability to (3) without assigning a high probability to (1) or (2).

**The philosophical question** is whether the simulation hypothesis is a *sceptical* hypothesis in the traditional sense. Chalmers (*Reality+*, 2022) argues that it is not, or at least not in the same way as the BIV. The simulation hypothesis does not threaten the existence of the world; it threatens the *ultimate nature* of the world. If we are in a simulation, the objects we perceive are still real — they are digital objects with causal powers, and they are as real as any other objects. The simulation hypothesis is a hypothesis about the *substrate*, not about the *existence*, of the world.

This is an important move, because it suggests that the BIV and the simulation are different kinds of sceptical scenario. The BIV threatens the existence of the external world; the simulation threatens only its *fundamental* description. Whether this distinction is stable is contested (see the exchange between Chalmers and the “simulation realists”), but it is a useful corrective to the tendency to treat all sceptical scenarios as equivalent.

### 7.2 Dreaming

The dream argument has been revived by the empirical literature on **lucid dreaming** and on the **continuity** of dreaming and waking cognition. The findings are interesting: dreams are more cognitively sophisticated than was once thought; lucid dreamers can perform tasks (e.g., counting, eye movements) that were previously thought impossible; the boundary between dreaming and waking is less sharp than the Cartesian picture assumes.

The philosophical upshot is ambiguous. On the one hand, the findings make the dream scenario more vivid: if dreams can be as coherent as waking life, the epistemic gap is narrower. On the other hand, the findings suggest that dreams are *not* indistinguishable from waking — there are subtle cues (the “dream sign” literature), and the dreamer’s cognition is impaired in ways that the waking subject can detect. The empirical work therefore cuts both ways, and it is a useful reminder that the dream argument is not a purely *a priori* argument: its force depends on empirical claims about the nature of dreams.

### 7.3 The digital environment

The most practically important new scepticism is not philosophical but **epistemic**: the erosion of the conditions of testimonial knowledge.

- **Deepfakes** and AI-generated images: the visual evidence that was once the paradigm of certainty is now reproducible. The sceptical scenario (I cannot tell whether this image is real) is no longer a thought experiment; it is a daily occurrence.
- **AI-generated text**: the proliferation of machine-generated prose makes it increasingly difficult to identify the source of a claim, and therefore to assess its reliability.
- **Algorithmic curation**: the information environment is shaped by recommendation algorithms that select for engagement, not truth. The result is not scepticism about the external world, but scepticism about *testimony* — the conditions under which we can trust what we are told (Ch. 11, §6).

The philosophical point is that the *locus* of scepticism has shifted. The classical sceptic doubted perception; the modern sceptic doubts *testimony*. The arguments are structurally similar (the evidence underdetermines the belief), but the responses are different, because the epistemology of testimony has its own structure (Ch. 11).

### 7.4 The common structure

All the new scepticisms share the classical structure: a scenario is described in which the subject’s evidence is exactly the same as in the actual world, but the beliefs are false. The philosophical response is therefore the same: the debate is about whether the scenario is *possible*, whether it is *relevant*, and whether the subject’s evidence is really the same.

The novelty is not in the logic; it is in the **practical stakes**. The classical sceptic’s doubt was a philosophical exercise; the digital sceptic’s doubt is a condition of contemporary life. This is why the chapter’s final section asks not “can we refute the sceptic?” but “how should we live with the sceptic’s question?”

## 8. Living with Scepticism

### 8.1 What progress would look like

The honest verdict on the debate is that it has not produced a refutation of scepticism, and it is not likely to. What it has produced is a **clarification of the options**: the paradox of §1.3, the machinery of §5, the taxonomy of responses in §6. This is progress of a kind — the kind described in Ch. 1, §5 — but it is not the kind the naive picture expects.

The question is: what would a *satisfying* response look like? Three candidate answers:

**(i) The refutation.** A valid argument whose premises the sceptic must accept, and whose conclusion is $\neg \text{scepticism}$. The history of the debate suggests that this is not available: every premise strong enough to refute the sceptic is a premise the sceptic can question, and every premise the sceptic must accept is too weak to do the work.

**(ii) The dissolution.** A demonstration that the sceptical question is not a genuine question — that it rests on a false presupposition, or that it is a pseudo-question in the sense of Ch. 2, §1. This is the Wittgensteinian/Austinian option, and it has the virtue of explaining why the debate has not been resolved (the question is not the kind of thing that *can* be resolved). The cost is that it is *quietist*: it gives up the project of answering the sceptic, and it is not clear that giving up is a form of progress.

**(iii) The insulation.** A demonstration that the sceptical conclusion, even if true, has no practical or theoretical consequences — that it is a *philosophical* result that leaves everything else in place. This is the option explored by Hume (in the *Enquiry*, XII) and by Clarke (1972). The idea: the sceptic’s doubt is not a *live* doubt; it cannot be sustained in action, and it cannot be integrated into a working epistemic life. The philosophical result is real, but it is *isolated*.

None of the three is entirely satisfying, and the honest position is that the debate is **unresolved**. But the debate has taught us something important, and the rest of this chapter is about what.

### 8.2 The Humean consolation

Hume, at the end of the *Enquiry* (XII), describes the sceptic’s predicament with characteristic honesty:

> “The *Pyrrhonian* or *excessive* scepticism … may be consistently supported by the sceptic himself, and … no reasoning can ever be so convincing as to cure him of it. … But … nature … cures me of this philosophical melancholy … by relaxing … this bent of mind … I dine, I play a game of backgammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends; and when after three or four hours’ amusement, I would return to these speculations, they appear so cold, and strained, and ridiculous, that I cannot find in my heart to enter into them any further.” (*Enquiry*, XII.ii)

The consolation is not a refutation; it is a **psychological fact**. The sceptical doubt cannot be sustained; it is not a *practical* position. This is sometimes called the **“insulation” thesis**: scepticism is a theoretical result that is insulated from practice.

The consolation is honest, and it is not enough. The sceptic can reply that the fact that we cannot sustain the doubt is a fact about *us*, not about the *justification* of our beliefs. The doubt may be true even if it is unlivable. (This is the force of Stroud’s critique in *The Significance of Philosophical Scepticism*, 1984.)

### 8.3 The sceptical residue

What remains, after all the responses, is a **residue**: the recognition that our epistemic situation is not one of *certainty*. We do not have a foundation; we have a web of beliefs, supported by one another, with no external validation (Ch. 8, §3). The sceptic’s question — “is the whole web justified?” — cannot be answered from within the web, and there is no place outside it from which to answer.

This is the **coherentist** moral of the debate, and it is the one this book endorses, with qualifications. The web is not arbitrary: it is constrained by experience, by the practices of inquiry, and by the fact that it *works*. But the constraint is not a foundation; it is a *condition*.

The residue has a practical upshot: **epistemic humility**. The recognition that our beliefs are not certain is not a reason for paralysis; it is a reason for *calibrated confidence* (Ch. 4, §4.1), for *openness to revision*, and for *the awareness that the sceptic might be right*. The philosopher who has internalised the sceptical problem is a philosopher who holds her beliefs with the awareness that they could be otherwise — and that awareness is a virtue, not a defect.

### 8.4 The dialectical map

The chapter’s conclusion can be summarised in a table.

| Response | Core move | What it preserves | What it gives up | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Moore** | Assert the premise | Common sense | Dialectical force | Widely discussed, widely rejected |
| **Ordinary language** | Dissolve the question | Ordinary practice | The philosophical project | Powerful, quietist |
| **Externalism** | Redefine justification | Knowledge | Internalist access | Widely defended, question-begging |
| **Contextualism** | Relativise “knows” | Both common sense and scepticism | Stability | Dominant, unsatisfying |
| **Hinge epistemology** | Distinguish hinges from beliefs | The framework of inquiry | The demand for justification | Subtle, contested |
| **Disjunctivism** | Deny HCF | Perceptual knowledge | A unified theory of experience | Local, controversial |
| **Insulation** | Separate theory from practice | Practical life | Theoretical resolution | Honest, incomplete |
| **Scepticism** | Accept the conclusion | Consistency | Common sense | The position to beat |

The table is not a verdict. It is a map of the costs. The reader who wants to choose a position must decide which cost she is willing to pay — and that decision is itself a philosophical argument, not a matter of taste.

### 8.5 The criterion problem (preview)

There is a deeper question beneath the debate, which Ch. 13 will address: **the problem of the criterion**.

> How do we know which method of knowing to use, without already having a method of knowing?

The sceptic’s challenge is, at bottom, a challenge to the *criterion* of knowledge. The responses in §6 are all attempts to meet the challenge without begging the question — and each of them, in its own way, presupposes a criterion. The problem is not solved by the debate; it is *relocated*. Whether relocation is progress is a question for Ch. 13.

## Summary

- **Scepticism** is a family of positions. The chapter focuses on **external-world scepticism**, but it draws on the Pyrrhonian, Cartesian, and Humean traditions.
- **Descartes’ three waves of doubt** — the senses, the dream, the demon — are the canonical formulation of the problem. The dream argument is the strongest of the first two, and the demon is the ancestor of the BIV. The *cogito* is the foundation, and the **Cartesian circle** is the structural problem of the foundationalist response.
- **Ancient scepticism** (Pyrrho, Sextus, Agrippa) provides the argumentative resources — the **modes**, the **trilemma**, the **self-refutation objection** — that the modern debate still uses. The Pyrrhonian response to self-refutation (scepticism as a *capacity*, not a doctrine) anticipates the quietist responses of §6.
- **Putnam’s BIV argument** is a **semantic** argument: the BIV’s words do not refer to the things the sceptical hypothesis requires, so the hypothesis is self-refuting. The argument is *partial*: it depends on externalism, it establishes only that the hypothesis cannot be *said*, and it does not touch the dream or the simulation.
- **The epistemic machinery** — **closure**, **relevant alternatives**, **sensitivity** (Nozick), **safety** (Sosa, Pritchard) — has clarified the debate without resolving it. The **sceptical paradox** (DeRose) shows that the responses are choices among three plausible principles, and every choice has a cost.
- **The responses** — Moore, ordinary-language dissolution, externalism, contextualism, hinge epistemology, disjunctivism, insulation — each preserves something and gives up something. The **table** in §8.4 is the map of costs.
- **The new scepticisms** — simulation, dreaming, deepfakes — are structurally similar to the classical ones, but the *locus* of doubt has shifted from perception to **testimony**. The **simulation hypothesis** (Bostrom) is a *substrate* hypothesis, not a *sceptical* hypothesis in the classical sense (Chalmers).
- **The honest verdict**: the debate has not refuted scepticism, and it is not likely to. What it has produced is a clarification of the options, and a **residue** — the recognition that our epistemic situation is not one of certainty. The residue has a practical upshot: **epistemic humility**, calibrated confidence, and openness to revision.

> *A closing note.* The problem of the external world is the most persistent problem in philosophy, and it is the one most likely to be dismissed. This chapter has tried to show that it is not a game — that it is generated by a genuine feature of our epistemic situation, that it has a history, and that the responses to it have real costs. The chapter has not resolved the problem, and it has not pretended to. What it has done is to put the reader in a position to *see* the problem clearly, and to *choose* among the responses with an awareness of what each one gives up. That is what philosophical progress looks like here: not a refutation, but a clarification — and a clarification that leaves the question open is not a failure. It is the honest form of success.

> *And a final note on method.* This chapter has applied the instruments of Part I to the oldest problem in epistemology. The reader should notice how the instruments work: the reconstruction of the arguments (Ch. 2), the diagnosis of the presuppositions (Ch. 2, §3), the comparison of the costs (Ch. 4, §3), and the recognition that the problem may be *dissolved* rather than solved (Ch. 3, §7). The next chapter turns to the analysis of knowledge, which is the theory that the responses to scepticism presuppose. The connection is direct: every response in §6 is a claim about what knowledge *is*, and the debate about knowledge is the debate about which response is available.
