# Chapter 15 — Universals, Properties, and Abstract Objects

> “The world of universals, therefore, may be described as a world of being, which is not a world of existence. … The universal is not a mere idea; it is something which exists independently of any particular instance.”
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Ch. IX (paraphrased from the argument)

> “It is the same thing that we call ‘red’ in this rose, in that sunset, and in this drop of blood. But what is it that is the same?”
> — the one-over-many problem, in its plainest form

---

Chapter 14 gave us the method. It gave us the criterion of ontological commitment (to be is to be the value of a bound variable), the test of parsimony (do not multiply without necessity), and the meta-debate (naturalism, fundamentalism, deflationism). This chapter applies the method to the oldest and most contested case: **universals**.

The case is old because the problem is plain. Many things are red. The word “red” applies to each of them. What makes them red? The realist says: a *universal* — a single entity, redness, that is wholly present in each red thing. The nominalist says: nothing — only the word, or the class, or the resemblance among the things. The trope theorist says: neither a universal nor nothing, but a *particular* property — this redness here, and that redness there, which resemble one another.

The case is contested because the arguments on each side are strong, and the costs are real. The realist has the best explanation of predication, laws, and resemblance; the nominalist has the best account of parsimony and of the causal closure of the physical; the trope theorist has the best account of the *particularity* of properties. None of them has won, and the debate is, in a sense, *permanent*.

The chapter proceeds in seven movements. §1 states the one-over-many problem and Russell's Platonist solution, including the third-man regress. §2 examines the three main forms of nominalism and their objections. §3 examines trope theory and the bundle theory. §4 examines realism about universals, in its immanent and transcendent forms. §5 examines the contemporary debate about *naturalness* and about *dispositions* — the two places where the property debate does the most work. §6 examines abstract objects, Benacerraf's dilemma, and the epistemology of the abstract. §7 examines structuralism, the most important recent development. §8 gives an honest verdict.

## 1. The One Over Many

### 1.1 The problem

The problem is stated in the plainest possible form by Aristotle (*Metaphysics* Z.1, 1028a):

> “The question which was raised of old and is always raised and will always be raised, namely ‘What is being?’ is the question ‘What is substance?’”

And it is stated in the plainest possible form by anyone who notices that the word “red” applies to *many* things.

**The structure of the problem.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{This rose is red.} \\
2. & \text{That sunset is red.} \\
3. & \text{This drop of blood is red.} \\
4. & \text{The three things are *different* particulars.} \\
5. & \text{They are *the same* in respect of colour.} \\
\hline
6. & \therefore \text{There is *something* that they share — or the appearance of sharing must be explained away.}
\end{array}
$$

The conclusion is deliberately disjunctive. The problem is not “what is the shared thing?”; it is “is there a shared thing, and if not, what explains the appearance of sharing?” The *realist* takes the first horn; the *nominalist* takes the second; the *trope theorist* takes a third, which is that there is a shared *respect* but no shared *entity*.

**Why it matters.** The problem is not a *puzzle about words*. It is the entry point to three of the largest debates in metaphysics:

- **Predication.** What makes a predicate *apply* to a subject? (The semantic form.)
- **Resemblance.** What makes two things *similar*? (The comparative form.)
- **Law.** What makes a regularity *necessary*, or a disposition *real*? (The explanatory form.)

The *realist* has a single answer to all three: universals. The *nominalist* has a different answer to each, and the answers are less unified. The *trope theorist* has an answer that is unified but costly. The *choice* among them is the choice among *explanatory packages*, and the choice is the subject of this chapter.

### 1.2 Russell's Platonist solution

Russell's treatment is in *The Problems of Philosophy*, Chs. IX–X, and it is the *clearest* statement of *transcendent* realism in the analytic tradition.

**The argument.** Russell begins from *perception*. When I see a white patch, I am *acquainted* with the *colour* — an entity that is not the *patch* (which is a particular) and not my *experience* (which is private). The colour is a **universal**: it is *one* entity that can be *wholly present* in *many* patches.

**The key claims.**

1. **Universals are real.** They are not *fictions* or *conventions*; they are *constituents* of the world.
2. **Universals are known by acquaintance.** In Ch. X, Russell argues that we are *acquainted* with universals — with whiteness, with before-ness, with identity — just as we are *acquainted* with sense-data.
3. **Universals are *not* located in space and time.** They are *not* particulars, and they do not have a *position*; they are *present* wherever they are *instantiated*, but not *located* anywhere.
4. **Universals are *prior* to particulars in the order of knowledge.** We come to know the universal *through* the particular, but the universal is *not* *derivative* from the particular.

**The Platonism.** Russell's view is *transcendent* realism: universals exist *independently* of their instances. Even if no red thing existed, redness would *be* — not, perhaps, *exist*, but *subsist*. (Russell's terminology varies; in *Problems* he is careful to say that universals have *being* rather than *existence*, but the distinction is not stable.)

**The argument for *transcendence*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The proposition "red is a colour" is *true* even if no red thing exists.} \\
2. & \text{The truth of the proposition *requires* that redness *be* something.} \\
3. & \text{The *something* is not a *particular* (it is *shared* by all possible red things).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Redness is a *universal* that *exists* independently of its instances.}
\end{array}
$$

**The objection.** The argument is a *modal* argument, and it *presupposes* that *truth* requires *truthmakers* that exist *independently* of the *actual* *instances*. The *nominalist* denies the *presupposition*: the proposition is *true* because of the *meaning* of “red” and “colour,” not because of a *transcendent* *entity*.

### 1.3 The third man regress

The *most* *famous* *objection* to *transcendent* realism is the **third-man regress**. It is stated by Plato in the *Parmenides* (132a–b), and it is repeated by Aristotle (*Metaphysics* Z.13, 1039a2; *Sophistical Refutations* 178b36).

**The argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The man, Callias, is a man because he *participates* in the Form of Man.} \\
2. & \text{The Form of Man is *itself* a man (it is the *paradigm* of man).} \\
3. & \text{Callias and the Form of Man are *both* men.} \\
4. & \text{They are both men because they *participate* in a *third* Form of Man.} \\
5. & \text{The third Form is *itself* a man, and so on.} \\
\hline
6. & \therefore \text{The regress is *infinite*, and the theory *explains* nothing.}
\end{array}
$$

**The structure.** The regress is generated by the **self-predication assumption** (the Form of Man is a man) and the **non-identity assumption** (the Form of Man is not identical with Callias). If *both* assumptions are *accepted*, the regress is *unavoidable*.

**The replies.**

**(i) Deny self-predication.** The Form of Man is *not* a man; it is the *property* of being a man. (This is the *Aristotelian* reply: universals are *predicable* of particulars, but they are not *instances* of themselves.)

**(ii) Deny non-identity.** The Form of Man *is* the *collection* of men (a *nominalist* reply). (This is the *class* *nominalism* of §2.2.)

**(iii) Accept the regress and deny it is vicious.** The regress is *infinite*, but it is not *vicious*: it is a *structure* of *higher-order* *universals*, and the *structure* is *real*. (This is Armstrong's *structural* *realism*; §4.4.)

**The *application* to Russell.** Russell *acknowledges* the regress in Ch. IX, and he *does* not *resolve* it. His *reply* is that the regress is *not* a *refutation* of the *theory*, but a *feature* of the *relation* between *universals* and *particulars*. The *reply* is *unsatisfying*, and it is the *reason* the *contemporary* *realists* (Armstrong) have *abandoned* the *transcendent* *picture* for the *immanent* one.

### 1.4 Four arguments for universals

The *positive* *case* for *realism* has *four* *components*.

**(i) The semantic argument.** Predicates *apply* to *many* *subjects*, and the *application* is *not* *arbitrary*. The *best* *explanation* of the *application* is that the *predicate* *expresses* a *universal*.

**The *objection*.** The *explanation* *presupposes* that the *predicate* *refers* to *something*, and the *presupposition* is *precisely* what is *at issue*. (The *nominalist* *reply*: the *application* is *explained* by *convention*, *resemblance*, or *causal* *history*.)

**(ii) The resemblance argument.** Things *resemble* one another, and the *resemblance* is *not* *brute*: it *consists* in the *sharing* of a *property*. The *best* *explanation* of *resemblance* is *property* *sharing*.

**The *objection*.** Resemblance can be *primitive*: the *nominalist* *holds* that *similarity* is a *relation* *among* *particulars* that *does* not *require* a *shared* *property* (§2.3). The *debate* is *about* whether the *primitive* *resemblance* is *adequate*.

**(iii) The explanatory argument.** Universals *explain* *laws*: “all electrons have charge $e$” is *necessary* because *charge* is a *universal*, and the *universal* *necessitates* the *behaviour*. The *best* *explanation* of *law* is *universals*.

**The *objection*.** The *explanation* *presupposes* that *laws* are *necessary*, and the *presupposition* is *contested* (the *Humean* *tradition*; Ch. 18, §5; Ch. 39, §1). The *anti-Humean* *reply*: the *necessity* is *real*, and *universals* are the *best* *explanation* of it.

**(iv) The truthmaker argument.** Statements *about* *properties* (“redness is a colour”) are *true*, and the *truth* *requires* a *truthmaker*. The *best* *candidate* is the *universal*.

**The *objection*.** The *truthmaker* *requirement* is *contested* (Ch. 22, §2): some *truths* are *true* *without* a *truthmaker* (the *nominalist* *holds* that *analytic* *truths* and *negative* *existentials* are *of* this kind). The *reply*: the *truthmaker* *requirement* is *defeasible*, but the *burden* is on the *nominalist* to *show* that *property* *claims* are *of* the *exceptional* *kind*.

### 1.5 The state of the problem

The *one-over-many* *problem* is *not* *solved*, and it is *not* *likely* to be. The *debate* is *not* about the *data* (everyone *agrees* that *red* *things* *resemble* one another); it is about the *best* *explanation* of the *data*. The *three* *families* (realism, nominalism, trope theory) *each* have a *coherent* *package*, and the *choice* *among* them is a *comparative* *judgment* (Ch. 4, §3.2).

The *most* *important* *development* in the *contemporary* *debate* is the *shift* from *the* *question* “are there universals?” to the *question* “what *work* do universals *do*, and is the *work* *best* done by them?” The *shift* is the *method* of *ontological* *commitment* (Ch. 14, §3), applied to *properties*.

## 2. Nominalism

### 2.1 Predicate nominalism

**The view.** Predicate nominalism is the *simplest* form of nominalism: “redness” is *nothing over and above* the *predicate* “red.” To say that a thing is red is to say that the predicate *applies* to it, and the *application* is *conventional*.

**The statement.** Hobbes (*Leviathan*, 1651, I.iv) — “there is nothing in the world universal but names” — and, in the *contemporary* *literature*, Sellars (“Abstract Entities,” 1963) and Quine (in his *earlier* work).

**The argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{All there is is *particulars* (the *nominalist* *premise*).} \\
2. & \text{The *word* "red" is a *particular* (a *sound*, a *mark*).} \\
3. & \text{The *application* of "red" to a thing is a *convention*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Redness is *nothing* *over* *and* *above* the *word* and the *convention*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The objections.**

**(i) The *predicate* *nominalism* *is* *circular*.** The *convention* *explains* *which* things are called “red,” but it does *not* *explain* *why* *those* things are *red*. If the *convention* were *arbitrary*, it *could* have *applied* to *any* things; the *fact* that it *applies* to *red* things *requires* an *explanation*.

**The *reply*.** The *convention* is *not* *arbitrary*: it is *caused* by the *perceptual* *similarity* of the things, and the *similarity* is *primitive*. (The *reply* *leads* to *resemblance* nominalism.)

**(ii) The *translation* *problem*.** Predicate nominalism *cannot* *translate* statements *about* *properties* *that* are *not* *predicable* *of* *things* — “red is a colour,” “red is *closer* to *orange* than to blue.” The *translation* would have to be *about* the *word* “red,” but the *statement* is *about* *the* *colour*, not the word.

**The *reply*.** The *translation* can be *metalinguistic*: “red is a colour” is *translated* as “‘red’ is a *colour* *predicate*.” The *reply* is *artificial*, and it is *subject* to the *objection* that the *translation* *changes* the *subject*.

### 2.2 Class nominalism

**The view.** A property is the **class** of its instances: redness = the class of all red things.

**The statement.** The *view* is *historically* *influential* (it is *Carnap’s* *view* in the *Aufbau*, and the *view* of *most* *set-theoretic* *reductions*), and it is *developed* by *Lewis* (in *Parts of Classes*, 1991, as a *form* of *reduction*) and *others*.

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *reduces* *properties* to *sets*, and *sets* are *already* *in* the *ontology* *of* *mathematics* (Ch. 14, §3.4). The *reduction* is *parsimonious*.

**The objections.**

**(i) Coextensive but distinct properties.** Two *properties* can have the *same* *instances* and yet be *distinct*. The *classic* *example*: *cordate* (having a *heart*) and *renate* (having a *kidney*) are *coextensive* in *our* *world* (all *cordates* are *renates*), but they are *distinct* *properties*. The *class* *nominalist* *identifies* them, and the *identification* is *false*.

**The *reply*.** The *class* *nominalist* can *distinguish* the *properties* *modally*: the *class* of *cordates* is *possibly* *distinct* from the *class* of *renates* (there is a *possible* *world* in which a *creature* has a *heart* but no *kidney*). The *reply* *reintroduces* *modality* (Ch. 16), and it *commits* the *nominalist* to *possible* *worlds* — which is *not* *nominalistically* *innocent*.

**(ii) The *membership* *problem*.** The *class* *nominalist* *identifies* *redness* with the *class* of *red* *things*. But the *class* *contains* *red* *things*, and the *things* *are* *red* *because* of *redness*. If *redness* = the *class*, then the *things* *are* *red* because they are *members* of the *class* — and *membership* is a *relation* that *requires* an *explanation*.

**The *reply*.** The *membership* *relation* *is* the *explanation*: the *thing* is *red* *because* it is a *member* of the *class*. The *reply* is *circular*: the *class* is *defined* as the *class* of *red* *things*, and the *definition* *presupposes* the *property*.

**(iii) The *modality* *problem*.** The *class* of *red* *things* is *contingent*: it *could* have *contained* a *blue* *thing* (if a *blue* *thing* had *been* *red*). But *redness* *could* not have *been* *blue*. The *class* *nominalist* *identifies* *redness* with a *contingent* *class*, and the *identification* *loses* the *modal* *profile* of the *property*.

### 2.3 Resemblance nominalism

**The view.** A property is *nothing* *over* *and* *above* the *resemblance* *relation* *among* *particulars*. To say that $a$ and $b$ are *both* *red* is to say that they *resemble* one another *in* *respect* *of* *colour* — and the *resemblance* is *primitive*.

**The statement.** Price (*Thinking and Experience*, 1953), and in the *contemporary* *literature*, Rodriguez-Pereyra (*Resemblance Nominalism*, 2002).

**The argument.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Properties are *explanatorily* *redundant*: the *resemblance* *among* *red* *things* *explains* *everything* that *redness* *explains*.} \\
2. & \text{The *resemblance* is a *relation* *among* *particulars* (it is *not* a *universal*).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{There are *no* *universals*; there is *only* *resemblance*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtues*.** The *view* *avoids* the *third-man* *regress* (resemblance is *primitive*), and it *avoids* the *one-over-many* *problem* (the *many* *are* *similar*, not *sharing*).

**The objections.** The *view* *has* *three* *classic* *problems*, and they are *serious*.

**(i) The *imperfect* *community* (Goodman 1951).** Suppose that $a$, $b$, $c$ are *red*, and $d$, $e$, $f$ are *blue*. The *resemblance* *nominalist* *says* that *redness* is the *resemblance* *among* $a, b, c$. But consider the *class* $\{a, b, d\}$: $a$ *resembles* $b$ (both *red*), $b$ *resembles* $d$ (?), and $a$ *resembles* $d$ (?) — the *resemblance* *relation* *among* the *members* is *not* *transitive*, and the *class* $\{a, b, d\}$ has *no* *shared* *property*. The *resemblance* *nominalist* *cannot* *distinguish* the *genuine* *community* (the *red* things) from the *imperfect* *community* (the *arbitrary* *collection*).

**The *reply*.** The *resemblance* *nominalist* *can* *define* the *community* *as* the *maximal* *class* of *mutually* *resembling* *things*, and the *definition* *excludes* the *imperfect* *community*. The *reply* *requires* a *notion* of *degree* of *resemblance*, and the *notion* is *not* *primitive*.

**(ii) The *companionship* *problem* (Goodman).** If *every* *red* *thing* *is* *also* *round*, then the *resemblance* *among* *red* *things* *cannot* *distinguish* *redness* from *roundness*: the *class* of *red* *things* is *also* the *class* of *round* *things*, and the *resemblance* *relation* *does* *not* *tell* us *which* *property* is *which*.

**The *reply*.** The *reply* *requires* a *notion* of *respect* in which the *things* *resemble*: the *things* *resemble* *in* *colour*, not *in* *shape*. But the *notion* of *respect* *is* *precisely* the *notion* of *property*, and the *reply* *reintroduces* *properties*.

**(iii) The *regress*. Resemblance is a *relation*, and *relations* *can* *resemble* *one* *another*. The *resemblance* *between* *two* *resemblance* *relations* *requires* a *third* *resemblance* *relation*, and the *regress* is *infinite*. (This is the *same* *structure* as the *third-man* *regress, applied to the *relation* *itself.)

**The *reply*.** The *regress* is *not* *vicious*: the *resemblance* *relations* are *primitive*, and the *chain* of *resemblances* is *not* *required* to *terminate*. The *reply* is *contested* — the *infinite* *chain* is *not* an *explanation* of the *similarity*, and the *nominalist* *owes* an *account* of *why* the *chain* *grounds* the *similarity*.

### 2.4 The state of nominalism

The *nominalist* *programme* is *promising* and *incomplete*. The *three* *forms* *each* *have* *costs*: predicate nominalism is *circular*; class nominalism *loses* the *modal* *profile*; resemblance nominalism *struggles* with *imperfect* *communities* and the *regress*.

**The *contemporary* *nominalism***. The *most* *defensible* *form* is a *hybrid*: *resemblance* nominalism *plus* a *natural* *class* *theory* (Lewis 1983), or *class* nominalism *plus* *naturalness*. The *hybrid* *reintroduces* *properties* *under* *another* *name* (the *“natural”* *classes*), and the *reintroduction* is *precisely* what the *nominalist* *wanted* to *avoid*. (See §5.1 on *naturalness*.)

**The *honest* *position*.** Nominalism is *not* *refuted*, but it is *not* *established*. It is *the* *most* *parsimonious* *option*, and it is *the* *least* *explanatory*. The *choice* *between* it and *realism* is the *choice* *between* *parsimony* and *explanatory* *power* — and the *choice* *depends* on the *weight* *given* to *each* (Ch. 4, §3.2).

## 3. Trope Theory

### 3.1 The view

**Trope theory** is the *view* that *properties* are *particulars*: they are **tropes** — *individual* *property* *instances* that are *located* in *space* and *time*, and that *resemble* one another without *sharing* a *universal*.

**The terminology.** A trope is *this* *redness* (here, now), *that* *redness* (there, then). The *two* *tropes* are *distinct* *entities*; they are *similar* but *not* *identical*. The *universal* “redness” is the *class* (or the *resemblance* *class*) of *all* *red* *tropes*.

**The statement.** D. C. Williams (“On the Elements of Being,” 1953); Keith Campbell (*Abstract Particulars*, 1990); Anna-Sofia Maurin (*If Tropes*, 2002); E. J. Lowe (*The Four-Category Ontology*, 2006).

**The *virtue*.** Trope theory *splits* the *difference* between *realism* and *nominalism*:

| Feature | Realism | Trope theory | Nominalism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Properties are *real* | Yes | Yes | No |
| Properties are *universal* | Yes | No | No |
| Properties are *located* | No | Yes | — |
| Properties are *causally* *efficacious* | (Disputed) | Yes | — |
| The *third-man* *regress* | Threatens | Avoided | Avoided |

**The *key* *move*.** The trope theorist *denies* that *similarity* *requires* a *shared* *universal*. Similarity is a *relation* *among* *tropes* — a *primitive* relation of *resemblance* — and the *relation* is *grounded* in the *tropes* *themselves*.

### 3.2 The bundle theory

**The view.** A *particular* *object* is a **bundle** of *compresent* *tropes*. The *redness*, the *roundness*, the *sweetness* of this apple are *tropes*, and the *apple* is the *bundle* of them.

**The statement.** Hume (*Treatise*, 1739–40, I.iv.6) — the *bundle* *theory* of the *self*; Williams (1953); Campbell (1990).

**The *virtue*.** The *bundle* *theory* *avoids* the *notion* of a *bare* *particular* (a *substratum* that *has* properties but is *nothing* *in* *itself*). The *object* is *nothing* *over* *and* *above* its *properties*, and the *properties* are *tropes*.

**The objection: the compresence regress.** The *bundle* *theory* *requires* a *relation* of **compresence** — the relation that *binds* the *tropes* *together* into a *single* *object*. But *compresence* is *itself* a *relation*, and the *relation* *must* be *bound* to the *tropes* by *another* *relation*, and so on.

**The *reply*.** *Compresence* is *primitive*: it is *not* a *further* *trope* that *needs* *binding*; it is the *form* of *unity* *among* *tropes*. The *reply* is *contested*: the *notion* of a *primitive* *form* of *unity* is *not* *an* *explanation*, and the *bundle* *theorist* *owes* an *account* of *why* *some* *tropes* are *compresent* and *others* are *not*.

**The objection: the pairing problem.** If *objects* are *bundles* of *tropes*, then *two* *objects* *cannot* have *exactly* the *same* *tropes* (they would be *the* *same* *bundle*). But *two* *distinct* *objects* *can* have *exactly* the *same* *properties* (the *principle* of the *identity* of *indiscernibles* is *false* if *haecceities* are *possible*). The *bundle* *theory* *implies* the *identity* of *indiscernibles*, and the *implication* is *contested*.

**The *reply*.** The *bundle* *theorist* *can* *accept* the *identity* of *indiscernibles* (Leibniz did), and the *acceptance* is a *substantive* *metaphysical* *claim*. The *reply* is *coherent*, but it *commits* the *theorist* to a *controversial* *principle*.

### 3.3 The state of trope theory

**The *programme* is *promising* and *incomplete*. The *virtues* are *real* (it *avoids* the *third-man* *regress*, it *locates* *properties*, it *captures* the *particularity* of *property* *instances*). The *costs* are *real* (the *compresence* *regress*, the *pairing* *problem*, the *primitive* *resemblance*).

**The *most* *defensible* *version***. A *moderate* *trope* *theory*: tropes *plus* a *primitive* *relation* of *resemblance* *plus* a *primitive* *relation* of *compresence*. The *primitives* are *not* *explanations*, but they are *not* *mysteries* either: they are the *basic* *structure* of the *theory*.

**The *relationship* to *realism***. Trope theory and *immanent* realism *are* *close*: both *hold* that *properties* are *real* and *located*; they *differ* on whether *properties* are *universals* or *particulars*. The *debate* *between* them is *about* whether the *similarity* *among* *tropes* *requires* a *universal* — and the *answer* *depends* on the *theory* of *resemblance*.

## 4. Realism about Universals

### 4.1 Immanent versus transcendent

The *realist* *tradition* *divides* into *two* *families*.

| Family | Where universals are | When they exist | Paradigm |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Transcendent** (Platonic) | In a *separate* *realm*, *independent* of *particulars* | *Always* (even if *uninstantiated*) | Plato; Russell (1912) |
| **Immanent** (Aristotelian) | *In* the *particulars* that *instantiate* them | *Only* when *instantiated* | Aristotle; Armstrong |

**The *transcendent* *view*.** Universals are *abstract* *objects* (Ch. 14, §6): they are *not* *located* in *space* and *time*, and they *exist* *independently* of *their* *instances*. The *view* *has* the *virtue* of *explaining* *uninstantiated* *properties* (“being a unicorn” *is* a *property*, even if *nothing* has it).

**The *immanent* *view*.** Universals are *immanent* in their *instances*: the *redness* of *this* *apple* *is* the *universal* *redness*, and the *universal* *exists* *only* *in* the *apple* (and *other* *red* *things*). The *view* *has* the *virtue* of *avoiding* the *separate* *realm*, and the *cost* of *making* *uninstantiated* *properties* *problematic*.

**The *debate*.** The *transcendent* *view* is *more* *Platonic*, *more* *profligate*, and *more* *explanatory* (it *explains* *uninstantiated* *properties*). The *immanent* *view* is *more* *Aristotelian*, *more* *parsimonious*, and *less* *explanatory*. The *choice* *depends* on the *weight* *given* to *parsimony* (Ch. 14, §4).

### 4.2 Armstrong's truthmaker argument

David Armstrong (*Universals: An Opinionated Introduction*, 1989; *A World of States of Affairs*, 1997) offers the *most* *systematic* *defence* of *immanent* realism.

**The *core* *thesis*.** The *world* is a *world* of **states of affairs** — *complex* *entities* that *consist* of a *particular* *instantiating* a *universal* (or *universals* *related* by a *higher-order* *universal*). The *state* *of* *affairs* is the *truthmaker*: it is *what* *makes* a *true* *proposition* *true*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Every *contingent* *truth* *requires* a *truthmaker*.} \\
2. & \text{The *truth* “this apple is red” is *contingent*.} \\
3. & \text{The *truthmaker* is a *state* of *affairs*: the *apple*’s *instantiating* *redness*.} \\
4. & \text{The *state* of *affairs* *requires* both the *particular* and the *universal*.} \\
\hline
5. & \therefore \text{Universals are *real*, and they are *constituents* of *states* of *affairs*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *structure*.** Armstrong’s *ontology* has *three* *categories*: *particulars*, *universals*, and *states* of *affairs*. The *state* *of* *affairs* is *not* a *third* *kind* of *thing* *alongside* the *other* *two*; it is the *combination* of *them*. The *combination* is *primitive*: it is *not* *further* *analysable*.

**The *virtue*.** The *truthmaker* *argument* *connects* *ontology* to *truth* (Ch. 32), and the *connection* is *the* *strongest* *argument* for *realism*. The *nominalist* *must* *explain* *what* *makes* the *proposition* *true*, and the *explanation* is *less* *direct*.

**The *cost*.** The *truthmaker* *principle* is *contested* (Ch. 22, §2): some *philosophers* *hold* that *truths* can be *true* *without* a *truthmaker* (the *deflationary* *view*; Ch. 32, §5). The *argument* *presupposes* the *principle*, and the *presupposition* is *not* *neutral*.

### 4.3 The instantiation regress

The *most* *serious* *objection* to *immanent* realism is the **instantiation** *regress*, a *version* of *Bradley’s* *regress*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{A *particular* } a \text{ *instantiates* a *universal* } F. \\
2. & \text{The *instantiation* relation is *itself* a *relation*, and *relations* are *universals* (on the *realist* *view*).} \\
3. & \text{The *instantiation* relation must be *instantiated* by } a \text{ and } F \text{ — which *requires* a *further* *relation*, and so on.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *regress* is *infinite*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *structure*.** The *regress* is the *same* as the *third-man* *regress*, *applied* to the *instantiation* *relation* *itself*. The *regress* is *generated* by the *assumption* that *instantiation* is a *universal*, and the *assumption* is *precisely* what the *realist* *needs*.

**Armstrong’s *reply*** (1978, vol. 2, p. 45; 1997, p. 116). Instantiation is *not* a *relation* *in* the *ordinary* *sense*. It is a **fundamental* *tie*** — a *primitive* *form* of *unity* that *cannot* be *analysed* into a *relation* *plus* *relata*. The *tie* is *not* a *universal*; it is the *form* of *combination* *between* a *particular* and a *universal*.

**The *objection*.** The *reply* is a *label*, not an *explanation*. The *realist* *owes* an *account* of *what* the *tie* *is*, and the *account* is *not* *available*. (This is the *ad hocness* *objection* of Ch. 3, §1.6, and it is *serious*.)

**The *counter-objection*.** *Every* *theory* *has* *primitives*: the *nominalist*’s *resemblance*, the *trope* *theorist*’s *compresence*, the *realist*’s *instantiation*. The *question* is *which* *set* of *primitives* is *most* *explanatory*, and the *answer* is *comparative*.

### 4.4 Armstrong's structural realism

The *most* *ambitious* *part* of Armstrong’s *system* is his **structural* *realism*** about *universals* (1997, Ch. 5; 2004).

**The *thesis*.** Every *universal* is *related* to *every* *other* *universal* by *higher-order* *relations* of *resemblance* and *difference*. The *totality* of *universals* is a **block**: a *structured* *whole* in which *each* *universal* has its *identity* *determined* by its *position* in the *structure*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Each *universal* has a *nature*: what it is.} \\
2. & \text{The *nature* is *determined* by the *relations* of *resemblance* and *difference* *among* the *universals*.} \\
3. & \text{The *relations* are *real*, and they are *higher-order* *universals*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *totality* of *universals* is a *structured* *structure* *without* an *external* *foundation*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *avoids* the *“third-man”* *regress* by *making* the *regress* *constitutive* of the *universals*: the *structure* *is* the *explanation*, and the *structure* *does* not *require* a *further* *level*.

**The *problem*.** The *view* *commits* Armstrong to a *form* of *structuralism* that is *close* to the *ontic* *structuralism* of Ch. 19, §6 — and the *costs* are *similar*: the *individual* *universals* *lose* their *intrinsic* *natures*, and the *structure* *becomes* the *fundamental* *reality*.

**The *significance*.** The *view* is the *most* *systematic* *attempt* to *reconcile* *realism* with the *contemporary* *structuralist* *insight*. Whether it *succeeds* is *contested*, but it is the *most* *interesting* *development* in the *contemporary* *debate*.

## 5. Properties: Natural and Non-Natural

### 5.1 Naturalness

The *most* *influential* *development* in the *contemporary* *debate* is David Lewis’s *notion* of **naturalness** (“New Work for a Theory of Universals,” 1983).

**The *thesis*.** Some *properties* are **natural** (or *sparse*): they are the *properties* that *carve* *nature* at its *joints*. Others are *artificial* (or *abundant*): they are *mere* *sets* of *things*, *defined* by *arbitrary* *predicates*.

**The *examples*.**

| Natural | Artificial |
|---|---|
| Having *charge* $e$ | Being *either* *charged* or *a* *donkey* |
| Being *green* | Being *grue* (Ch. 10, §3) |
| Being *a* *gold* *atom* | Being *a* *gold* *atom* *or* *a* *sunday* |

**The *role* of *naturalness***. Natural properties *do* *explanatory* *work*:

1. **Similarity**: natural properties *ground* *objective* *resemblance*. Two *things* *resemble* one another *because* they *share* a *natural* *property*.
2. **Law**: the *laws* *of* *nature* *mention* *natural* *properties*. The *laws* are *not* *arbitrary* *generalizations*; they *express* *real* *connections* (Ch. 18, §5).
3. **Induction**: *natural* *properties* *support* *induction*. The *projectibility* of a *predicate* (Ch. 10, §3) *depends* on the *naturalness* of the *property* it *expresses*.
4. **Reference**: *natural* properties *ground* *reference*. The *predicates* that *refer* to *natural* properties are *the* *ones* that *carve* *nature*; the *predicates* that *refer* to *artificial* *properties* are *parasitic* on the *natural* *ones*.

**The *argument*.** The *argument* for *naturalness* is an *IBE* (Ch. 10, §6): the *notion* *explains* *similarity*, *law*, *induction*, and *reference*, and *no* *rival* *notion* *explains* *them* *as* *well*.

**The *cost*.** The *notion* *is* *primitive*: *naturalness* *cannot* be *defined* in *more* *basic* *terms*, and the *primitive* is *not* *independently* *motivated*. (This is the *same* *objection* as the *instantiation* *regress*, and it is *serious*.)

### 5.2 Reference magnetism

The *most* *striking* *application* of *naturalness* is Lewis’s **reference* *magnetism***, which is a *response* to Putnam’s *model-theoretic* *argument* (Putnam 1980; Ch. 12, §3).

**Putnam’s *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Any *theory* has *infinitely* *many* *models* (by the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem).} \\
2. & \text{The *models* *assign* *different* *referents* to the *terms* of the *theory*.} \\
3. & \text{Nothing *in* the *theory* *selects* *one* *model* *over* *the* *others*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Reference is *indeterminate*; there is *no* *fact* *of* the *matter* *about* what our *terms* *refer* to.}
\end{array}
$$

**Lewis’s *reply*.** The *models* are *not* *equal*: the *interpretations* that *assign* *natural* *properties* to the *predicates* are *better* than the *interpretations* that *assign* *artificial* *properties*. The *naturalness* *breaks* the *symmetry*, and the *breaking* *selects* the *intended* *model*.

**The *slogan*.** “Reference is *magnetic*: *natural* *properties* *attract* *reference*.”

**The *objection*.** The *reply* *presupposes* *naturalness*, and the *presupposition* is *precisely* what is *at* *issue*. (The *reply* is *circular* if *naturalness* is *itself* *reference-dependent*; it is *not* *circular* if *naturalness* is *a* *primitive* *feature* of the *world*.)

**The *state* of the debate.** Reference magnetism is *influential*, and it is *contested*. The *debate* is *connected* to the *realism/anti-realism* *debate* (Ch. 22, §1), and it is *one* of the *deepest* *issues* in *contemporary* *metaphysics*.

### 5.3 Dispositions and categorical bases

The *most* *consequential* *debate* *about* *properties* is the *debate* *between* **dispositionalism** and **categoricalism**.

**The *distinction*.**

| Kind of property | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Dispositional** | A *power* or *capacity* to *behave* in a *certain* *way* | Solubility, charge, fragility |
| **Categorical** | A *qualitative* *feature* that is *not* *defined* by its *effects* | Shape, mass (on the *categorical* *view*) |

**The *dispositionalist* *thesis*** (Bird 2007; Mumford 1998; Ellis 2001). All *fundamental* *properties* are *dispositional*: they are *powers*, and their *nature* is *exhausted* by their *causal* *role*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Every *property* is *known* *through* its *causal* *role*.} \\
2. & \text{The *causal* *role* is *constitutive* of the *property* (there is *nothing* *more* to *know*).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{All *properties* are *dispositional*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *categoricalist* *thesis*** (Armstrong 1997; Lewis 1986). The *fundamental* *properties* are *categorical*: they are *qualitative* *features* that *ground* the *dispositions* but are *not* *exhausted* by them.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{A *disposition* is *grounded* in a *categorical* *basis*.} \\
2. & \text{The *basis* is *not* *itself* a *disposition* (otherwise the *regress* is *infinite*).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The *fundamental* *properties* are *categorical*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *“always* *packing, never* *travelling”* *objection*** (Blackburn 1990). If all *properties* are *dispositional*, the *world* is a *world* of *pure* *powers* with *no* *categorical* *basis* — a *world* of *“always* *packing, never* *travelling.”* The *objection* is *rhetorical*, but it *points* to a *real* *problem*: *what* *is* *the* *power* *power* *to* *do*, *if* *not* *to* *produce* a *categorical* *effect*?

**(ii) The *“no* *necessary* *connections”* *objection*** (Lewis 1986; Humean *tradition*). The *dispositionalist* *holds* that *properties* *necessitate* their *effects*, and the *necessitation* is *not* *explained* by *the* *Humean* *picture* (Ch. 18, §5). The *dispositionalist* *reply*: the *necessitation* is *real*, and *Humeanism* is *false*.

**(iii) The *“circularity”* *objection***. The *dispositionalist* *defines* a *property* by its *effects*, and the *effects* are *defined* by their *causes*. The *definition* is *circular*, and the *circle* is *not* *broken*.

**The *state* of the debate.** The *debate* is *unresolved*, and it is *connected* to the *debate* about *laws* (Ch. 18, §5; Ch. 39, §1) and about *modality* (Ch. 16). The *most* *defensible* *position* is a *structural* *realism* that *combines* the *dispositionalist* *insight* (properties are *causal* *roles*) with the *categoricalist* *insight* (the *roles* are *grounded* in *something*).

### 5.4 The state of the debate

| Position | Core claim | Virtue | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Transcendent realism** | Universals exist independently | Explains *uninstantiated* properties | Profligate; third-man regress |
| **Immanent realism** | Universals in particulars | Parsimonious; truthmaker | Instantiation regress |
| **Trope theory** | Properties are particulars | Locates properties; avoids regress | Compresence regress; pairing |
| **Predicate nominalism** | Properties are words | Simplest | Circular |
| **Class nominalism** | Properties are sets | Reduces to sets | Coextensive properties |
| **Resemblance nominalism** | Properties are resemblances | Avoids universals | Imperfect community; regress |
| **Naturalness** | Some properties are primitive | Explains similarity, law, reference | Primitive; no definition |
| **Dispositionalism** | All properties are powers | Causal role is constitutive | No categorical basis |
| **Categoricalism** | Fundamental properties are categorical | Grounds dispositions | The *nature* of the categorical is *mysterious* |

The *table* is a *map*, not a *verdict*. The *reader* who wants to *choose* a *position* must decide which *cost* is *acceptable* — and the *decision* is a *philosophical* *argument*.

## 6. Abstract Objects

### 6.1 The distinction

The *distinction* between **abstract** and **concrete** *objects* is *central* to the *debate* about *properties* (which are *abstract* on the *transcendent* *view*, *concrete* on the *trope* *view*).

**Lewis’s *two* *ways* of *drawing* the *distinction*** (*On the Plurality of Worlds*, 1986, §1.7).

**(i) The way of negation.** An *object* is *abstract* iff it *lacks* *spatiotemporal* *location* and *causal* *powers*. (Numbers, sets, propositions, *universals* on the *transcendent* *view*.)

**(ii) The way of abstraction.** An *object* is *abstract* iff it is *arrived* *at* by *abstraction* *from* *concrete* *particulars*. (Properties, *kinds*, *relations*.)

**The *examples*.**

| Object | Abstract? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A *table* | No | Located; causal |
| The *number* 7 | Yes | Not located; not causal |
| The *property* of *redness* | Yes (transcendent) / No (trope) | Depends on the *view* |
| A *proposition* | Yes | Not located |
| A *set* | Yes | Not located |
| A *possible* *world* | Yes (ersatz) / No (Lewis) | Depends on the *view* |
| A *soul* | (Contested) | (Contested) |

**The *problem*.** The *distinction* is *not* *sharp*: *some* *objects* are *borderline* (the *self*? *institutions*? *fictional* *characters*?), and the *borderline* *cases* *raise* the *question* of *whether* the *distinction* is *a* *difference* *in* *kind* or a *difference* *in* *degree*.

### 6.2 Benacerraf's dilemma

The *most* *famous* *problem* for *abstract* *objects* is **Benacerraf’s dilemma** (“Mathematical Truth,” 1973), which was *introduced* in Ch. 9, §7.2 and is *developed* here.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Mathematical *truths* are *about* *abstract* *objects* (the *semantic* *premise*).} \\
2. & \text{Knowledge *requires* a *causal* *connection* *between* the *knower* and the *known* (the *causal* *theory* of *knowledge*).} \\
3. & \text{Abstract *objects* are *causally* *inert*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Mathematical *knowledge* is *impossible*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *dilemma*.** Either the *semantics* is *realist* (the *truths* are *about* *abstract* *objects*) or the *epistemology* is *naturalistic* (knowledge *requires* *causal* *contact*). The *two* are *incompatible*, and *each* is *plausible*.

**The *solutions*.**

**(i) Platonism with a non-causal epistemology.** *Abstract* *knowledge* is *a* *priori* (Ch. 9), and the *a* *priori* *does* not *require* *causal* *contact*. The *solution* *rejects* *premise* 2, and the *rejection* is *contested*: the *a* *priori* *is* *itself* *mysterious* (Ch. 9, §3.6).

**(ii) Structuralism.** Mathematics is *about* *structures*, and the *structures* are *instantiated* in *concrete* *systems*. The *access* is *through* the *concrete* *instances*. The *solution* *rejects* *premise* 1, and the *rejection* is *contested* (§7).

**(iii) Nominalism.** Mathematics is *about* *concrete* *things* (or is a *fiction*). The *solution* *rejects* *premise* 1, and the *rejection* is *contested* (Ch. 14, §5).

**(iv) Indispensability.** Mathematics is *indispensable* to *science*, so we are *committed* to its *objects*, and the *epistemic* *access* is *through* *science*. The *solution* *rejects* *premise* 2, and the *rejection* is *contested* (Ch. 14, §3.4).

**The *state* of the debate.** The *dilemma* is *unresolved*, and it is the *deepest* *problem* in the *philosophy* of *mathematics* (Ch. 43, §1). The *contemporary* *consensus* is that *some* *form* of *structuralism* or *naturalism* is the *best* *option*, but the *consensus* is *not* *unanimous*.

### 6.3 Propositions

**Propositions** are the *objects* of *belief*, *desire*, and *assertion*. They are *abstract* on *most* *views*, and the *debate* about their *nature* is a *test* *case* for the *debate* about *abstracta*.

**The *three* *main* *views*.

| View | What a proposition is | Proponents | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Structured** | A *complex* of *objects*, *properties*, and *relations* | Russell; Soames; King | The *unity* problem; the *Frege* puzzle |
| **Sets of worlds** | A *set* of *possible* *worlds* | Lewis; Stalnaker | The *coarse-grained* *individuation* |
| **Fregean senses** | A *mode* of *presentation* | Frege; Church | The *nature* of *senses* is *mysterious* |

**The *unity* *problem*** (for the *structured* *view*). If a proposition is a *complex* of *constituents*, what *binds* the *constituents* *together*? The *binding* is a *relation*, and the *relation* *requires* a *further* *binding*, and so on. (This is *Bradley’s* *regress* again, and it is *one* of the *deepest* *problems* in the *philosophy* of *language*.)

**The *coarse-grained* *problem*** (for the *sets-of-worlds* *view*). If a *proposition* is a *set* of *worlds*, then *all* *necessary* *truths* are *the* *same* *proposition* (the *set* of *all* *worlds*). The *consequence* is *counterintuitive*: “$2+2=4$” and “Fermat’s Last Theorem” *express* the *same* *proposition*. The *reply* (Stalnaker 1976) is to *add* a *level* of *structure*, and the *reply* *reintroduces* the *fine-grained* *view*.

### 6.4 Numbers and sets

**The *two* *paradigms* of *abstract* *objects* are *numbers* and *sets*. The *debate* about them is the *test* *case* for the *realism/nominalism* *debate*.

**Numbers.**

- **Frege’s *theorem*** (Boolos 1987; Wright 1983; Hale and Wright 2001): *arithmetic* can be *derived* from *Hume’s* *principle* ($\#F = \#G \leftrightarrow F \approx G$) plus *second-order* *logic*. The *result* is *neo-logicism*: the *foundations* of *arithmetic* are *logical* (in a *broad* *sense*), and the *numbers* are *abstract* *objects*.
- **The *Caesar* *problem*** (Frege 1884, §56; Benacerraf 1965): *Hume’s* *principle* *does* not *tell* us *whether* *Julius* *Caesar* *is* a *number*. The *principle* *fixes* the *identity* *conditions* of *numbers*, but it does *not* *fix* their *nature*.
- **Structuralism** (§7) is the *most* *influential* *response*: numbers are *positions* in *structures*, and the *question* of *what* a *number* *is* *in* *itself* is *misplaced*.

**Sets.**

- **The *iterative* *conception*** (Boolos 1971; Shoenfield 1977): sets are *formed* in *stages*, and the *universe* of sets is *cumulative*. The *conception* *avoids* the *paradoxes* (Russell, Burali-Forti) by *denying* that there is a *set* of all sets.
- **The *problem*.** The *universe* of sets is *not* a *set*, and the *question* of *what* the *universe* is *raises* the *question* of the *ontology* of *proper* *classes*. (Ch. 43, §7.)

### 6.5 The state of the debate

The *debate* about *abstract* *objects* is *unresolved*, and it is *one* of the *most* *technical* and *most* *consequential* *areas* of *metaphysics*. The *stakes* are *high*: the *answer* *determines* the *ontology* of *mathematics*, *logic*, *language*, and *modality*.

**The *most* *defensible* *position***. A *qualified* *realism*: *abstract* *objects* *exist*, but their *nature* is *structural* (they are *positions* in *structures*), and their *epistemology* is *structural* (we *know* them *through* the *structures* we *instantiate*). The *position* *combines* the *realist* *intuition* with the *structuralist* *insight*, and it *avoids* the *worst* *consequences* of *both* *platonism* and *nominalism*.

## 7. Structuralism

### 7.1 Mathematical structuralism

**Structuralism** is the *view* that *mathematics* is *about* *structures*, not *objects*. The *numbers* are *positions* in the *structure* of *arithmetic*; the *points* are *positions* in the *structure* of *geometry*; the *sets* are *positions* in the *structure* of *set* *theory*.

**The *statement*.** Benacerraf (“What Numbers Could Not Be,” 1965); Shapiro (*Philosophy of Mathematics: Structure and Ontology*, 1997); Resnik (*Mathematics as a Science of Patterns*, 1997).

**The *forms*.**

| Form | What a structure is | Where it is | Proponents |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ante rem** | A *universal* — a *pattern* that *exists* *independently* | In a *Platonic* *realm* | Shapiro; Parsons |
| **In rebus** | A *pattern* *instantiated* in *concrete* *systems* | In the *systems* | Resnik; Hellman (modal) |
| **Eliminative** | *Talk* of *structures* is *talk* of *systems*; no *abstract* *structures* | — | the *nominalists* |

**Benacerraf’s *argument*** (“What Numbers Could Not Be,” 1965). The *natural* *numbers* can be *identified* with the *von* *Neumann* *ordinals* or the *Zermelo* *ordinals*; the *two* *identifications* are *equally* *adequate* to *arithmetic*; the *choice* *between* them is *arbitrary*; therefore *numbers* are *not* *particular* *objects* — they are *positions* in a *structure*.

**The *slogan*.** “Arithmetic is *about* the *structure*, not *about* the *objects* that *occupy* the *positions*.”

### 7.2 Ontic structuralism

The *most* *radical* *form* of *structuralism* is **ontic* *structuralism*** (French 2014; Ladyman and Ross 2007), which is the *view* that *there* *are* *no* *objects* *over* *and* *above* *the* *structure*.

**The *thesis*.** The *fundamental* *level* of *reality* is a *structure*; the *“objects”* that *appear* in *physics* are *nodes* in the *structure*, not *independent* *entities*. There is *no* *intrinsic* *nature* to the *nodes*; their *identity* is *exhausted* by their *relations*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Physics *describes* the *world* *in* *terms* of *relations* (quantum *entanglement*, gauge *symmetry*, *spacetime* *structure*).} \\
2. & \text{The *relations* *do* not *presuppose* *relata* with *intrinsic* *natures* (the *entanglement* *relations* are *prior* to the *particles*).} \\
3. & \text{The *best* *interpretation* of *physics* *treats* the *structure* as *fundamental*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{There are *no* *objects* *over* *and* *above* the *structure*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *connection* to *Ch. 19***. Onic structuralism is the *metaphysical* *form* of *structural* *realism* about *science* (Ch. 19, §6). The *two* *are* *connected*: the *scientific* *realist* *argues* that *science* *gives* us *knowledge* of *structure*, not of *intrinsic* *natures*; the *ontic* *structuralist* *argues* that the *structure* is *all* *there* *is*.

**The *objection*.** The *view* *is* *unstable*: a *structure* *without* *objects* is *not* a *structure*; it is a *pure* *form* with *no* *content*. (The *reply*: the *form* *is* the *content*; the *distinction* *between* *form* and *content* is a *legacy* of the *substance* *ontology* that *structuralism* *rejects*.)

**The *state* of the debate.** Onic structuralism is a *minority* *position*, but it is *taken* *seriously*, and it is *connected* to the *most* *active* *areas* of *contemporary* *metaphysics* (the *philosophy* of *physics*, the *metaphysics* of *relations*, the *debate* about *fundamentality*).

### 7.3 The connection to the rest of the book

Structuralism is the *most* *important* *recent* *development* in the *debate* about *universals*, and it is *connected* to the *rest* of the *book* in *four* *ways*.

**(i) The *ontology* of *mathematics***. Structuralism is the *most* *influential* *response* to *Benacerraf’s* *dilemma* (Ch. 43, §3).

**(ii) The *philosophy* of *science***. Structural *realism* (Ch. 19, §6) is the *scientific* *counterpart* of *ontic* *structuralism*, and the *two* *are* *mutually* *supporting*.

**(iii) The *theory* of *properties***. Armstrong’s *structural* *realism* (§4.4) is a *form* of *structuralism*: the *universals* *have* their *natures* *in* *virtue* of their *positions* in the *structure*.

**(iv) The *theory* of *reference***. The *structuralist* *view* *challenges* the *descriptivist* and *causal* *theories* of *reference* (Ch. 12, §3): if *objects* *have* *no* *intrinsic* *natures*, the *reference* to them *cannot* *be* *grounded* in *descriptions* or in *causal* *chains* *that* *presuppose* *intrinsic* *natures*.

## 8. The State of the Debate

### 8.1 What is settled

- **The one-over-many problem is real.** The *similarity* *among* *things* *requires* an *explanation*, and the *explanation* is the *subject* of the *debate*.
- **The third-man regress is a serious objection to transcendent realism.** The *immanent* *realists* *have* *replaced* the *transcendent* *picture*, and the *replacement* is *motivated* by the *regress*.
- **Nominalism is not refuted, but it is on the defensive.** The *three* *forms* *each* *have* *serious* *costs*, and the *costs* *are* *not* *eliminated*.
- **Trope theory is a genuine third way.** It *splits* the *difference* between *realism* and *nominalism*, and it *avoids* the *worst* *consequences* of *both*.
- **Naturalness is indispensable.** The *notion* *does* *explanatory* *work* *that* *no* *rival* *does*, and it is *the* *most* *important* *development* in the *contemporary* *debate*.
- **Benacerraf’s dilemma is unresolved.** The *access* *problem* is the *deepest* *problem* in the *philosophy* of *mathematics*, and the *solutions* are *all* *costly*.

### 8.2 What is open

- **The *nature* of *properties*.** Are they *universals*, *tropes*, *classes*, or *resemblances*?
- **The *status* of *naturalness*.** Is it *primitive*, *reducible*, or *illusory*?
- **The *disposition/categorical* *distinction*.** Are all *fundamental* *properties* *powers*, or are some *categorical*?
- **The *ontology* of *abstracta*.** Are *numbers*, *propositions*, and *sets* *real*, and if so, *how* do we *know* them?
- **The *status* of *structuralism*.** Is the *structure* *fundamental*, or is it *grounded* in *objects*?
- **The *relation* between *structure* and *reference*.** How do we *refer* to *positions* in *structures*?

### 8.3 The methodological lesson

The chapter is a *case* *study* in the *method* of Part I.

- **Conceptual analysis** (§1 of Ch. 3) generated the *distinction* between *universals* and *particulars*.
- **Thought experiments** (§2 of Ch. 3) — *Twin* *Earth*, the *cordate/renate* *case*, the *round* *square* — are the *engine* of the debate.
- **Formalization** (§3 of Ch. 3) — the *quantifier* *analysis*, the *set-theoretic* *reduction* — is *essential* for the *criterion*.
- **Reflective equilibrium** (§4 of Ch. 3) is the method by which the *intuitions* and the *theories* are adjusted.
- **Negative capability** (§7 of Ch. 3) is the *response* to the *residue*: the *humility* to hold the *question* open.

The *honest* verdict: the *debate* about *universals* is a *field* in *transition*. The *transcendent* *realism* is *untenable*; the *immanent* *realism* is *dominant* but *incomplete*; the *nominalist* *alternative* is *live*; the *trope* *theory* is *serious*; the *structuralist* *turn* is the *most* *important* *development*. The *unresolved* character is *instructive*: it shows what philosophical progress looks like — not a *settled answer*, but a *sharper understanding* of what the *question* is.

### 8.4 The dialectical map

| Position | Core claim | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Transcendent realism** | Universals exist independently | Explains *uninstantiated* properties | Third-man regress; profligate |
| **Immanent realism** | Universals in particulars | Parsimonious; truthmaker | Instantiation regress |
| **Trope theory** | Properties are particulars | Locates properties; avoids regress | Compresence; pairing |
| **Predicate nominalism** | Properties are words | Simplest | Circular |
| **Class nominalism** | Properties are sets | Reduces to sets | Coextensive properties |
| **Resemblance nominalism** | Properties are resemblances | Avoids universals | Imperfect community; regress |
| **Naturalness** | Some properties are primitive | Explains similarity, law, reference | Primitive; no definition |
| **Dispositionalism** | All properties are powers | Causal role is constitutive | No categorical basis |
| **Structuralism** | Properties are positions in structures | Explains mathematics; unifies | No intrinsic natures |

The *table* is a *map*, not a *verdict*. The *reader* who wants to *choose* a *position* must decide which *cost* is *acceptable* — and the *decision* is a *philosophical* *argument*.

## Optional threads

Cross-cutting material, for the reader who wants to go deeper.

**1. The *problem* of *uninstantiated* *properties*.** The *transcendent* *realist* *accepts* them; the *immanent* *realist* *denies* them; the *nominalist* *denies* *properties* *altogether*. The *problem* is *connected* to the *ontology* of *modality* (Ch. 16) and to the *theory* of *fiction* (Ch. 49, §4). The *contemporary* *debate* has *shifted* from “are there *uninstantiated* properties?” to “what *work* do *uninstantiated* properties *do*?” — and the *answer* *depends* on the *theory* of *modality*.

**2. The *problem* of *individuation*.** What *makes* a *property* *the* *property* it is? The *categoricalist* says its *qualitative* *nature*; the *dispositionalist* says its *causal* *role*; the *structuralist* says its *position* in the *structure*. The *debate* is *connected* to the *debate* about *essence* (Ch. 16, §3) and to the *debate* about *identity* (Ch. 26).

**3. The *metaphysics* of *relations*.** Are *relations* *real*? Are they *universals*? Are they *reducible* to *monadic* *properties*? The *debate* is *connected* to the *theory* of *structuralism* (§7), to the *philosophy* of *physics* (Ch. 19), and to the *theory* of *properties*. The *relationalist* *view* (which *holds* that *relations* are *fundamental*) is *one* of the *most* *active* *areas* of *contemporary* *metaphysics*.

**4. Non-Western *theories* of *universals*.** The *Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika* *school* *developed* a *realist* *theory* of *universals* (*jāti*) that *distinguishes* *real* *universals* from *nominal* *ones* by *five* *conditions* (the *jāti-bādhaka*), and the *Buddhist* *apoha* *theory* *developed* a *nominalist* *alternative* (the *meaning* of a *word* is the *exclusion* of *others*). The *two* *theories* *anticipate* the *realist/nominalist* *debate*, and they *are* *systematic* *alternatives* (Matilal 1986; Ganeri 2001; the *essays* in *The Oxford Handbook of Indian Philosophy*, 2017).

**5. The *ethics* of *properties*.** The *choice* of *properties* is *not* *purely* *metaphysical*: the *classification* of *people* by *race*, *gender*, or *disability* *turns* on the *question* of *which* *properties* are *natural* (Ch. 33, §5; Ch. 11, §4). The *metaphysics* of *naturalness* is *entangled* with the *politics* of *classification*, and the *entanglement* is *one* of the *most* *urgent* *issues* in *contemporary* *philosophy*.

## Summary

- **The one-over-many problem** is the *source* of the *debate* about universals. The *realist* *explains* *similarity* by *shared* *universals*; the *nominalist* *explains* it by *words*, *classes*, or *resemblances*; the *trope* *theorist* *explains* it by *particular* *properties*.
- **Russell’s Platonism** is *transcendent* realism: universals exist *independently* of their instances, and they are *known* by *acquaintance*. The **third-man regress** is the *most* *serious* *objection*, and Russell *does* not *resolve* it.
- **Nominalism** has *three* *forms*: predicate (words), class (sets), and resemblance (relations). Each *has* *serious* *costs*: predicate is *circular*; class loses the *modal* *profile*; resemblance *struggles* with *imperfect* *communities* and the *regress*.
- **Trope theory** is the *third way*: properties are *particulars*, and the *similarity* *among* *tropes* is *primitive*. The **compresence regress** and the **pairing problem** are the *main* *costs*.
- **Realism about universals** divides into *transcendent* (Platonic) and *immanent* (Aristotelian). Armstrong’s **truthmaker argument** is the *strongest* *positive* *case*, and the **instantiation regress** (Bradley) is the *strongest* *objection*. Armstrong’s **structural realism** is the *most* *ambitious* *attempt* to *reconcile* realism with structuralism.
- **Naturalness** (Lewis) is the *most* *influential* *contemporary* *notion*: some *properties* are *primitive* and *carve* *nature* at its *joints*. The *notion* *explains* *similarity*, *law*, *induction*, and *reference* (**reference magnetism**), and it is *contested* because it is *primitive*.
- **The disposition/categorical debate** is the *most* *consequential* *debate* about *properties*: are *fundamental* *properties* *powers* (dispositionalism) or *qualitative* *features* (categoricalism)? The *debate* is *connected* to *laws* (Ch. 18) and *modality* (Ch. 16).
- **Abstract objects** are *the* *test* *case* for *ontology*. **Benacerraf’s dilemma** is the *deepest* *problem*: the *semantics* *requires* *abstracta*, the *epistemology* *resists* them. **Propositions** are *structured*, *sets* *of* *worlds*, or *senses*. **Numbers** are *positions* in *structures* (**structuralism**).
- **Structuralism** is the *most* *important* *recent* *development*: mathematics is *about* structures, not objects. **Ontic structuralism** is the *radical* *form*: there are *no* *objects* *over* *and* *above* the *structure*. The *view* is *connected* to *scientific* *realism* (Ch. 19) and to the *theory* of *reference* (Ch. 12).

The result is *not* a *settled* *ontology* of *properties*, and it is *not* a *refutation* of *realism*. It is a *clarification*: the *debate* is *not* about *whether* there are *properties* (everyone *agrees* that *red* *things* *resemble* one another); it is about *what* *best* *explains* the *similarity*, and the *explanation* is a *comparative* *judgment* among *explanatory packages*.

The *next* *chapter* *examines* the *modality* that *underlies* *all* of these *debates*, and it *asks* the *question* that the *properties* *debate* *presupposes*: *what* *is* *necessity*, and *how* do we *know* it?
