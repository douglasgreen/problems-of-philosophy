# Chapter 9 — Intuition, the A Priori, and the Analytic

> “All knowledge of general principles is derived from experience; but … the principles themselves are not derived from experience. … We are compelled to admit that some general principles are known to us by intuition.”
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Ch. VII (paraphrased from the argument)

> “The truth of the analytic-synthetic distinction cannot be settled by appeal to the meaning of ‘analytic.’”
> — W. V. O. Quine, “Two Dogmas of Empiricism” (1951), in effect the whole paper

---

Chapter 8 followed the regress of justification down to its stopping points, and at every stopping point it found the same unresolved question: **what are the reasons made of?** The foundationalist pointed to basic beliefs; the coherentist pointed to the system; the reliabilist pointed to the process. But a basic belief is basic only if its justification is *immediate* — and immediacy is the traditional mark of **intuition**. The regress ends, if it ends, in the intuitive.

This chapter examines intuition, and the two notions that have travelled with it for four centuries: the **a priori** and the **analytic**. The three are not the same, but they have been entangled since Kant, and the entanglements are the subject of the chapter.

The chapter has a distinctive shape, because the territory has been *contested twice*. The first contest was Kant's: how can there be synthetic a priori knowledge? The second was Quine's: is the analytic/synthetic distinction coherent at all? The first contest produced the modern conception of the a priori; the second nearly destroyed it. The chapter's argument is that both contests were genuine, that neither was decisively won, and that the contemporary settlement — a *moderate* revival of both notions, with a *thin* conception of the a priori — is the best we have.

We proceed in eight movements. §1 recovers Russell's account, and the crisis it generated. §2 takes up the analytic/synthetic distinction, from Kant to Quine to the moderate revival. §3 examines the a priori directly: what it is, and whether anything answers to it. §4 takes up intuition as evidence, and the experimental challenge. §5 examines modality, and Kripke's revolution. §6 examines rational insight, self-evidence, and the problem of disagreement. §7 takes up the mathematics case, which is the test case for the whole chapter. §8 gives an honest verdict.

## 1. Russell and the Crisis of Intuition

### 1.1 The setting: Russell's epistemology of principles

Russell’s *Problems of Philosophy* is built on a distinction between two kinds of knowledge: **knowledge by acquaintance** and **knowledge by description** (Ch. 12, §1). Acquaintance is direct awareness of a thing — a sense-datum, a memory, an introspected state, a universal. Description is knowledge *that* something is the case, which is analysed into acquaintance with the constituents plus a general principle that connects them.

The analysis generates an obvious question: **where do the general principles come from?** If all knowledge *that* is analysed into acquaintance plus principles, then the principles themselves must be known somehow. Russell’s answer, in Chs. VII–VIII and XI, is that they are known by **intuition**.

### 1.2 Russell’s account of intuitive knowledge

Russell distinguishes two kinds of self-evidence:

| Type | Example | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Intuitive self-evidence** | The proposition that $2+2=4$; that red is a colour; that if $A$ is before $B$ and $B$ before $C$, then $A$ is before $C$ | Immediate; we *see* that it is true |
| **Derivative self-evidence** | The conclusions of a proof | Mediate; we see that the conclusion follows, given the premises |

Intuitive self-evidence is the foundation. Russell’s claim (Ch. XI) is that there is a class of propositions that are “self-evident” in the sense that, when we understand them, we cannot doubt them — not because of psychological compulsion, but because the understanding *is* the justification.

Russell is careful: self-evidence is **fallible**. “It is not always easy to know whether we have self-evident knowledge or not” (*Problems*, Ch. XI). The fallibility is important, because it is the seed of the crisis.

### 1.3 The principles Russell identifies

Russell (Ch. VII) lists the principles that he thinks are known by intuition:

1. **The principle of induction** — that the future will resemble the past, or that observed regularities will continue (Ch. 10, §1).
2. **The principle of generalisation** — that if a property is observed in a number of instances, it may be projected to unobserved instances.
3. **The principle of the uniformity of nature** — that the laws of nature are constant.
4. **The principles of logic** — the laws of identity, non-contradiction, excluded middle.
5. **The principles of mathematics** — the axioms of arithmetic, the laws of geometry.
6. **The principle of the reality of relations** — that relations are real, and not reducible to properties (Ch. 15).

The list is heterogeneous, and that is the problem. Some of the principles (the laws of logic) seem to be **conceptual truths** — they are true in virtue of the meanings of the logical constants. Others (the principle of induction) seem to be **empirical** — they are confirmed by experience, not by intuition. Others (the axioms of geometry) have turned out to be **false** of the actual world (non-Euclidean geometry), or to be **conventional** (the choice of axioms).

The heterogeneity is the first sign of the crisis: *intuition* is being asked to do too many different jobs, and the jobs have different credentials.

### 1.4 The crisis

The crisis has three sources.

**(i) The fallibility of intuition.** Russell admits that intuition can be mistaken. But if intuition can be mistaken, how do we know when it is correct? The answer cannot be "by intuition," because the same faculty is in question. The fallibility generates a **calibration problem** (Ch. 3, §2.4): we need a story about when intuition is reliable, and the story cannot be given from within the faculty.

**(ii) The variability of intuition.** Different cultures, different epochs, and different individuals have different intuitions. The history of philosophy is a graveyard of intuitions that were once self-evident and are now rejected (the natural slavery of Aristotle, the inferiority of women, the impossibility of non-Euclidean geometry). The variability is evidence that intuition is not a pure faculty of reason, but a product of history and culture.

**(iii) The experimental philosophy challenge.** The modern form of (ii) is empirical: the experimental literature (Ch. 3, §6.3) claims to show that intuitions vary with culture, order, framing, and affect. If the intuition that $p$ is sensitive to factors that are irrelevant to the truth of $p$, then the intuition is not reliable evidence for $p$.

The crisis is not that intuition is worthless. It is that intuition has been asked to bear more weight than it can carry. Russell’s epistemology is *intuitionist* in the sense that it treats intuition as the foundation of all knowledge — and the foundation has turned out to be shaky.

### 1.5 The modern form of the problem

The modern debate has separated three questions that Russell ran together.

| Question | Form | Domain |
|---|---|---|
| **The psychological question** | When do we have an intuition? What causes it? | Cognitive science |
| **The epistemic question** | Does the intuition justify the belief? Is it evidence? | Epistemology |
| **The semantic question** | What is the content of the intuition? Is it propositional? | Philosophy of mind and language |

Russell conflated them: he assumed that an intuition that seems compelling is (i) caused by rational insight, (ii) justifies the belief, and (iii) has propositional content that is self-evident. Each assumption is contestable, and the contemporary debate is largely the attempt to disentangle them.

The separation is the methodological move that structures the rest of the chapter. A failure at the psychological level (the intuition is caused by bias) does not entail a failure at the semantic level (the proposition has no content). And a success at the epistemic level (the intuition is a justification) does not entail a success at the semantic level (the content is self-evident).

## 2. The Analytic and the Synthetic

### 2.1 Kant's distinction

The distinction is Kant's, and it is stated most clearly in the *Prolegomena* (§2) and the *Critique of Pure Reason* (A6–9/B10–14).

**The definitions.**

- **Analytic judgment**: a judgment in which the predicate is *contained* in the concept of the subject. (“All bodies are extended.”)
- **Synthetic judgment**: a judgment in which the predicate *adds* to the concept of the subject. (“All bodies are heavy.”)

**The criterion.** Kant offers a *conceptual containment* criterion, and a *negation* test: an analytic judgment is one whose negation is self-contradictory (that is, an analytic truth is a logical truth in the broad sense).

**The taxonomy.**

|  | **A priori** | **A posteriori** |
|---|---|---|
| **Analytic** | Analytic a priori (“All bachelors are unmarried”) | (Empty) |
| **Synthetic** | Synthetic a priori (“$7+5=12$”; “Every event has a cause”) | Synthetic a posteriori (“The cat is on the mat”) |

Kant’s question is about the **synthetic a priori**: how is it possible? His answer — that the mind imposes the forms of intuition and the categories on experience — is the transcendental deduction, and it is the foundation of his system.

The **problem with the containment metaphor** is that it is a metaphor. What is "containment"? A spatial relation between concepts? Kant does not say, and the metaphor has been the target of criticism from Frege onward. The criterion is also circular: the negation test uses logical truth, and logical truth uses analyticity.

### 2.2 Frege and the logicist analysis

Frege (*Foundations of Arithmetic*, 1884, §§3–4) replaced Kant's containment metaphor with a **logical** criterion.

**The definitions.**

- **Analytic**: a truth that is derivable from logical laws plus definitions.
- **Synthetic**: a truth that is not so derivable.
- **A priori**: a truth whose justification does not depend on experience.

Frege's criterion is more precise than Kant's, but it has a different extension. Frege's logicism — the thesis that arithmetic is reducible to logic — makes arithmetic analytic in Frege's sense but synthetic (and a priori) in Kant's sense. This is the "Frege–Kant" disagreement, and it is the first sign that "analytic" is a cluster concept, not a single notion.

Frege's most important contribution to the debate is the **context principle** (*Grundlagen*, §62): "never to ask for the meaning of a word in isolation, but only in the context of a proposition." The principle is the foundation of the truth-conditional theory of meaning (Ch. 31, §3), and it is also the seed of the later *semantic* conception of analyticity: a sentence is analytic if it is true in virtue of meaning alone.

### 2.3 The logical positivists

The **logical positivists** (Carnap, Ayer, Schlick) made the analytic/synthetic distinction the cornerstone of their philosophy. Their programme:

- **Analytic truths** are true in virtue of meaning; they are **tautologies**; they say nothing about the world.
- **Synthetic truths** are empirical; they are verified or falsified by experience.
- The **verification principle**: a sentence is meaningful only if it is analytic or empirically verifiable.
- The **rejection of the synthetic a priori**: there is no such thing; the propositions Kant thought were synthetic a priori are either analytic (mathematics and logic) or empirical (the principles of science).

The positivist programme is the high-water mark of the analytic. It is also the point at which the distinction begins to break down, because the verification principle is itself neither analytic nor empirically verifiable. (This is the "self-refutation" problem, and it was the beginning of the end for positivism.)

### 2.4 Quine's attack

Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) is the most influential attack on the analytic/synthetic distinction in the twentieth century. It has two parts.

**Part one: the attack on analyticity.**

Quine surveys the attempts to define "analytic," and argues that they all fail.

1. **The definitional route**: an analytic sentence is one that is true by definition. But definition presupposes synonymy, and synonymy is as obscure as analyticity.
2. **The interchangeability route**: two expressions are synonymous if they are interchangeable *salva veritate* (without change of truth value) in all contexts. But interchangeability fails in intensional contexts (belief, necessity), and the restriction to extensional contexts is circular.
3. **The semantic-rule route**: an analytic sentence is one that is true by the semantic rules of the language. But the semantic rules are themselves a fiction: they are a description of the language, not a constraint on it, and the description is as holistic as the language itself.

**The conclusion.** There is no *non-circular* way to define analyticity. The concept is, in Quine's phrase, "a metaphysical article of faith."

**Part two: the attack on reductionism.**

The second dogma is **reductionism**: the thesis that every meaningful sentence is equivalent to a sentence about immediate experience (the "verification theory of meaning"). Quine argues that reductionism is false: our beliefs form a **web** (Ch. 1, §5 of *Two Dogmas*: "a man-made fabric which impinges on experience only along the edges"), and no individual sentence is confirmed or disconfirmed by experience in isolation. The unit of empirical significance is the *whole of science*.

**The consequence.** If the unit of significance is the whole, then the "analytic" sentences are simply the ones that are *central* to the web — the ones we are most reluctant to give up. There is no sharp line between the analytic and the synthetic; there is a **continuum** of entrenchment.

**The famous image.** "Any statement can be held true come what may, if we make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere in the system." (Quine 1951, p. 43.) The logical laws themselves are revisable: "even the law of excluded middle … could be given up" if the adjustment were worth it. (Quine is thinking of quantum logic, and of the revisionary proposals of the 1930s.)

### 2.5 The responses

Quine's attack was immediately contested, and the responses fall into three families.

**(i) Grice and Strawson (1956): the distinction is not *defined*, but it is *used*.** The argument: Quine sets an impossible standard — a non-circular definition of analyticity — and concludes that the distinction is incoherent. But many concepts are used without being defined (e.g., "red"), and the philosophical task is to *characterize* the distinction, not to *define* it. The distinction is marked by a *family* of criteria: synonymy, contradiction, necessity, a priority, and the behaviour of speakers. The criteria converge in paradigm cases, and the absence of a definition does not entail the absence of a distinction.

**(ii) The "epistemic" conception of analyticity (Boghossian 1996).** Boghossian distinguishes two conceptions:

- **Metaphysical analyticity**: a sentence is analytic if it is true in virtue of meaning alone (the Fregean/positivist conception).
- **Epistemic analyticity**: a sentence is analytic if *grasping its meaning is sufficient for knowing it* to be true (Boghossian's "epistemic" conception).

Boghossian argues that the epistemic conception is the one that matters for epistemology, and that it survives Quine's attack. The argument: the epistemic conception does not require a *metaphysical* notion of meaning-truth; it requires only that the *competent speaker's grasp of the meaning* is sufficient for *knowledge* of the truth. And that is a *psychological* claim that can be defended without the problematic metaphysics.

The **problem** with Boghossian's defence is that it makes analyticity depend on a *psychological* fact (the speaker's grasp of meaning), and the fact is contestable. The "grasp of meaning" may be a *capacity* that is exercised in *some* contexts and not others, and the exercise may be contaminated by empirical beliefs.

**(iii) The "moderate revival" (Jackson 1998; Chalmers 2012).** The moderate revival accepts that the *classical* analytic/synthetic distinction is untenable — the sharp line, the logical definition, the "truth in virtue of meaning alone" — but argues that the *distinction* survives in a *weaker* form.

Jackson's version: conceptual analysis is the analysis of the *possible cases* to which a concept applies, and the analysis is *a priori* in the sense that it does not require empirical investigation. The analysis is *fallible* and *revisable*, but it is not *empirical*.

Chalmers's version: the distinction is between **conceptual truths** (truths that are *a priori* and *necessary* in the *epistemic* sense) and **empirical truths** (truths that are *a posteriori*). The conceptual truths are *conditional* on our concepts: they tell us what follows from the concepts we have, not what is true of the world. The two-dimensional framework (Ch. 9, §5) is the technical machinery for the distinction.

The moderate revival is the *dominant* position in the contemporary literature, and it is the position this book adopts, with a caveat: the distinction is *real* but *fuzzy*, *useful* but not *sharp*, and *epistemic* rather than *metaphysical*.

### 2.6 The state of the analytic

The twenty-first-century position is a **qualified vindication** of the distinction, with three qualifications.

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| There is a sharp line between analytic and synthetic | **Rejected** (Quine's continuum) |
| Analytic truths are true "in virtue of meaning alone," with no contribution from the world | **Rejected** (the "metaphysical" conception is untenable) |
| There are sentences whose truth is *a priori*, *necessary*, and *conceptual* | **Accepted** (the moderate revival) |
| The distinction is *epistemic*: it marks a difference in the *kind* of warrant, not in the *kind* of truth | **Accepted** (Boghossian, Chalmers) |
| The distinction is *useful* for philosophy | **Accepted** (Jackson, Chalmers) |

The lesson: **the analytic/synthetic distinction survived Quine, but it did not survive intact.** It is a *cluster* concept, not a *definition*; it is a *continuum*, not a *line*; it is a *tool*, not a *foundation*.

## 3. The A Priori

### 3.1 The three questions

The a priori is the most contested concept in the chapter. Three questions must be separated.

| Question | Form | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| **The source question** | How is a priori knowledge *acquired*? | Reason, intuition, conceptual competence, or nothing (it is not a source but a status) |
| **The warrant question** | What *justifies* a priori knowledge? | Non-empirical warrant: self-evidence, conceptual necessity, or a structural feature of the belief |
| **The content question** | What *propositions* are knowable a priori? | Logical, mathematical, conceptual, modal, moral? |

The classical conception runs the three together: a priori knowledge is *acquired* by reason, *justified* by self-evidence, and *content*-wise it is necessary and certain. The modern debate has pried them apart.

### 3.2 The classical trinity

The classical conception, inherited from Kant and the rationalists, is a **trinity**:

$$
\text{A priori} \ = \ \text{necessary} \ = \ \text{certain}.
$$

The trinity is the source of the classical a priori's prestige, and of its vulnerability. If any one of the three fails, the others are called into question.

**Kripke's revolution** (1980) broke the trinity, and the break is the most important event in the theory of the a priori since Kant.

### 3.3 Kripke's three distinctions

Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1980) distinguishes three pairs of concepts that the classical tradition had conflated.

| Pair | Distinction | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **A priori / a posteriori** | *Epistemic*: how the truth is known | Known by reason vs. known by experience |
| **Necessary / contingent** | *Metaphysical*: whether the truth could be otherwise | True in all possible worlds vs. not |
| **Analytic / synthetic** | *Semantic*: whether the truth depends on meaning alone | True by meaning vs. true by world |

The classical trinity assumed that the three pairs coincide: the a priori is necessary and analytic; the a posteriori is contingent and synthetic. **Kripke denies the coincidence**, and the denial is the source of the "new" modal epistemology.

**The necessary a posteriori.** Kripke's examples:

1. **Hesperus is Phosphorus.** The identity is *necessary* (the two names refer to the same object, the planet Venus, in every possible world), but it was discovered *a posteriori* (by astronomical observation).
2. **Water is H₂O.** The identity is necessary (in every possible world, water is H₂O), but it was discovered empirically.
3. **This table is made of wood.** The material constitution is necessary for *this* table (it could not have been made of ice and still be this table), but it is known a posteriori.

The structure: **the identity statement is necessary because the two terms are rigid designators** (they refer to the same object in every possible world in which they refer at all), but the *discovery* of the identity is empirical. The necessity is *metaphysical*; the knowledge is *a posteriori*.

**The contingent a priori.** Kripke's examples:

1. **The standard metre.** "The standard metre is one metre long." The sentence is *contingent* (the standard metre could have been a different length, or not existed), but it is known *a priori* (by the stipulation that fixes the reference of "one metre").
2. **"I am here now."** Contingent (I could be elsewhere), but known a priori (or at least without empirical investigation).

The structure: **the truth is contingent because the reference is fixed by a contingent fact**, but the knowledge is a priori because the *stipulation* is the source of the warrant. The distinction between the *content* (contingent) and the *warrant* (a priori) is the key.

**The consequence.** The classical trinity is broken. The a priori is not the same as the necessary, and the necessary is not the same as the analytic. The three distinctions are *independent*, and the relations among them are the subject of the "new" modal epistemology.

### 3.4 The thin conception of the a priori

The contemporary consensus is a **thin** conception: the a priori is not a *faculty* or a *source*, but a **status** of certain beliefs.

| Thick conception (classical) | Thin conception (contemporary) |
|---|---|
| A special faculty of reason | A structural feature of certain beliefs |
| Infallible and certain | Fallible and revisable |
| Necessary and analytic | Independent of the *kind* of necessity |
| The foundation of knowledge | One kind of warrant among others |

The thin conception has three components.

**(i) Negative characterisation.** A belief is a priori if its *warrant does not depend on experience*. This is a *negative* characterisation, and it is deliberately minimal. It does not say *how* the belief is warranted; it says only that the warrant is *not empirical*.

**(ii) The warrant is *structural*.** The warrant is *internal* to the belief — it is a feature of the *content* or the *concept* or the *inference*, not a feature of the *world*. The warrant is *a priori* because it can be *seen* without *looking*.

**(iii) The warrant is *fallible*.** The belief can be *defeated* by further reflection or by empirical evidence. The a priori is not a guarantee; it is a *kind* of justification.

The **advantage** of the thin conception is that it is compatible with the naturalistic picture: the a priori is not a mysterious faculty but a *feature* of certain kinds of cognition. The **disadvantage** is that it is hard to say *what* the feature is: the "structural" warrant is a label, not an explanation.

### 3.5 The case for the a priori

The case for the a priori is the case for the *indispensability* of non-empirical warrant.

**(i) Logic.** The laws of logic cannot be justified by experience, because any empirical argument presupposes them. (This is the "logocentric predicament": to argue against logic, you must use logic.) The laws of logic are the paradigm case of a priori knowledge.

**(ii) Mathematics.** Mathematical truths are not confirmed by experience in the way that empirical truths are. The evidence for a mathematical theorem is a *proof*, not an observation. (The case is examined in §7.)

**(iii) Conceptual truths.** "All bachelors are unmarried" is *known* to be true by anyone who grasps the concepts. The knowledge is not empirical; it is *conceptual*. (The case is the descendant of the analytic.)

**(iv) Modal truths.** "It is impossible for a proposition to be both true and false" is known *a priori*. (The case is the descendant of the necessary.)

**(v) Moral truths (contested).** Some moral truths — "torturing a child for fun is wrong" — seem to be known *a priori*, or at least not by empirical investigation. (The case is contested: Ch. 44, §6.)

### 3.6 The case against the a priori

The case against the a priori is the *naturalistic* case: all knowledge is empirical, and the "a priori" is an illusion.

**(i) Quine's holism.** If all beliefs form a web, and the web is tested against experience as a whole, then no belief is *immune* from empirical revision. The a priori is a *degree* of entrenchment, not a *kind* of warrant. (This is the "no a priori" conclusion of *Two Dogmas*.)

**(ii) The empirical evidence for "rational" intuition.** Cognitive science suggests that "intuition" is a *fast, automatic, associative* process, and that it is shaped by experience. The "a priori" intuitions may be *empirical* after all — just *implicit* and *automatic*. (This is the "dual-process" challenge; Ch. 3, §6.3.)

**(iii) The history of failed a priori knowledge.** The history of philosophy is a graveyard of "a priori" claims that turned out to be false: the axioms of Euclidean geometry, the principle of sufficient reason, the "natural" inferiority of women. The failures suggest that the a priori is not a reliable source.

**(iv) The evolutionary challenge.** If our cognitive faculties are shaped by natural selection, they are adapted to the *mesocosm* — the world of medium-sized objects and everyday interactions. There is no reason to think they are reliable about the *deep* structure of reality. (This is the "evolutionary argument against" the a priori, and it is a version of the debunking challenge of Ch. 3, §5.)

### 3.7 The verdict

The debate is unresolved, but the contemporary consensus is close to the following.

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| There is a priori knowledge | **Contested**, but the majority view is yes |
| The a priori is a special faculty | **Rejected** (the thin conception) |
| The a priori is infallible | **Rejected** (fallibility is compatible with a priority) |
| The a priori is necessary | **Rejected** (Kripke: the contingent a priori) |
| The a priori is analytic | **Rejected** (the distinction between the analytic and the a priori) |
| The a priori is a *kind of warrant* | **Accepted** (the thin conception) |
| The a priori is *indispensable* | **Accepted** (the case from logic and mathematics) |

The thin conception is the best we have: it is *compatible* with naturalism, *indispensable* for logic and mathematics, and *honest* about its own fallibility.

## 4. Intuition as Evidence

### 4.1 The four accounts

Ch. 3, §2.3 presented the four accounts of intuition as evidence. Here we go deeper, because the debate about intuition is the *practical* form of the debate about the a priori.

| Account | What intuition is | Why it is evidence | Main problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conceptual competence** | The exercise of a conceptual capacity | It is the faculty by which we detect the structure of concepts | The faculty is gerrymandered; it has no clear domain |
| **Modal intuition** | A species of conceivability | Conceivability is evidence for possibility | The conceivability–possibility thesis is contested |
| **Seeming** | A propositional attitude with a distinctive phenomenology | Seemings are default justified | The view is too permissive |
| **Deflationism** | A suppressed argument | The warrant derives from the argument, not the intuition | It cannot explain the *particularity* of case intuitions |

The choice among these is the choice among the *epistemic* models of intuition, and it is unresolved. The most defensible position is a **pluralism**: different intuitions may have different warrants, and the model should be *local*, not *global*.

### 4.2 The experimental challenge

The **experimental philosophy** challenge (Ch. 3, §6.3) is the empirical form of the *variability* objection. The findings:

| Effect | Description | Study | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Framing** | Different descriptions of the same case elicit different judgments | Petrinovich & O'Neill 1996; Greene et al. 2001 | Robust |
| **Order** | Presentation order affects judgment | Schwitzgebel & Cushman 2012 | Mixed |
| **Affect** | Emotional salience affects judgment | Schnall et al. 2008 | Contested (replication) |
| **Culture** | Cross-cultural variation in Gettier intuitions | Weinberg et al. 2001; Machery et al. 2004 | Contested |
| **Expertise** | Experts differ from novices | Knobe & Nichols 2008 | Mixed |

The findings do not *refute* the method of cases, but they *complicate* it. The **calibration problem** is the central one: if intuitions vary with irrelevant factors, we need a story about *which* intuitions are reliable, and the story cannot be given from within the method.

The **responses**:

**(i) The "expertise" defence.** Philosophical intuitions are reliable only in experts, just as medical intuitions are reliable only in doctors. The defence has two problems: (a) the experts disagree, and the disagreement is not converging; (b) the "expert" intuitions are the ones that *formed* the disagreement, so the expertise defence is circular.

**(ii) The "robustness" defence.** The *paradigm* intuitions — the Gettier cases, the trolley cases — are robust across cultures and framings. The variation is in the *peripheral* cases, not the paradigms. The defence is plausible, but it is an *empirical* claim that needs to be tested.

**(iii) The "methodological" defence.** The method of cases does not require *universal* agreement; it requires *convergence among the informed*. The convergence is the evidence, not the individual intuition. The defence shifts the burden from the *intuition* to the *community*, and it is a version of the "expertise" defence.

### 4.3 The debunking challenge

A deeper challenge: even if intuitions are *stable*, they may not be *truth-tracking*. The **debunking** argument (Ch. 3, §5):

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Our intuitions are produced by a process } P. \\
2. & P \text{ is not truth-tracking (it is shaped by evolution, culture, or bias).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{Our intuitions are not evidence.}
\end{array}
$$

The argument is *local* or *global*. The **local** version: some intuitions (e.g., about moral responsibility) are shaped by processes that are not truth-tracking. The **global** version: all intuitions are shaped by evolution, and evolution does not track the deep structure of reality.

The **responses**:

**(i) The "companions in guilt" response.** The debunking argument applies equally to *perception*, *memory*, and *reason*. If it refutes intuition, it refutes all knowledge. (This is the standard response, and it is powerful, but it may prove too much: Ch. 44, §5.)

**(ii) The "local" response.** The debunking is *local*: some intuitions are debunked, others are not. The task is to *identify* the reliable ones, not to abandon the method.

**(iii) The "evolutionary" response.** Evolutionary pressures may have selected for *truth-tracking* faculties, at least in the domains that mattered for survival (Ch. 3, §5.5). The response is speculative, but it is not obviously false.

The debate is unresolved, and it is the *deepest* challenge to the method of cases. The honest position is that intuitions are *defeasible evidence*: they are *default* justified, but they can be defeated by (a) empirical evidence of unreliability, (b) a successful debunking explanation, or (c) a conflicting intuition that is more robust.

## 5. Modality and the Epistemology of Possibility

### 5.1 The varieties of necessity

Kripke's revolution (1980) made modality a central topic in analytic philosophy, and it generated a taxonomy of necessity.

| Kind of necessity | What it is | Example | Epistemology |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Logical** | True in all logically possible worlds | $p \vee \neg p$ | A priori |
| **Metaphysical** | True in all metaphysically possible worlds | Water is H₂O; Hesperus is Phosphorus | A posteriori, in some cases |
| **Nomological** | True in all worlds with the same laws of nature | Nothing travels faster than light | Empirical |
| **Conceptual** | True in virtue of the concepts | All bachelors are unmarried | A priori |
| **Mathematical** | True in all mathematical structures | $2+2=4$ | A priori |

The taxonomy is contested, and the boundaries are fuzzy. The **key question** for this chapter is: *how do we know which kind of necessity a proposition has?*

### 5.2 Kripke's essentialism

Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* revived **essentialism**: the view that objects have *essential* properties — properties that they could not lack.

**The argument.** The identity statements (Hesperus is Phosphorus; water is H₂O) are *necessary* because the terms are *rigid designators*, and the *properties* they pick out are *essential*. The necessity is *metaphysical*, not *epistemic*: it is a fact about the world, not about our concepts.

**The examples.**

- **Origin essentialism.** The table could not have been made from a different block of wood; its material origin is essential. (Kripke 1980, pp. 113–114.)
- **Kind essentialism.** Water is essentially H₂O; a substance that is not H₂O is not water, even if it looks and tastes like water. (Putnam's Twin Earth; Ch. 25, §4.1.)
- **Individual essence.** A person's origin — the sperm and egg from which she came — is essential. (Kripke 1980, pp. 110–113.)

**The epistemology.** The essentialist claims are *a posteriori*: we discover the essences by empirical investigation. The necessity is *metaphysical*, and the *knowledge* is *a posteriori*. This is the "necessary a posteriori," and it is the most important consequence of Kripke's revolution.

### 5.3 Conceivability and possibility

The **conceivability–possibility thesis** is the bridge between the *epistemic* and the *metaphysical*:

$$
(\mathrm{CP}) \quad \text{If } p \text{ is ideally conceivable, then } p \text{ is metaphysically possible.}
$$

The thesis is the engine of many philosophical arguments, including the **zombie argument** (Chalmers 1996; Ch. 24, §3): if a being physically identical to me but without consciousness is *conceivable*, then it is *possible*; if it is possible, physicalism is not necessary; if physicalism is not necessary, it is false.

**The problems.**

**(i) The "ideal" conceivability problem.** What is *ideal* conceivability? It is conceivability by a *perfect* reasoner, with *all* the relevant information. But we are not perfect reasoners, and the "ideal" is an idealisation that may not be available. (Chalmers's response: the idealisation is *in principle*, and it can be approximated.)

**(ii) The Kripkean objection.** Kripke's necessary a posteriori is a *counterexample* to the CP thesis, if the necessity is *a posteriori*. Water is necessarily H₂O, but it is *conceivable* that water is not H₂O (we can imagine a world where the stuff that looks like water is XYZ). The CP thesis would imply that water is *not* necessarily H₂O, which contradicts Kripke's essentialism. (The response: the "conceivable" world is not a *metaphysically possible* world; it is an *epistemic* possibility that is *not* a metaphysical one. This is the "two-dimensional" response.)

**(iii) The "modal scepticism" objection.** The CP thesis is *unjustified*: we have no independent access to the space of possible worlds, and the "conceivability" intuition is a *psychological* fact, not an *epistemic* one. (This is the "modal scepticism" of van Inwagen, and it is the most radical challenge.)

### 5.4 The two-dimensional framework

The **two-dimensional framework** (Chalmers 2006, 2012; Jackson 1998) is the most sophisticated attempt to reconcile the a priori with the necessary a posteriori.

**The core idea.** Every sentence has *two* intensions:

- **The primary intension**: the function from *epistemic* possibilities (ways the world could be, considered a priori) to truth values. It captures the *a priori* content.
- **The secondary intension**: the function from *metaphysical* possibilities (ways the world could be, considered as counterfactual) to truth values. It captures the *a posteriori* content.

**The application.** "Water is H₂O" has a primary intension that is *contingent* (in the epistemic sense: it is a priori conceivable that water is not H₂O) and a secondary intension that is *necessary* (in the metaphysical sense: in all worlds where water is H₂O, it is H₂O). The two intensions are *different* functions, and the necessity is *secondary*, while the *a priori* content is *primary*.

**The payoff.** The framework allows the *a priori* and the *necessary* to come apart, without giving up either. It is the technical machinery for the "moderate revival" of the analytic, and it is the most influential framework in the contemporary debate.

**The problems.** (i) The framework is *technical*, and the interpretation is contested. (ii) The "primary intension" is defined in terms of *epistemic* possibility, and the notion of epistemic possibility is itself contested. (iii) The framework may be *ad hoc*: it is designed to save the CP thesis, and the *independent* motivation is not clear.

### 5.5 Modality and the a priori: the state of the debate

The contemporary state:

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| There are necessary truths | **Accepted** |
| Some necessary truths are a posteriori | **Accepted** (Kripke) |
| Some a priori truths are contingent | **Accepted** (Kripke) |
| Conceivability is a guide to possibility | **Contested** |
| The two-dimensional framework resolves the tensions | **Contested** |
| Modal scepticism is false | **Contested** |

The chapter's verdict: modality is *real*, the a priori is *real*, and the relation between them is *not* the classical trinity. The thin conception (§3.4) is the best available: the a priori is a *kind of warrant*, not a *kind of necessity*; the necessary is a *metaphysical* status, not an *epistemic* one; and the two can come apart.

## 6. Rational Insight, Self-Evidence, and Disagreement

### 6.1 The classical conception of self-evidence

The classical conception: a proposition is **self-evident** if, when understood, it *compels* assent — it cannot be doubted *by a rational mind that grasps it*.

The properties:

1. **Immediacy**: the knowledge is not inferred.
2. **Universality**: any rational mind that grasps the proposition assents to it.
3. **Indubitability**: the proposition cannot be doubted.
4. **Necessity**: the proposition is necessarily true.

The classical conception is *foundationalist* (Ch. 8, §2): self-evident propositions are the basic beliefs on which knowledge rests. It is also *intuitionist*: the faculty of rational insight is the source of the self-evidence.

### 6.2 The problem of disagreement

The problem: **the experts disagree**. The disagreement is not merely about the *conclusions*; it is about the *self-evident* propositions themselves. Some philosophers find the principle of sufficient reason self-evident; others find it obviously false. Some find the zombie argument compelling; others find it a *reductio* of the conceivability–possibility thesis.

The **argument**:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{If a proposition is self-evident, then any rational mind that grasps it assents to it.} \\
2. & \text{Rational minds disagree about the "self-evident" propositions.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{No proposition is self-evident (in the classical sense).}
\end{array}
$$

The argument is valid, and premise 2 is empirically true. The classical conception is *empirically* false: the *universality* condition is not met.

### 6.3 The responses

**(i) The "ideal reasoner" response.** The universality condition applies to the *ideal* reasoner, not the *actual* one. The disagreement among actual reasoners is evidence of *error*, not of *non-self-evidence*. (The problem: the ideal reasoner is an *idealisation*, and it is *unavailable* as a standard. The response is a *promissory note*.)

**(ii) The "expertise" response.** The disagreement among experts is *less* than the disagreement among novices, and the *trend* is toward convergence. (The problem: the trend is *not* toward convergence; it is toward *specialisation* and *fragmentation*. The "expertise" response is empirically *dubious*.)

**(iii) The "fallibilist" response.** Self-evidence is *defeasible*: a proposition can be *self-evidently* justified, but the justification can be *defeated* by further reflection. The classical conception of *indubitability* is abandoned; the *immediacy* and the *warrant* are retained. (This is the thin conception, and it is the dominant position.)

**(iv) The "meta-induction" response.** Chalmers (2015) argues that the *history* of philosophy suggests that the disagreement will *not* converge. The "meta-induction": if the experts have disagreed for 2,000 years, they will probably continue to disagree. The response is *pessimistic*, and it is *empirically* supported. (The *philosophical* consequence is *humility*, not scepticism: the disagreement is *evidence* for caution, not for the *falsehood* of the views.)

### 6.4 The epistemology of philosophical disagreement

The disagreement is *itself* a topic in epistemology (Ch. 11, §3). The three positions:

| Position | Claim | Proponents |
|---|---|---|
| **Equal weight** | When an epistemic peer disagrees, both should reduce confidence | Christensen 2007; Feldman 2006 |
| **Steadfast** | One may retain confidence if one has reason to think one's assessment is better | Kelly 2005; Sosa 2010 |
| **Total evidence** | The disagreement is *evidence*, but its weight depends on the details | Lackey 2010; Ballantyne 2019 |

The **application to philosophy**: the disagreement is *pervasive*, and it is *not* obviously due to the *incompetence* of the disagreeing parties. The *equal weight* view implies that philosophers should be *much less confident* than they are. The *steadfast* view is *self-serving* and *hard to justify*. The *total evidence* view is the most defensible, but it is *unspecific*: it does not tell us *how much* to reduce confidence.

The **practical upshot**: the philosophical diary (Ch. 4, §5.1), calibrated confidence (Ch. 4, §4.1), and the *steel man* (Ch. 2, §4.3) are the *technologies* for managing the disagreement. The *virtue* is *humility* — the recognition that the disagreement is *evidence*, and that the confidence is *calibrated*, not *absolute*.

## 7. The Mathematics Case

### 7.1 Why mathematics is the test case

Mathematics is the *paradigm* of a priori knowledge, and it is the *test case* for the whole chapter. If the thin conception of the a priori is correct, mathematics is its clearest instance. If the a priori is an illusion, mathematics is the *reductio* of the illusion.

The case has three parts: the *nature* of mathematical knowledge (what justifies it), the *objects* of mathematical knowledge (what it is about), and the *applicability* of mathematics (why it fits the world).

### 7.2 The access problem

**Benacerraf's dilemma** (1973) is the central problem in the philosophy of mathematics.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Mathematical truths are about abstract objects (numbers, sets, functions).} \\
2. & \text{Abstract objects are causally inert (they have no causal powers).} \\
3. & \text{Knowledge requires a causal connection between the knower and the known (the "causal theory of knowledge").} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{We cannot know mathematical truths.}
\end{array}
$$

The dilemma: either the *semantics* of mathematics is *realist* (mathematical statements are about abstract objects) or the *epistemology* is *naturalistic* (knowledge requires causal contact). The two are *incompatible*, and each is *plausible*. The problem is to give an account that satisfies both.

**The solutions.**

| Solution | How it solves the dilemma | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Platonism with a non-causal epistemology** | Knowledge of abstracta is *a priori*, not causal | The non-causal epistemology is *mysterious* |
| **Structuralism** | Mathematics is about *structures*, not objects | The structures are still abstract |
| **Nominalism** | Mathematics is about *concrete* things (or is a *fiction*) | The nominalist reconstruction is *hard* and *unintuitive* |
| **Fictionalism** | Mathematical statements are *useful fictions* | The *applicability* of the fictions is unexplained |
| **Indispensability** | Mathematics is *indispensable* to science, so we are *committed* to its objects | The *epistemic* access is still unexplained |

The dilemma is unresolved, and it is the *deepest* problem in the philosophy of mathematics (Ch. 43, §1).

### 7.3 The epistemology of mathematics

The candidate sources of mathematical knowledge:

| Source | What it is | Paradigm | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intuition** | A direct grasp of mathematical truth | The axiom of choice; the continuum | The "intuition" is *contested* |
| **Proof** | A derivation from axioms | The proof of Fermat's Last Theorem | The *axioms* are not proved |
| **Conceptual analysis** | The analysis of mathematical concepts | The definition of "number" | The *concepts* are *contested* |
| **Empirical** | The application of mathematics to the world | The use of statistics | The *necessity* is unexplained |
| **Computer-assisted proof** | A proof verified by a computer | The four-colour theorem | The *epistemic* status of the verification |

The **state of the debate**: the *sources* are *plural*, and the *justification* is *holistic*. The mathematical community *accepts* a theorem when it is *proved*, and the proof is *checked* by the community. The *justification* is *social* as well as *individual* (Ch. 11, §5), and the *a priori* is *not* a *private* intuition but a *public* practice.

### 7.4 The applicability puzzle

**Wigner's puzzle** (1960): "The unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences." Why does abstract mathematics — developed with no thought of application — turn out to describe the physical world with astonishing precision?

The **candidate answers**:

**(i) The "instrumentalist" answer.** Mathematics is a *tool*; it is effective because we *choose* the tools that work. The *apparent* effectiveness is a *selection effect*.

**(ii) The "realist" answer.** The world *is* mathematical; the effectiveness is *evidence* for mathematical realism (Ch. 43, §6).

**(iii) The "structuralist" answer.** Mathematics describes the *structure* of the world, and the structure is *real* (Ch. 19, §6).

**(iv) The "mystery" answer.** The effectiveness is *unexplained*, and it is a *brute fact*. (This is Wigner's own view, and it is *honest* but *unsatisfying*.)

The puzzle is *not* resolved, and it is *connected* to the a priori: if mathematics is *a priori*, and it is *applicable*, then the *a priori* is *not* disconnected from the world. The connection is the *deepest* mystery in the philosophy of mathematics.

### 7.5 The verdict on the mathematics case

The mathematics case is *ambiguous*:

- It is the *strongest* case for the a priori (the truths are *necessary*, *a priori*, and *indispensable*).
- It is the *strongest* case *against* the classical a priori (the *access* problem is *unsolved*, and the *applicability* is *unexplained*).

The *honest* position: mathematics is *a priori* in the *thin* sense (the warrant is *not* empirical), but the *thick* conception (the *faculty* of rational insight) is *untenable*. The *a priori* is *real*, but it is *not* the *foundation* that the classical tradition hoped for.

## 8. The State of the Debate

### 8.1 What is settled

- **The classical trinity is broken.** The a priori, the necessary, and the analytic are *distinct* notions, and they do not coincide. Kripke's revolution is *permanent*.
- **The analytic/synthetic distinction is real but fuzzy.** The *sharp* distinction is untenable; the *moderate* distinction is *useful*.
- **The a priori is not a special faculty.** The *thin* conception is the best available: the a priori is a *kind of warrant*, not a *source*.
- **Intuition is fallible.** The *classical* conception of self-evidence (indubitable, universal) is *false*. The *fallibilist* conception is *defensible*.
- **Mathematics is the test case.** The *access* problem is *unsolved*, and the *applicability* is *unexplained*.

### 8.2 What is open

- **The nature of the a priori.** Is it a *kind of warrant* (Boghossian), a *structural feature* (Chalmers), or an *illusion* (Quine)?
- **The reliability of intuition.** Is intuition *truth-tracking*, or is it a *product* of evolution and culture?
- **The conceivability–possibility thesis.** Is conceivability a *guide* to possibility, or is it a *psychological* fact with no *epistemic* force?
- **The epistemology of mathematics.** How do we *know* mathematical truths, and why are they *applicable*?
- **The problem of disagreement.** How should the *pervasive* disagreement among philosophers affect *confidence*?

### 8.3 The methodological lesson

The chapter is a case study in the method of Part I.

- **Conceptual analysis** (§1 of Ch. 3) generated the *classical* distinctions and the *objections* to them.
- **Thought experiments** (§2 of Ch. 3) — the Twin Earth cases, the zombie argument, the Gettier cases — are the *engine* of the modal epistemology.
- **Formalization** (§3 of Ch. 3) — the two-dimensional framework, the modal logic — is *essential* for the *technical* debate.
- **Reflective equilibrium** (§4 of Ch. 3) is the method by which the *intuitions* and the *theories* are adjusted.
- **Genealogy and debunking** (§5 of Ch. 3) is the *challenge* to intuition, and it is *unresolved*.
- **Negative capability** (§7 of Ch. 3) is the *response* to the disagreement: the *humility* to hold the question open.

The *honest* verdict: the theory of the a priori is a *field in transition*. The *classical* conception is *untenable*; the *thin* conception is *promising* but *incomplete*; the *naturalistic* challenge is *serious* but *not decisive*. The *unresolved* character is *instructive*: it shows what philosophical progress looks like — not a settled answer, but a sharper understanding of what the question is.

### 8.4 The dialectical map

| Position | Core claim | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Classical rationalism** | The a priori is a faculty of reason; it is necessary and certain | Explains the *certainty* of logic and mathematics | *Refuted* by Kripke and the *fallibility* of intuition |
| **Logical positivism** | The analytic/synthetic distinction is *sharp*; the synthetic a priori is *empty* | *Clear* and *rigorous* | *Refuted* by Quine and the *self-refutation* of verificationism |
| **Quinean naturalism** | There is *no* a priori; all beliefs are *revisable* | *Honest* about the *web* of belief | *Cannot* explain the *indispensability* of logic and mathematics |
| **The moderate revival** | The distinction is *epistemic*; the a priori is a *kind of warrant* | *Compatible* with naturalism; *defends* the a priori | *Vague*; the "warrant" is a *label* |
| **Modal scepticism** | Conceivability is *not* a guide to possibility | *Radical* and *consistent* | *Cannot* explain the *practice* of modal reasoning |
| **The two-dimensional framework** | The a priori and the necessary are *distinct* | *Technical* and *powerful* | *Contested*; the primary intension is *ad hoc* |

The table is a map, not a verdict. The reader who wants to choose a position must decide which cost is acceptable — and the decision is a philosophical argument.

## Summary

- **Russell’s account of intuition** treats intuitive knowledge as the *foundation* of all knowledge. The account is *intuitionist*, and it is *fallible*. The *crisis* of intuition has three sources: fallibility, variability, and the experimental challenge.
- **The analytic/synthetic distinction** is Kant’s. It was *sharpened* by Frege, *radicalised* by the logical positivists, and *attacked* by Quine. The *attack* has two parts: the *circularity* of the definition of analyticity, and the *holism* of the web of belief. The *moderate revival* (Boghossian, Jackson, Chalmers) accepts the *fuzziness* of the distinction but *defends* its *epistemic* usefulness.
- **The a priori** is *not* a *faculty* but a *kind of warrant* (the *thin* conception). The *classical trinity* — a priori = necessary = certain — is *broken* by Kripke’s revolution. The *necessary a posteriori* (water is H₂O) and the *contingent a priori* (the standard metre) are *real*, and they *refute* the classical identification.
- **Intuition as evidence** is *defended* by the *conceptual competence*, *modal*, and *seeming* accounts, and *challenged* by *experimental philosophy* and *debunking*. The *calibration problem* is *unsolved*.
- **Modality** is *real*, and the *conceivability–possibility thesis* is *contested*. The *two-dimensional framework* is the most sophisticated *attempt* to reconcile the *a priori* with the *necessary a posteriori*, and it is *influential* but *not decisive*.
- **Rational insight and self-evidence** are *undermined* by *disagreement*. The *classical* conception (indubitable, universal) is *empirically false*. The *fallibilist* conception is *defensible*, and the *meta-induction* on philosophical disagreement is *evidence* for *humility*.
- **Mathematics** is the *test case*. The *access problem* (Benacerraf) is *unsolved*; the *applicability puzzle* (Wigner) is *unexplained*. Mathematics is *a priori* in the *thin* sense, but the *thick* conception is *untenable*.
- **The state of the debate**: the *classical* conceptions are *untenable*; the *thin* conceptions are *promising* but *incomplete*; the *naturalistic* challenge is *serious* but *not decisive*. The *unresolved* character is *instructive*.

> *A closing note.* This chapter has followed the tradition’s oldest claim — that some knowledge is *independent of experience* — through the *three contests* that have shaped it: Kant’s *synthetic a priori*, Quine’s *attack* on the analytic, and Kripke’s *separation* of the a priori from the necessary. The result is *not* a *refutation* of the a priori, and it is *not* a *vindication* of the classical conception. It is a *narrowing*: the a priori is *real*, but it is *thin*, *fallible*, and *not* the *foundation* that the rationalist tradition hoped for.

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter turns to *induction* — the *other* source of justification that the tradition has treated as *foundational*. If the a priori is *thin*, the *empirical* is *thick*; and the *thickness* raises its own problems. The problem of induction is the *empirical* counterpart of the problem of the a priori: it is the question of *how experience can justify beliefs that go beyond it*. The next chapter examines Hume’s problem, Goodman’s *grue*, the Bayesian framework, and the *inference to the best explanation*. The connection is direct: the *a priori* and the *empirical* are the *two* sources of justification, and the *theory* of justification must *reconcile* them.
