# Chapter 1 — What Philosophy Is, and What It Is For

> “The value of philosophy is, in fact, to be sought largely in its very uncertainty. The man who has no tincture of philosophy goes through life imprisoned in the prejudices derived from common sense, from the habitual beliefs of his age or his nation, and from convictions which have grown up in his mind without the co-operation or consent of his deliberate reason.”
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Ch. XV

---

## 1. The Naive Picture, and Why It Fails

Begin with a scene that will be familiar to anyone who has taught the subject. A student arrives at the first lecture expecting to be told the answers. When she is instead given two incompatible answers, each defended with care, she concludes — politely but firmly — that philosophy is a matter of opinion. “That’s just, like, your perspective.” She has not been persuaded by an argument; she has been disappointed by the absence of a verdict.

This is the **naive picture**: philosophy is a catalogue of worldviews. There are Platonists, Aristotelians, Cartesians, Kantians, Utilitarians, Existentialists; each has a system; you choose one the way you choose a political party or a football team. On this picture, the history of philosophy is a museum of incompatible doctrines, and the activity of philosophy is the activity of picking a room in the museum and settling in.

The naive picture is not stupid. It is the natural inference from three genuine features of the discipline:

1. **Philosophy contains real, unresolved disagreement**, and this disagreement is not merely a matter of ignorance awaiting data. Two philosophers of equal brilliance and equal information can disagree about free will forever.
2. **Philosophy has no agreed method of adjudication.** There is no experiment that settles whether the will is free, no measurement that decides whether numbers exist.
3. **Philosophy’s answers are entangled with the philosopher’s values, history, and temperament** in ways that scientific answers are supposed not to be.

Each of these is true. Together they seem to force the conclusion that philosophy is opinion. But the inference is invalid, and seeing why is the first lesson of the subject.

**First error: confusing the question with the answer.** “What is justice?” is not the same kind of thing as “Justice is the interest of the stronger” (Thrasymachus) or “Justice is the health of the soul” (Plato). The question has a structure; the answers are candidates; and candidates can be evaluated — for consistency, for explanatory power, for fidelity to the phenomena they purport to explain — *without our having a decisive verdict*. That a jury cannot reach a verdict does not make the trial a matter of taste. It makes it a hard trial.

**Second error: treating disagreement as evidence of arbitrariness.** Disagreement is evidence of *difficulty*, not of *subjectivity*, unless we have independent reason to think that agreement would have been forthcoming if there were a fact of the matter. In the sciences, disagreement is normally local and temporary; in philosophy it is global and durable. That difference is real and must be explained. But the explanation might be that philosophical questions are *harder*, or *more general*, or *more closely tied to the whole of our framework of belief* — not that they have no answers. Compare: there is durable, global disagreement about how to raise children, and yet some ways of raising children are clearly worse than others. Durability of disagreement underdetermines the metaphysics.

**Third error: mistaking the sociology for the content.** That philosophy is *practised* by people with temperaments, in departments with politics, in cultures with histories, is a fact about philosophers. It does not follow that the *propositions they assert* are merely expressions of those facts. This is a special case of a general fallacy: the genetic fallacy, the confusion of an account of a belief’s *origin* with an account of its *warrant*. (We will return to this in Ch. 3, §5, when we discuss genealogical debunking.)

There is, however, a fourth feature of philosophy that the naive picture obscures and that any honest account must accommodate: **philosophy is reflexive in a way the sciences are not.** The physicist can take for granted what physics is, what a theory is, what evidence is, and what it is for one theory to be better than another. The philosopher cannot take these for granted, because they are among her questions. This reflexivity is the source of philosophy’s peculiar difficulty — and of its peculiar value. It is also the reason philosophy so often *looks* like opinion from the outside: it is the discipline in which the standards of assessment are themselves on the table.

So the naive picture fails. But it fails for a reason that points toward the right picture. If philosophy is not a catalogue of opinions, what is it?

## 2. Three Working Definitions

Philosophers have proposed many definitions of their subject. Three are worth distinguishing, because they correspond to three genuine and complementary conceptions of the activity, and because this book uses all three.

### (a) Philosophy as conceptual analysis

The classical conception: philosophy’s job is to analyse concepts — to give necessary and sufficient conditions for their application. What is *knowledge*? What is *causation*? What is *justice*? What is a *person*? On this view, the philosopher’s characteristic product is a biconditional:

> $x$ is a $K$ if and only if $x$ is $F_1$ and $F_2$ and … and $F_n$.

The model has real virtues. It explains why philosophy can deliver *a priori* knowledge (Ch. 9): if you are analysing a concept you already possess, you do not need to go and look at the world. It explains why conceptual analysis feels like *discovery* rather than *invention*. And it has produced some of philosophy’s most durable achievements — Plato’s analysis of knowledge in the *Theaetetus*, the theory of descriptions (Ch. 12, §2), the distinction between *de dicto* and *de re* modality (Ch. 16).

It also has well-known vices, which the book will treat in detail (Ch. 3, §1):

- **The classical model is probably false for most interesting concepts.** Since Gettier (1963), we know that “justified true belief” is not sufficient for knowledge (Ch. 7). Since Wittgenstein and Quine, we have reason to doubt that most concepts have sharp necessary-and-sufficient conditions at all. Many are prototypes, clusters, family-resemblance structures, or essentially contested.
- **Analysis tends to be either trivial or revisionary.** If the analysans is synonymous with the analysandum, the analysis is uninformative; if it is not, it is not analysis but *replacement* — conceptual engineering rather than conceptual analysis (Ch. 33, §5).
- **The method of cases is empirically fragile.** Experimental philosophy has shown that intuitions about cases vary with culture, order of presentation, and affect (Ch. 3, §6; Ch. 9, §4).

Still, the impulse behind conceptual analysis is sound and ineliminable: before we can say whether there is knowledge, or whether justice requires equality, we need to know what we are talking about. The book therefore keeps conceptual analysis as one instrument among others, not as the whole toolkit.

### (b) Philosophy as rational reconstruction

A second conception: philosophy makes explicit the principles that are *implicit* in a practice. We already reason scientifically, morally, mathematically, and linguistically; the philosopher’s task is to reconstruct the rules we are following, the commitments we are incurring, and the structure that our practice presupposes.

This is the conception behind Carnap’s *logical syntax*, behind the formal semantics of natural language (Ch. 31), behind decision theory (Appendix A) and Bayesian epistemology (Ch. 10), behind Rawls’s construction of a “basic structure” and a veil of ignorance (Ch. 47), and behind the contemporary project of *conceptual engineering* (Ch. 33, §5). Its characteristic question is not “What does *knowledge* mean?” but “What would a system of belief-forming processes have to be like for it to be truth-conducive, and how do our actual processes compare?”

Rational reconstruction is powerful because it is *accountable*: a reconstruction can be tested against the practice it reconstructs. If your theory of assertion implies that most of what we say is not assertion, so much the worse for your theory. It is also the conception that most naturally connects philosophy to the special sciences, since the practices being reconstructed are often the practices of physicists, biologists, or statisticians (Part VI).

Its danger is that reconstruction can become *legislation*: the philosopher tells the practice what it must be, and when the practice refuses, the philosopher concludes that the practice is confused. Sometimes this is right — the practice *is* confused. But the burden of proof is on the reconstructor, and it is heavy.

### (c) Philosophy as the study of the conditions of intelligibility

The third conception, and the one this book adopts as its working definition, is broader than either of the first two. Philosophy is the study of **what would have to be the case — what we would have to show, say, and assume — for our ordinary beliefs, concepts, and practices to be coherent.**

This is a descendant of Kant’s transcendental question (“What are the conditions of the possibility of experience?”), but it is *naturalized*: it does not appeal to a mysterious faculty of pure reason, and it does not assume that the conditions it finds will be a priori or necessary. It asks, of any domain of human thought:

- What are the **presuppositions** of this practice? (For there to be promises, there must be conventions; for there to be assertions, there must be norms of truthfulness; for there to be property, there must be a system of recognition.)
- What are the **commitments** of this belief? (If determinism is true, what follows for responsibility? If physicalism is true, what follows for the causal efficacy of the mental?)
- What are the **limits** of this concept? (Where does “cause” stop applying? Where does “person” run out? What is the difference between a deep question and a pseudo-question?)
- What would **coherence** require? (Can I hold this belief together with that one? If not, which gives way?)

The advantage of this formulation is that it covers the whole of the book without dissolving its unity. Conceptual analysis is the special case where the practice being studied is *concept application*. Rational reconstruction is the special case where the practice is *inference*. The conditions-of-intelligibility formulation also makes room for the parts of philosophy that are not analysis at all: the critique of ideology, the diagnosis of conceptual confusion, the construction of alternatives, and the articulation of what a practice would have to become in order to be defensible.

The unifying thesis of this book, then, is this:

> **Philosophy is not a catalogue of opinions but a discipline of systematic analysis — the disciplined study of what we would have to show, say, and assume in order to make our ordinary beliefs, concepts, and practices coherent.**

The three definitions are three aspects of one activity: analysis clarifies the concept, reconstruction makes explicit the practice, and the conditions-of-intelligibility question tells us what the whole thing is *for*. None of them is a definition in the sense of a set of necessary and sufficient conditions — and a reader who has already absorbed Chapter 3 will recognise that as a feature, not a bug.

**A note on the history of philosophy.** On this conception, the history of philosophy is not the subject; it is *data*. The great dead philosophers are not authorities to be cited but interlocutors to be assessed — and, as we shall see in §6, the *careers* of their questions are themselves philosophically informative. To treat Plato as an oracle is to insult him; to treat him as a colleague is to honour him.

## 3. The Perennial Questions and the “Permanent Problems” Illusion

Russell’s book opens with the table. He asks whether there is a table with a certain intrinsic nature that continues to exist when no one is looking, or whether the table is a product of the imagination — a “dream-table in a very prolonged dream.” From this small domestic puzzle he derives the whole of the subject: appearance and reality, matter, mind, universals, truth, knowledge, value.

The list of Russell’s questions — what is there? what can we know? what is a mind? what is truth? — has proved remarkably durable, and the tradition has added questions of its own: what is a person? what is good? why is there something rather than nothing? These questions recur in every century, in every tradition, in every language in which philosophy has been done.

But the *durability* of the questions is not the same as the *permanence* of the problems, and the difference matters enormously for how we should read the history of the subject.

There is a tradition — the *philosophia perennis* to which Leibniz gave his name (though the term is older, going back to Agostino Steuco), and which Aldous Huxley popularised as “the perennial philosophy” — according to which the great questions have been asked and answered, and the answers are essentially the same across cultures. The tradition has a grain of truth: the questions do recur. But the claim that the *problems* are permanent is a different and much stronger claim, and it is false in a way that has misled generations of students.

Consider three kinds of case.

**Case 1: The problem is transformed.** The medieval problem of universals — do general terms name real entities, or only particulars? — is recognisably the same question as the contemporary debate between realism about properties, trope theory, and nominalism (Ch. 15). But the *form* of the debate has been transformed: Ockham’s nominalism is not Quine’s, and Armstrong’s immanent realism is not Plato’s transcendent realism. The question persists; the space of answers has been reconstructed; the standards of adequacy have changed (we now demand, for example, that an account of properties explain the projectibility of predicates — Ch. 39, §3 — not merely the possibility of predication). The problem was not solved; it was *re-engineered*.

**Case 2: The problem is dissolved.** “What is the substance of the soul?” “How many angels can dance on the head of a pin?” “Is the will free in the sense that it could have done otherwise under *exactly* the same circumstances, including the same desires?” The first presupposed a substance ontology of the mental that most of us no longer accept; the second presupposed a metaphysics of angels that no one now accepts; the third, as compatibilists have argued for three centuries (Ch. 27), may be a question whose presuppositions are incoherent. Dissolution is a form of progress — one of the most important forms — but it is not the form the naive picture recognises as progress, because it does not produce a *doctrine*.

**Case 3: The problem is answered.** This happens more often than philosophers like to admit, and the answers are often absorbed so thoroughly that they cease to look like philosophy. That there is no such thing as a “natural slave” — the view of much of the ancient world, and defended by Aristotle himself in the *Politics* (I.6) — is now a philosophical consensus, and the arguments that produced it — arguments about the moral irrelevance of natural endowment, about the universality of the capacity for practical reason — are philosophical arguments. That the state exists for the sake of the governed, not the governed for the sake of the state, is a philosophical achievement of the early modern period. That women have the capacity for rational agency is a philosophical achievement whose recency should chasten us. We do not notice these answers because they have become the *background* against which we ask the remaining questions.

The lesson is a distinction the reader should carry through the whole book:

> **A question can be permanent because it is deep, or because it is badly posed. The first kind deserves our labour; the second deserves our diagnosis.**

The diagnostic question is: *what would count as progress on this question?* If we can specify in advance what would count as an answer — a new distinction, a new argument, a new empirical finding, a demonstration that the question rests on a false presupposition — then the question is a real one, even if the answer is far off. If we cannot specify any such thing, we should suspect that the question is a pseudo-question, or a verbal dispute in disguise (Ch. 33, §4.3), or a question whose form has been inherited from a framework we no longer accept.

Two errors must be avoided here, and they are mirror images of each other.

- **Triumphalist historicism**: the view that all previous philosophy was confused, and that we have now got it right. This is almost always false, and it is usually a sign that the philosopher has not read enough history to know which of her own assumptions are historically contingent.
- **Antiquarianism**: the view that the ancients already said everything, and that our only task is to recover it. This is almost always false too, and it is usually a sign that the philosopher has not noticed how much of the ancient framework has been dismantled by subsequent work — sometimes by philosophy itself.

The truth is in the middle, and it is the truth this book tries to practise: **the questions are real, the answers are provisional, and the history of the answers is part of the evidence.**

## 4. Philosophy and Its Neighbours: Where the Handoff Happens

Philosophy has no proprietary subject matter. It has a proprietary *question* — the question of what is presupposed — and this question can be asked of any domain. That is why philosophy has neighbours, and why the boundaries are contested.

### Science

The standard picture is a **handoff**: philosophy generates questions; when a question becomes tractable by empirical methods, it is handed to science; philosophy moves on to the next frontier. On this picture, philosophy is the nursery of the sciences — natural philosophy became physics, moral philosophy became psychology and economics, the philosophy of mind is being absorbed into cognitive science.

The handoff picture is largely true as history. But it is misleading as a picture of the *current* relation, for three reasons.

First, **the handoff is never complete.** Science does not merely inherit questions from philosophy; it inherits *presuppositions* that it cannot examine from within. That the future will resemble the past (Ch. 10), that the external world exists (Ch. 6), that our theories approximate the truth (Ch. 40), that the mathematics in which our theories are expressed refers to something (Ch. 43) — these are not scientific results. They are commitments that science makes, and philosophy is the discipline that examines them. This is not a criticism of science; it is a description of the division of labour. A discipline cannot audit its own foundations while standing on them.

Second, **the traffic runs both ways.** Philosophy constrains science as much as science constrains philosophy. The interpretation of quantum mechanics (Ch. 19, §5; Ch. 41) is not an empirical question; it is a question about what the formalism *means*, and it is settled by arguments about locality, determinism, and the nature of measurement. The ethics of research (Ch. 39, §6) is not an empirical question either. The philosophy of biology has changed how biologists think about species and function (Ch. 42). The influence is not one-directional.

Third, **some questions are permanently philosophical** because they are normative. No amount of empirical data settles whether a distribution is just, whether a being is a person with rights, or whether a life is meaningful (Part VII). This is not because the answers are arbitrary; it is because the answers are not *empirical*. The is/ought gap (Ch. 44, §1) is a real feature of the logical geography, and no scientific discovery closes it.

### Logic and mathematics

Philosophy of logic is not logic, and philosophy of mathematics is not mathematics. The logician proves theorems; the philosopher asks what a proof *is*, what a logical constant *means*, whether classical logic is correct, and what follows from the existence of independent set-theoretic questions (Ch. 35, Ch. 43). The relationship is intimate — the tools of logic are the philosopher’s instruments (Ch. 3, §3) — but the questions are distinct.

### Art

Philosophy of art is not art criticism, though it is informed by it. Its questions are: what is an aesthetic property? What is a work of art? Can art be a source of knowledge? (Ch. 49.) But there is a deeper connection, which the reader should notice: **philosophy itself uses literary techniques.** The thought experiment is a literary form (Ch. 3, §2); the dialogue is a genre; the essay is a genre. Philosophy’s form is not incidental to its content. A philosopher who cannot write cannot think clearly, because clarity of thought is largely clarity of formulation.

### Religion

Philosophy of religion is not theology. Theology reasons within a tradition, from premises the tradition supplies; philosophy of religion asks what can be rationally held, and by whom, and on what evidence (Ch. 50). But the relationship is not adversarial by nature. The greatest philosophers of religion — Aquinas, Maimonides, al-Ghazālī, Śaṅkara — were believers using philosophical tools to understand their faith. The philosopher’s task is not to debunk religion but to ask what religion commits one to, and whether those commitments can be sustained. The answer may be yes or no; the point is that it must be argued.

### The general lesson

Philosophy is the **residual discipline** — it takes up what other disciplines presuppose but cannot examine from within. “Residual” sounds like “leftover,” but that is the wrong metaphor. The better metaphor is **foundational**: philosophy is what you do when you ask what a practice rests on. Every practice rests on something, and the something is usually unexamined. The philosopher’s job is to examine it — not in order to replace the practice, but in order to know what the practice is committed to.

This is also why philosophy is so often *unwelcome*. A practice that is working does not want its foundations excavated. The philosopher is the person who asks, at the dinner party, whether the host actually owns the house. Sometimes the question is rude. Sometimes it is the most important question in the room.

## 5. The Payoff Problem

We come to the objection that has followed philosophy since Socrates: **what is it for?**

The charge takes several forms. The vulgar form: philosophy makes no progress, so it is a waste of time. The polite form: philosophy’s results are incommensurable with its ambitions — it promises wisdom and delivers distinctions. The political form: philosophy is a luxury of the leisured classes, and its questions are distractions from the practical business of living. The internal form, which is the most serious: philosophy’s own history suggests that its problems are insoluble, and a discipline with insoluble problems is either an art form or a pathology.

The answer is not that philosophy *does* make progress in the way science does. It does not, and pretending otherwise is the surest way to lose the argument. The answer is that **progress is not one thing**, and philosophy produces several kinds of it. Five are worth distinguishing.

### (i) Progress in clarity

The dissolution of a pseudo-problem is a genuine advance, even though it produces no doctrine. When Ryle (1949) argued that the “ghost in the machine” is a category mistake, he did not prove that minds are physical; he showed that a certain way of posing the mind–body problem was confused. When Wittgenstein asked what a rule is, and what following a rule consists in, he did not solve the problem of normativity; he made it possible to see what a solution would have to look like (Ch. 31, §6). When contemporary philosophers distinguish the *hard problem* of consciousness from the *easy problems* (Ch. 24), they do not solve either; they reorganise the terrain so that the hard part is visible.

This is not a consolation prize. A problem that is clearly posed is a different problem from one that is not, and the difference is practical: clear problems can be worked on, confused ones cannot.

### (ii) Progress in technique

Philosophy has produced instruments that are used far beyond philosophy: formal logic, probability theory, decision theory, the theory of definitions, the analysis of counterfactuals, the apparatus of possible worlds, the distinction between validity and soundness, the concept of a counterexample, the method of reflective equilibrium. These are not opinions; they are tools, and they work. A student who learns them can use them in law, in medicine, in public policy, in computer science, and in the ordinary business of arguing with her family.

### (iii) Progress in vision

Russell’s own answer, and still the best one. Philosophy enlarges the self by forcing it to consider alternatives to what it has always assumed. This enlargement operates in three dimensions:

- **Temporal**: the history of philosophy shows that our most obvious beliefs were once contested, and that the contest was not stupid. The reader who has understood why Aristotle defended natural slavery, and why the argument fails, has learned something about the contingency of her own moral certainties.
- **Cultural**: the non-Western traditions — Buddhist, Confucian, Indian, African, Islamic — are not decorations. They contain systematic alternatives to positions we take for granted, and some of them are better than the positions we take for granted. (This book treats them as theories, not as curiosities; see the optional threads throughout Parts II–VII.)
- **Conceptual**: philosophy teaches the imagination of alternatives. To see that our concept of *person* might be otherwise, that our concept of *cause* might be otherwise, that our concept of *justice* might be otherwise, is to be freed from the tyranny of the actual.

This is Russell’s union with the not-self, and it is a genuine good — not because it makes us happy, but because it makes us less parochial. A mind that has never been forced to consider a serious alternative to its own framework is not free; it is merely confident.

### (iv) Negative results are results

A great deal of philosophy consists in showing that a question cannot be answered as posed, that two apparently distinct positions collapse into one, that an argument begs the question, that a concept is incoherent, or that a debate is verbal. These are results. They change what we can say. The history of philosophy is littered with positions that are now *dead* — not because they were refuted by a single decisive argument, but because the cumulative weight of objections made them untenable. That is progress, even if it is progress by subtraction.

### (v) Self-knowledge

Philosophy articulates what we already believe, so that we can criticise it. This is the Socratic function: the unexamined life is not worth living because an unexamined life is one in which the agent does not know what she is committed to. The method of reflective equilibrium (Ch. 3, §4) is precisely the method of bringing our particular judgments and our general principles into a stable, criticisable relation. The result is not a system; it is a *self* that knows what it thinks and why.

### The honest qualification

None of this shows that *all* philosophy is valuable. A great deal of it is not. The book will try to be honest about the difference, and the criteria are not mysterious:

- Does the work **change what we can say**? (Does it introduce a distinction, close a loophole, open a question?)
- Does it **survive objections**? (Has it been tested against the strongest available counterarguments, or only against straw men?)
- Does it **connect**? (Does it bear on other domains — science, ethics, politics, ordinary life — or is it a closed game?)
- Is it **honest about its own status**? (Does it claim more certainty than it has?)

These criteria are themselves philosophical, and they are themselves contestable. That is not a defect; it is the subject matter.

## 6. Two Case Studies: The Careers of Questions

The best way to see what philosophy is is to watch it work. We will trace two questions across some twenty-four centuries — one from ethics, one from metaphysics — and observe what happens to a question when it is asked by different people with different tools.

### Case A: “What is justice?”

**Plato (*Republic*, c. 380 BCE).** Justice is a property of a *soul* as well as of a *city*, and it consists in each part performing its proper function under the rule of reason. The question is answered by a metaphysical psychology: to know what justice is, you must know what the soul is. Justice is *harmony*; injustice is *disease*.

**Aristotle (*Nicomachean Ethics*, Book V, c. 340 BCE).** Justice is a virtue, and the central form of it is *distributive*: justice consists in proportional equality — treating equals equally and unequals unequally, in proportion to relevant difference. The question becomes: *which differences are relevant?* Aristotle’s answer (merit, and in practice, status) is one we now reject; his *form* of answer — proportional equality — remains one of the permanent options.

**Hobbes (*Leviathan*, 1651).** Justice is a matter of *convention*. There is no justice in the state of nature, because there is no property, and there is no property without a sovereign to define it. Justice becomes *artificial*: it is what the sovereign commands, and its ground is the mutual transfer of rights that constitutes political society.

**Hume (*A Treatise of Human Nature*, 1739–40).** Justice is an *artificial virtue*: it is not natural to human beings but arises from convention, and its point is *utility* — the stability of possession. The question shifts from metaphysics to *explanation*: what is the origin of the institution of justice, and what sustains it?

**Kant (*Groundwork*, 1785; *Doctrine of Right*, 1797).** Justice is grounded in *autonomy*. A maxim is permissible if the will can will it as a universal law; persons are ends in themselves, never merely means. The question shifts from the *content* of justice to its *ground*: what makes a claim of justice valid? Answer: its universalizability.

**Mill (*Utilitarianism*, 1861).** Justice is a department of morality, and morality is the maximisation of happiness. Justice is the set of rules whose general observance conduces to the greatest happiness; the feeling of justice is the natural sentiment of retaliation, moralised by social sympathy. The question becomes *aggregative*.

**Rawls (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971).** Justice is the first virtue of social institutions, and the question is: which principles would free and equal persons choose behind a veil of ignorance? Answer: the two principles — equal basic liberties, and inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged. The method shifts from intuition and metaphysics to *contract* and *reflective equilibrium*.

**Nozick (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974).** Justice is *historical* and *entitlement-based*: a distribution is just if it arose by just acquisition and just transfer, whatever its pattern. The question shifts from *end-state* to *process*.

**Sen and Nussbaum (1980s–2000s).** Justice is about *capabilities*: what people are actually able to do and be. The question shifts from resources and welfare to *functionings*, and from the ideal to the comparative.

**Anderson (1999), Fraser, Honneth, Fricker (2007).** Justice is about *relations*: equality of standing, not merely of stuff; and the distribution of *epistemic* goods — credibility, interpretive resources — is itself a matter of justice. The question shifts from the individual to the structure of recognition.

**What changed?** The unit of analysis (soul → city → individual → basic structure → global order); the method (metaphysical psychology → convention → transcendental argument → contract → reflective equilibrium → empirical critique); the scope (who counts: citizens, property-holders, all persons, all sentient beings, future generations); and the *content* of the answer (harmony, proportion, command, utility, universal law, difference principle, entitlement, capability, standing).

**What stayed?** The question of the *ground* of normative authority — why does justice bind? The tension between equality and desert. The problem of the second-best: what justice requires when the ideal is unattainable. And the phenomenon that started it all: the *experience* of injustice, which is the data that any theory must explain.

**The lesson.** “Justice” is what W. B. Gallie (1956) called an **essentially contested concept**: a concept whose correct application is not merely uncertain but *essentially* a matter of dispute, because the dispute is part of what the concept is for. That does not make the dispute arbitrary. It makes it *permanent*, and it makes the history of the dispute part of the evidence. A theory of justice that has not learned from the failures of its predecessors is not a theory; it is a repetition.

### Case B: “What is a cause?”

**Aristotle (*Physics*, Book II).** There are four causes: material, formal, efficient, final. The question “why?” has four legitimate answers, and the philosopher’s task is to distinguish them. This is not yet a theory of *causation* in the modern sense; it is a theory of *explanation*.

**Hume (*An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, 1748).** Cause is not a relation we perceive; it is a relation we *infer*, on the basis of constant conjunction and the custom of the mind. The two definitions — priority and contiguity, and necessary connection — are the seed of the whole modern debate. Causation becomes an *empirical* relation, and the question becomes: what is the *content* of the idea of necessary connection?

**Mill (*A System of Logic*, 1843).** Causation is invariable succession, and the methods of difference, agreement, and concomitant variation are the instruments for discovering it. The question becomes *methodological*: how do we identify causes in complex circumstances?

**Russell (“On the Notion of Cause,” 1912).** The concept of cause is a relic. Physics has no use for it; the word “cause” is “so inextricably bound up with misleading associations as to make its complete extrusion from the philosophical vocabulary desirable.” The question becomes *eliminative*: should we abandon the concept altogether?

**Lewis (“Causation,” 1973).** Causation is analysed counterfactually: $c$ causes $e$ iff, had $c$ not occurred, $e$ would not have occurred (with complications for pre-emption and overdetermination). The question becomes *semantic* and *modal*.

**Woodward (*Making Things Happen*, 2003).** Causation is *intervention*: $X$ causes $Y$ if an intervention on $X$ changes $Y$. The question becomes *practical* and *manipulability*-theoretic, and it connects directly to experimental method and to the special sciences.

**Mechanists (Machamer, Darden, Craver, 2000).** Causation is the production of change by organised mechanisms; to explain is to describe the mechanism. The question becomes *structural* and *levels*-theoretic.

**Contemporary pluralism.** There is no single relation of causation; there are several — difference-making, production, dependence, mechanism — and they answer different questions (Ch. 18). The question becomes *diagnostic*: which concept of cause is in play in this context, and what work is it doing?

**What changed?** The *science* (Aristotelian physics → Newtonian mechanics → quantum mechanics and statistics); the *logic* (syllogistic → Mill’s methods → counterfactual semantics → interventionist calculus); the *ambition* (from defining the essence of cause to mapping a family of related notions).

**What stayed?** The asymmetry of causation (causes explain effects, not vice versa); the connection between causation and *responsibility* (we hold people responsible for what they cause); the problem of distinguishing causation from mere correlation; and the experience of *agency* — of making something happen — which is arguably the root of the whole concept.

**The lesson.** Sometimes the science dissolves a concept, as Russell thought it dissolved causation; sometimes the concept is reconstructed to fit the science, as the interventionists reconstructed it; sometimes the question turns out to have a plural answer, as the pluralists argue. All three outcomes are philosophical results. None of them is the outcome the naive picture predicts, because the naive picture assumes that a question either has one answer or none.

### The meta-lesson

Philosophical questions have **careers**. They are asked, answered, criticised, reformulated, abandoned, and revived. The career of a question is not a distraction from the question; it is part of what the question is. A philosopher who does not know the career of her question is likely to rebuild a wheel that was shown, in 1748, not to roll.

This is why this book is organised around problems rather than around thinkers. The thinkers matter — Appendix D provides a chronological map — but they matter as participants in a conversation, not as monuments in a museum.

## Coda: How This Chapter Sets Up the Rest

We have arrived at a working conception of the subject. Philosophy is the disciplined study of the conditions of intelligibility: what we would have to show, say, and assume for our beliefs, concepts, and practices to be coherent. It proceeds by analysis, reconstruction, argument, and diagnosis. Its history is data, not authority. Its progress is real but plural: clarity, technique, vision, negative results, and self-knowledge. Its questions are permanent in some cases because they are deep and in others because they are badly posed, and the first task of the philosopher is to tell the difference.

The next three chapters supply the instruments. Chapter 2 takes apart the argument — the atom of philosophical work — and shows how to reconstruct one, attack one, and repair one. Chapter 3 surveys the toolkit: conceptual analysis, thought experiments, formalization, reflective equilibrium, genealogy, phenomenology, ordinary language, experimental philosophy, and the art of dissolution. Chapter 4 turns to the practice: how to read a paper, how to write one, how to evaluate one, and how to be honest in the process.

Only then, in Part II, do we turn to the terrain: knowledge, and its limits. The order is deliberate. The problems are intoxicating; the method is what keeps us sober. A reader who begins with the problems will have a splendid time and will believe everything she reads. A reader who begins with the method will be harder to fool — including by this book.

### Summary

- The **naive picture** — philosophy as a catalogue of opinions — mistakes difficulty for arbitrariness, disagreement for subjectivity, and the sociology of the discipline for its content.
- Philosophy is best understood as **systematic analysis**: the study of the conditions of intelligibility of our beliefs, concepts, and practices. Conceptual analysis and rational reconstruction are two of its instruments, not its whole nature.
- The **permanent problems** are not all permanent for the same reason. Some are deep; some are badly posed. Distinguishing them is a central philosophical skill.
- Philosophy’s relation to science is not a one-way handoff. Science inherits presuppositions it cannot audit; philosophy inherits data and constraints it cannot ignore.
- The **payoff** of philosophy is real but plural: clarity, technique, vision, negative results, and self-knowledge. Not all philosophy delivers it; the criteria for good work are themselves philosophical.
- Philosophical questions have **careers**. The history of a question is part of the evidence for its answer.

### Study Questions

1. The chapter claims that disagreement is evidence of difficulty, not of subjectivity. Is that always true? Can you construct a case in which durable disagreement *does* undermine the claim that there is a fact of the matter?
2. Which of the three definitions of philosophy (§2) do you find most defensible, and why? Can you give a case that one definition handles better than the others?
3. Identify a philosophical question that you believe has been *dissolved* rather than solved. What did the dissolution consist in, and was it progress?
4. Is the “handoff” picture of philosophy and science defensible? Give an example of a question that has been successfully handed off, and one that has not.
5. Russell thought philosophy’s value lay in its uncertainty. Is that a virtue or an admission of failure?
6. Trace a question of your own — “What is a person?”, “What is a right?”, “What is a number?” — through three historical figures, and identify what changed and what stayed.

### Further Reading

- **Primary**: Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912), Chs. I and XV — still the best short introduction to the spirit of the subject, and the text this book is in conversation with.
- **On method**: Gilbert Ryle, “Philosophical Arguments” (1945); Isaiah Berlin, “The Purpose of Philosophy” (1962); Bernard Williams, *Philosophy as a Human Discipline* (2000).
- **On the nature of the subject**: Timothy Williamson, *The Philosophy of Philosophy* (2007), for the ambitious view; Richard Rorty, *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979), for the sceptical view; David Chalmers, “Why Isn’t There More Progress in Philosophy?” (2015), for the honest stocktaking.
- **On essentially contested concepts**: W. B. Gallie, “Essentially Contested Concepts” (1956); on conceptual engineering, Cappelen, *Fixing Language* (2018) and Haslanger, *Resisting Reality* (2012).
- **Historical orientation**: Anthony Kenny, *A New History of Western Philosophy* (2004–07), for a reliable map; Peter Adamson’s *History of Philosophy without any gaps* (podcast and books), for the non-Western traditions that most histories omit.
