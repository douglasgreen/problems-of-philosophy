# Chapter 23 — The Mind–Body Problem

> “I am not a machine, and I am not a ghost in a machine. I am a person.”
> — a slogan of the *via media*, in the spirit of Strawson’s *Individuals* (1959)

> “The mind is what the brain does.”
> — a slogan of functionalism, in the spirit of Putnam (1967) and Fodor (1974)

> “If you think that the mind is the brain, you have not yet said *what* it is about the brain that is the mind.”
> — the functionalist’s objection to the identity theory, in a sentence

---

Part III surveyed *reality*, and it ended with the *meta*-debate about *method*. Part IV turns to the *region* of reality that is *closest* to us and *least* understood: the **mind**.

This chapter is the *entry point* to Part IV, and it is the *most* compressed chapter in the book. The *subject* — the relation between the mental and the physical — is *enormous*, and the *literature* is *vast*. The *chapter* does *not* attempt a *survey* of the *whole*. It *maps* the *space* of *options*, *states* the *arguments* for and against each, and *shows* how the *choice* among them *depends* on the *metaphysical* *framework* of Part III. The *deeper* *questions* — *consciousness* (Ch. 24), *intentionality* (Ch. 25), the *self* (Ch. 26), *free* *will* (Ch. 27) — are the *subject* of the *chapters* that *follow*.

The *chapter’s* thesis is that the *mind–body* *problem* is *not* a *single* *problem* but a *family* of *problems*, and that the *most* *defensible* *position* is a *form* of *non-reductive* *physicalism* — *qualified* by the *admission* that the *position* *fails* to *explain* *consciousness*, and that the *failure* is the *most* *important* *open* *problem* in *philosophy*.

The chapter proceeds in eight movements. §1 states the *problem* and *maps* the *options*. §2 examines *substance* *dualism*. §3 examines the *identity* theory. §4 examines *functionalism*. §5 examines *non-reductive* *physicalism* and the *causal* *exclusion* problem. §6 examines *eliminativism*. §7 examines *property* *dualism* and *epiphenomenalism*. §8 gives the *map* and the *criteria*.

---

## 1. The Problem Stated

### 1.1 The data

The *data* of the *mind–body* *problem* are *three* *apparently* *incompatible* *facts*.

**(i) The mental is real.** We have *experiences*, *thoughts*, *desires*, *beliefs*, and *emotions*. The *mental* *states* are *not* *illusions*; they are the *subject* *matter* of *psychology*, and the *content* of *introspection*.

**(ii) The physical is real.** We have *bodies*, *brains*, *neurons*, and *neurotransmitters*. The *physical* *states* are the *subject* *matter* of *physics*, *chemistry*, and *neuroscience*, and they are *causally* *closed* (or *nearly* so; Ch. 18, §4).

**(iii) The mental and the physical are correlated.** *Every* *mental* *state* is *correlated* with a *physical* *state*: *pain* with *C-fiber* *firing*, *visual* *experience* with *occipital* *cortex* *activity*, *decision* with *prefrontal* *activity*. The *correlation* is *not* *accidental*; it *demands* an *explanation*.

**The *problem*.** What is the *relation* between the *mental* and the *physical*?

### 1.2 The options

The *options* are *usually* *presented* as a *menu*, and the *menu* is *the* *map* of the *chapter*.

| Position | The *mental* is | The *physical* is | The *relation* | The *paradigm* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Substance dualism** | A *distinct* *substance* | A *distinct* *substance* | *Interaction* (or *parallelism*) | Descartes |
| **Type identity** | A *type* of *physical* *state* | The *brain* *state* | *Identity* | Place, Smart |
| **Token identity** | A *token* of a *physical* *state* | The *brain* *state* | *Identity* (token) | Davidson |
| **Functionalism** | A *functional* *role* | The *realizer* of the *role* | *Realization* | Putnam, Lewis |
| **Non-reductive physicalism** | *Supervenient* on the *physical* | *Fundamental* | *Supervenience* / *grounding* | Fodor, Kim (early) |
| **Eliminativism** | A *folk* *theory* (false) | *Fundamental* | *Elimination* | Churchland |
| **Property dualism** | A *distinct* *property* | The *brain* *property* | *Emergence* / *supervenience* | Chalmers |
| **Panpsychism** | A *fundamental* *property* | A *fundamental* *property* | *Identity* / *grounding* | Ch. 19, §7 |
| **Idealism** | *Fundamental* | *Derived* | *Construction* | Ch. 20 |
| **Neutral monism** | A *construction* | A *construction* | *Construction* | Ch. 20, §5 |

**The *map*.**


**The *structure*.** The *map* is a *space* of *positions*, and the *positions* differ on *two* *axes*: (i) the *number* of *fundamental* *kinds* (one, two, or many), and (ii) the *relation* between the *mental* and the *physical* (identity, realization, supervenience, causation, elimination).

**The *significance*.** The *positions* are *not* *exhaustive* (the *map* is a *simplification*), and the *boundaries* are *contested* (some *functionalists* are *also* *identity* *theorists*; some *non-reductive* *physicalists* are *also* *property* *dualists*). But the *map* *locates* the *arguments*, and the *arguments* are the *subject* of the *rest* of the *chapter*.

### 1.3 The criteria

**The *criteria* for *choosing* among the *positions***.

| Criterion | The *question* |
|---|---|
| **Consistency** | Is the *position* *internally* *consistent*, and *consistent* with *science*? |
| **Explanatory power** | Does the *position* *explain* the *correlations*, the *causal* *efficacy* of the *mental*, and the *phenomenology*? |
| **Simplicity** | Does the *position* *posits* *fewer* *kinds* of *entity*? |
| **Conservatism** | Does the *position* *preserve* the *data* of *common* *sense* and *introspection*? |
| **Fertility** | Does the *position* *generate* *productive* *research*? |
| **Adequacy** | Does the *position* *account* for *consciousness* (Ch. 24), *intentionality* (Ch. 25), and the *self* (Ch. 26)? |

**The *weighting*.** The *criteria* *conflict* (Ch. 4, §3.2), and the *choice* *depends* on the *weight* given to *each*. The *chapter* *does* not *settle* the *choice*; it *lays* out the *criteria* and *invites* the *reader* to *make* the *judgment*.

## 2. Substance Dualism

### 2.1 The Cartesian picture

**The *thesis*.** The *mind* is a *substance* — a *thing* — that is *distinct* from the *body*, and the *two* *substances* *interact*.

**The *statement*.** Descartes, *Meditations* (1641), II and VI; the *contemporary* *defenders*: Swinburne, *The Evolution of the Soul* (1986; rev. 1997); Foster, *The Immaterial Self* (1991); the *neo-Cartesians* (the *essays* in *The Case for Dualism*, ed. Smythies and Beloff, 1989).

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{I can *doubt* that I have a body (the *sceptical* *premise*; Ch. 6, §2).} \\
2. & \text{I *cannot* *doubt* that I have a *mind* (the *cogito*).} \\
3. & \text{If *A* can be *doubted* and *B* cannot, *A* is *not* *B*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *mind* is *not* the *body* (the *real* *distinction*).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *structure*.** The *argument* *relies* on the *principle* that *conceivability* *entails* *possibility* (Ch. 16, §6.2), and the *principle* is *contested*.

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *conceivability* *objection*** (Arnauld, *Fourth* *Objections*, 1641). The *argument* *proves* only that the *mind* and the *body* are *conceivable* as *distinct*, not that they *are* *distinct*. I can *conceive* that the *morning* *star* is *not* the *evening* *star*, but the *two* are *identical* (Ch. 9, §3.3). The *argument* *confuses* *epistemic* *possibility* with *metaphysical* *possibility*.

**Descartes’ *reply***. The *conceivability* of the *distinction* is *grounded* in the *clear* and *distinct* *perception* of the *two* *natures*, and the *perception* is *guaranteed* by *God*. The *reply* *depends* on the *theological* *premise* (Ch. 6, §2.5), and the *premise* is *contested*.

**(ii) The *interaction* *problem*** (§2.2).

**(iii) The *neural* *dependence* *objection***. The *mind* *depends* on the *brain*: *lesions*, *drugs*, *anesthesia*, and *degeneration* *alter* the *mind*. The *dependence* *suggests* that the *mind* is *not* a *separate* *substance*. (The *dualist* *reply*: the *dependence* is *causal*, not *constitutive*; the *reply* *is* *contested*.)

### 2.2 The interaction problem

**The *problem*.** If the *mind* and the *body* are *distinct* *substances*, how do they *interact*?

**The *argument*** (Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, *Correspondence* with Descartes, 1643).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{A *substance* *acts* on another *only* *if* it is *extended* (the *mechanistic* *picture* of *causation*).} \\
2. & \text{The *mind* is *not* *extended* (the *Cartesian* *definition*).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The *mind* *cannot* *act* on the *body*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *reply***. The *mind* *acts* on the *body* by *moving* the *pineal* *gland* (Descartes’ *solution*), and the *motion* is a *causal* *relation* that does *not* *require* *extension*.

**The *objection*.** The *reply* *assumes* that the *mind* can *cause* *motion* in an *extended* *substance*, and the *assumption* is *precisely* what *Elisabeth* *denies*. The *reply* *begs* the *question*.

**The *contemporary* *form*.** The *problem* is *not* *merely* the *medieval* *mechanics*; it is the *conservation* of *energy*. If the *mind* *causes* *physical* *events*, it *adds* *energy* to the *physical* *world*, and the *addition* *violates* the *conservation* of *energy* (or *requires* a *physical* *mechanism* for the *transfer*). The *dualist* *reply* (the *mind* *directs* the *energy* without *adding* it) is *contested*.

**The *responses*.**

**(i) Occasionalism** (Malebranche, *De la recherche de la vérité*, 1674–75). The *mind* and the *body* do *not* *interact*; *God* *causes* the *correlations* on the *occasion* of the *mental* or *physical* *events*.

**The *objection*.** The *view* *abandons* the *efficacy* of the *mental*, and it *makes* *God* the *only* *cause*.

**(ii) Parallelism** (Leibniz, *Monadology*, 1714). The *mind* and the *body* *run* in *parallel*, like *two* *clocks* *synchronised* by *God*, and they *do* not *interact*.

**The *objection*.** The *view* *is* *ad* *hoc*, and it *does* not *explain* the *correlations*.

**(iii) The *epiphenomenalist* *response*** (Ch. 23, §7). The *body* *causes* the *mind*, but the *mind* does *not* *cause* the *body* (the *mind* is *causally* *idle*).

**The *objection*.** The *view* *denies* the *efficacy* of the *mental*, and the *denial* *is* *counterintuitive* (§7.2).

### 2.3 The modern defenders

**The *revival*.** Substance dualism has been *revived* in the *last* *forty* *years*, and the *revival* is *driven* by the *failure* of *physicalist* *accounts* of *consciousness*.

**The *arguments*.**

**(i) The *argument* from *consciousness*** (Swinburne 1986, 1997; Foster 1991). The *phenomenal* *properties* (Ch. 24, §2) are *not* *reducible* to *physical* *properties*, and the *irreducibility* *supports* a *dualist* *ontology*.

**(ii) The *argument* from *unity*** (Swinburne 1986, Ch. 2). The *unity* of *consciousness* — the *fact* that *experiences* are *unified* in a *single* *subject* — is *not* *explained* by the *physical* *unity* of the *brain*. The *unity* *requires* a *simple* *substance* (the *soul*), and the *simplicity* *supports* *dualism*.

**(iii) The *argument* from *personal* *identity*** (Swinburne 1984). Personal *identity* *across* *time* (Ch. 26) *requires* a *simple*, *persisting* *subject*, and the *subject* *cannot* be the *body* (which *changes*) or the *stream* of *experience* (which *has* no *unity*). The *subject* is the *soul*.

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *interaction* *problem* *remains* (§2.2).**

**(ii) The *neural* *dependence* *remains* (§2.1).**

**(iii) The *simplicity* *of* the *soul* *is* *mysterious*. What *is* the *soul*? If it has *no* *parts*, how can it *have* *experiences* with *different* *contents*? The *question* is *the* *combination* *problem* (Ch. 19, §7.2), *applied* to the *soul*.

**(iv) The *scientific* *image*. The *dualist* *position* is *not* *integrated* into the *scientific* *worldview*, and the *integration* is *a* *virtue* of the *physicalist* *positions*.

**The *verdict*.** Substance *dualism* is *not* *refuted*, but it is *not* *established*. It is a *position* *motivated* by the *explanatory* *failures* of *physicalism*, and it *pays* the *costs* of the *interaction* *problem* and the *scientific* *image*.

## 3. The Identity Theory

### 3.1 Type identity

**The *thesis*.** The *mental* *states* are *identical* to *physical* *states*: *pain* is *C-fiber* *firing*, *belief* is a *brain* *state*, *experience* is a *neural* *state*.

**The *statement*.** Place, “Is Consciousness a Brain Process?” (1956); Smart, “Sensations and Brain Processes” (1959); Feigl, *The “Mental” and the “Physical”* (1967).

**The *argument*** (Smart 1959).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *mental* *states* are *correlated* with *brain* *states* (the *empirical* *premise*).} \\
2. & \text{The *correlation* is *not* *accidental*; it *requires* an *explanation*.} \\
3. & \text{The *best* *explanation* is *identity* (the *simplest* *explanation*; Ch. 14, §4).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *mental* *states* are *identical* to *brain* *states*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *analogy*.** The *identity* is *like* the *water–H₂O* *identity* (Ch. 9, §3.3): it is *a posteriori*, *necessary*, and *discovered* by *science*.

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *avoids* the *interaction* *problem* (the *mental* *is* the *physical*), and it *fits* the *naturalistic* *worldview*.

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *multiple* *realizability* *objection*** (§3.2).

**(ii) The *Leibniz* *law* *objection***. If *pain* = *C-fiber* *firing*, *pain* and *C-fiber* *firing* must have *all* the *same* *properties*. But *pain* is *felt*, and *C-fiber* *firing* is *not* *felt* — or so the *objection* *goes*. (The *reply*: the *felt* *quality* *is* the *C-fiber* *firing* *as* *experienced* from the *inside*; the *two* *descriptions* *are* *two* *ways* of *accessing* the *same* *state*.)

**(iii) The *explanatory* *gap* *objection*** (Levine 1983). Even if the *identity* *is* *true*, it *does* not *explain* *why* the *state* *feels* the *way* it *does* (Ch. 24, §2).

### 3.2 Multiple realizability

**The *argument*** (Putnam, “The Nature of Mental States,” 1967; Fodor, “Special Sciences,” 1974).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *mental* *state* } M \text{ is *realized* by *different* *physical* *states* in *different* *organisms* } (P_1, P_2, P_3, \ldots). \\
2. & \text{If } M \text{ is *identical* to a *physical* *state*, it must be *identical* to *one* of the } P_i. \\
3. & \text{No *single* } P_i \text{ is *common* to *all* the *organisms*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *mental* *state* is *not* *identical* to any *physical* *state*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *examples*.** *Pain* is *realized* by *C-fiber* *firing* in *humans*, by *different* *neural* *states* in *octopi*, by *silicon* *states* in *hypothetical* *AI* *systems*. The *type* *identity* *fails*, and the *higher*-level *properties* are *autonomous* (Ch. 21, §3.2).

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *token* *identity* *response*** (Davidson, “Mental Events,” 1970). The *type* *identity* *fails*, but the *token* *identity* *holds*: *each* *particular* *mental* *event* is *identical* to a *particular* *physical* *event*, even though the *types* are *different*. The *response* *preserves* *physicalism* without *type* *reduction*.

**(ii) The *disjunctive* *response***. The *mental* *type* is *identical* to a *disjunction* of *physical* *types* ($P_1 \vee P_2 \vee P_3 \vee \ldots$). The *response* *saves* the *type* *identity*, but the *disjunction* is *not* a *natural* *kind* (Ch. 15, §5.1), and the *identity* is *uninformative*.

**(iii) The *functional* *response*** (§4). The *mental* *type* is *identical* to a *functional* *type*, and the *functional* *type* is *realized* by *different* *physical* *types*.

### 3.3 The *a posteriori* identity

**The *distinction*.** The *identity* between *mental* and *physical* is *a posteriori*: it is *discovered* *empirically*, not *derived* *a priori*.

**The *significance*.** The *identity* *theorists* *argue* that the *mind–brain* *identity* is *like* the *water–H₂O* *identity*: it is a *scientific* *discovery*, and it is *necessary* *a posteriori* (Ch. 9, §3.3).

**The *objection*.** The *analogy* *fails* for *consciousness*: the *water–H₂O* *identity* *explains* the *observable* *properties* of *water*, but the *mind–brain* *identity* does *not* *explain* the *phenomenal* *properties* (why *pain* *feels* the way it does). The *explanatory* *gap* (Ch. 24, §2) is the *residue*.

### 3.4 The state of the identity theory

**The *assessment*.** Type *identity* is *untenable* for the *special* *sciences* (the *multiple* *realizability* *argument*), and the *token* *identity* is *too* *weak* (it does not *capture* the *dependence*; Ch. 21, §6). The *most* *defensible* *version* is *functionalization* (§4), which *preserves* the *identity* at the *functional* *level* and *defers* the *physical* *realization* to the *sciences*.

## 4. Functionalism

### 4.1 The thesis

**The *view*.** A *mental* *state* is *constituted* by its *causal* *role* — its *relations* to *inputs*, *outputs*, and *other* *mental* *states* — and *not* by its *physical* *realization*. The *mind* is *the* *software* that *runs* on the *brain*’s *hardware*.

**The *statement*.** Putnam, “Minds and Machines” (1960), and “The Nature of Mental States” (1967); Lewis, “An Argument for the Identity Theory” (1966), and “Psychophysical and Theoretical Identifications” (1972); Armstrong, *A Materialist Theory of the Mind* (1968); Fodor, “Special Sciences” (1974).

**The *structure*.**

$$
M = \text{the *state* that *plays* the *causal* *role* } R_M
$$

The *mental* *state* is *the* *occupant* of the *role*; the *physical* *state* is *the* *occupant* at the *physical* *level*.

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *reconciles* the *identity* *theory* with *multiple* *realizability* (the *identity* is at the *functional* *level*, and the *physical* *realization* *varies*), and it *fits* the *computational* *theory* of *mind*.

### 4.2 Machine-state functionalism

**The *thesis*.** The *mind* is a *Turing* *machine*, and the *mental* *states* are the *machine*’s *states*.

**The *statement*.** Putnam (1960, 1967); the *computational* *theory* of *mind* (Fodor 1975, 1983; the *classic* *statements*).

**The *structure*.** A *Turing* *machine* is defined by:

- The *set* of *states* $Q = \{q_1, \ldots, q_n\}$.
- The *input* *alphabet* $\Sigma$.
- The *output* *alphabet* $\Gamma$.
- The *transition* *function* $\delta: Q \times \Sigma \to Q \times \Gamma \times \{L, R\}$.

The *mental* *state* is a *state* $q_i$, and the *transition* *function* *defines* the *causal* *role*.

**The *virtue*.** The *account* *provides* a *precise* *model* of the *causal* *role*, and it *generates* the *research* *programme* of *cognitive* *science*.

**The *objection*.** The *account* *is* *too* *liberal*: *any* *system* with the *right* *transition* *function* *has* *mental* *states*, including *thermostats*, *collections* of *rocks*, and the *economy*. The *objection* is the **liberality* *problem*** (Block 1978), and it is the *central* *objection* to *machine-state* *functionalism*.

### 4.3 Causal-role functionalism

**The *thesis*.** The *mental* *state* is *defined* by its *causal* *role* — the *set* of *causal* *relations* to *inputs*, *outputs*, and *other* *mental* *states* — and the *role* is *constitutive* of the *state*.

**The *statement*.** Lewis (1966, 1972); Armstrong (1968); Block (1980, 1990).

**The *structure*.**

$$
M = \langle \text{inputs}, \text{outputs}, \text{other mental states} \rangle
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *account* *fits* the *common-sense* *psychology* (beliefs and desires are *defined* by their *roles*), and it *avoids* the *machine* *specifics*.

**The *objection*.** The *account* *is* *vague*: what *is* the *causal* *role* of a *belief*? The *role* is *specified* by the *theory*, and the *theory* is *contested*.

### 4.4 The Chinese Room

**The *argument*** (Searle, “Minds, Brains, and Programs,” 1980; *Minds, Brains and Science*, 1984).

**The *scenario*.** Searle is *locked* in a *room*. He is *given* a *book* of *rules* (in *English*) that *tell* him how to *manipulate* *Chinese* *characters* (he *does* not *understand* *Chinese*). *Chinese* *speakers* *pass* *questions* into the *room*, and Searle *follows* the *rules* to *produce* *answers*. The *answers* are *indistinguishable* from those of a *native* *speaker*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Searle *manipulates* *symbols* *according* to *rules* (syntax).} \\
2. & \text{Searle does *not* *understand* *Chinese* (no *semantics*).} \\
3. & \text{The *system* (the *room* + *Searle* + *rules*) *manipulates* *symbols* according to *rules*.} \\
4. & \text{The *system* does *not* *understand* *Chinese* (no *semantics*).} \\
\hline
5. & \therefore \text{Symbol *manipulation* (syntax) is *not* *sufficient* for *understanding* (semantics).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *conclusion*.** The *computational* *theory* of *mind* is *false*: *computation* *is* *syntax*, and *understanding* *requires* *semantics*.

**The *replies*.**

**(i) The *systems* *reply***. The *system* *does* *understand* *Chinese*: the *understanding* is a *property* of the *system*, not of *Searle*. (Searle’s *counter*: the *system* is *defined* by the *rules*, and the *rules* *are* *syntax*; the *system* *has* *no* *semantics*.)

**(ii) The *robot* *reply***. The *system* is *embodied* and *interacts* with the *world*, and the *interaction* *grounds* the *semantics* (the *“symbol* *grounding”* *problem*; Harnad 1990). (Searle’s *counter*: the *robot* *adds* *causal* *relations*, but the *causal* *relations* are *not* *understanding* unless they are *experienced*.)

**(iii) The *brain* *simulator* *reply***. If the *system* *simulates* the *brain*’s *causal* *structure*, it *has* *understanding* (because the *brain* has *understanding*). (Searle’s *counter*: the *simulation* is *not* the *realization*; the *simulation* of *digestion* does not *digest*.)

**The *significance*.** The *argument* is the *most* *influential* *objection* to *machine* *functionalism*, and the *agency* *debate* is *unresolved*. The *argument* *turns* on the *nature* of *understanding*, and the *nature* is the *subject* of Ch. 25.

### 4.5 The state of functionalism

**The *assessment*.** Functionalism is the *dominant* *position* in the *philosophy* of *mind*, and it is *qualified* by the *objections*.

| Version | The *virtue* | The *problem* |
|---|---|---|
| **Machine-state** | Precise; computational | The liberality problem |
| **Causal-role** | Fits common-sense psychology | The role is vague |
| **Homuncular** | Fits the computational architecture | The homunculus problem |
| **Teleological** | Fits the evolutionary history | The teleology is contested (Ch. 42, §2) |

**The *dialectical* *situation*.** Functionalism *captures* the *causal* *role* of the *mental*, and it *fits* the *multiple* *realizability* *argument*. But it *does* not *explain* *consciousness* (Ch. 24, §5), and it *may* be *too* *liberal* (the *liberality* *problem*). The *most* *defensible* *version* is a *causal-role* *functionalism* *combined* with a *teleological* *theory* of *content* (Ch. 25, §2), and the *combination* is *contested*.

## 5. Non-Reductive Physicalism and the Causal Exclusion Problem

### 5.1 The thesis

**The *view*.** The *mental* *supervenes* on the *physical*, but the *mental* *properties* are *not* *identical* to *physical* *properties*, and the *mental* *level* is *autonomous*.

**The *statement*.** Fodor (1974); Putnam (1967); Davidson (1970); Yablo (1992); the *contemporary* *literature*.

**The *structure*.**

$$
\text{Mental properties} \ \xrightarrow{\text{supervene on}} \ \text{Physical properties}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *captures* the *intuition* that the *mental* is *real* and *irreducible*, and it *fits* the *autonomy* of the *special* *sciences* (Ch. 21, §7.3).

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *causal* *exclusion* *argument*** (§5.2).

**(ii) The *explanatory* *gap*** (Ch. 24, §2). The *view* *does* not *explain* *consciousness*.

### 5.2 The causal exclusion argument

**The *argument*** (Kim 1989, 1998, 2005).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *physical* *domain* is *causally* *closed*: every *physical* *event* has a *sufficient* *physical* *cause*.} \\
2. & \text{The *mental* *event* } M \text{ *causes* the *physical* *event* } P. \\
3. & \text{The *physical* *event* } P \text{ has a *sufficient* *physical* *cause* } P^*. \\
4. & \text{If } M \text{ *causes* } P \text{ and } P^* \text{ *causes* } P, \text{ the *cause* is *overdetermined*.} \\
5. & \text{Overdetermination is *implausible* for *every* *case*.} \\
\hline
6. & \therefore \text{The *mental* *event* } M \text{ does *not* *cause* } P \text{ (or *epiphenomenalism* is *true*).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *consequence*.** The *argument* *threatens* the *autonomy* of the *mental*, and it *forces* a *choice* between *reductionism* and *epiphenomenalism*.

**The *responses*.**

**(i) The *reductionist* *response*** (Kim 1998, 2005). The *mental* is *reducible* to the *physical*, and the *reduction* *solves* the *exclusion*. The *cost* is the *loss* of the *autonomy* of the *special* *sciences*.

**(ii) The *emergentist* *response***. The *mental* *emerges* from the *physical* and has *causal* *powers* *not* *reducible* to the *physical*. The *cost* is the *mystery* of *emergence* (Ch. 21, §5).

**(iii) The *interventionist* *response*** (Woodward 2008; List & Menzies 2009). The *exclusion* *argument* *assumes* a *counterfactual* *account* of *causation*; the *interventionist* *account* *allows* *multiple* *causes* at *different* *levels*. The *cost* is the *pluralism* of *causation* (Ch. 18, §5).

**(iv) The *determinable* *response*** (Yablo 1992, 1997). The *mental* *properties* are *determinables* of *physical* *determinates*; the *determinable* is *not* *excluded* by the *determinate* (it is *realized* by it). The *cost* is the *mystery* of *determinables*.

**(v) The *grounding* *response*** (Ch. 16, §3.3). The *mental* *facts* are *grounded* in the *physical*, and the *grounding* *explains* the *dependence* *without* *requiring* *causal* *efficacy* at the *mental* *level*. The *cost* is the *mystery* of *grounding*.

### 5.3 The state of non-reductive physicalism

**The *assessment*.** Non-reductive *physicalism* is the *most* *defensible* *position* in the *philosophy* of *mind*, and it is *qualified* by the *exclusion* *argument* and the *explanatory* *gap*.

**The *qualified* *position*.** The *mental* *supervenes* on the *physical*, the *mental* *level* is *autonomous* *in* *explanation*, and the *causal* *efficacy* of the *mental* is *real* but *not* *competing* with the *physical* *causation* (the *interventionist* *response*). The *position* is *defensible*, and it is *incomplete*.

## 6. Eliminativism and Instrumentalism

### 6.1 The thesis

**The *view*.** The *folk* *psychological* *states* (beliefs, desires, pains) *do* not *exist*; the *folk* *theory* is *false*, and it *should* be *replaced* by *neuroscience*.

**The *statement*.** Churchland, “Eliminative Materialism and the Propositional Attitudes” (1981), and *Neurophilosophy* (1986); Churchland, *Neurophilosophy* (1986), and *Brain-Wise* (2002); Stich, *From Folk Psychology to Cognitive Science* (1983).

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *folk* *theory* *posits* *theoretical* *entities* (beliefs, desires) to *explain* *behaviour*.} \\
2. & \text{The *theory* is *stagnant* and *incomplete* (it does not *explain* *mental* *illness*, *dreams*, *memory*, *learning*).} \\
3. & \text{The *theory* is *scientifically* *inferior* to *neuroscience* (the *replacement* *theory*).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *folk* *entities* *do* not *exist*; they *should* be *eliminated*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *analogy*.** The *elimination* is *like* the *elimination* of *phlogiston* (Ch. 11, §1.1) and *caloric*: the *theory* was *replaced*, and the *entities* were *not* *reduced* to *other* *entities*; they were *eliminated*.

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *takes* *neuroscience* *seriously*, and it *avoids* the *exclusion* *problem* (the *mental* is *not* *real*, so there is *nothing* to *exclude*).

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *self-refutation* *objection*. The *eliminativist* *argument* is *itself* a *piece* of *folk* *psychology* (it *uses* *beliefs* and *desires* to *explain* the *theory*’s *failure*), and the *argument* *undermines* *itself*.

**(ii) The *explanatory* *success* *objection*. The *folk* *theory* is *spectacularly* *successful*: it *predicts* the *behaviour* of *conspecifics*, and it *supports* *social* *interaction*, *law*, *morality*, and *psychology*. The *success* *is* *evidence* that the *entities* are *real* (Ch. 10, §6).

**(iii) The *introspective* *objection*. We *know* our *own* *beliefs* and *desires* *by* *introspection* (Ch. 12, §6; Ch. 26, §6), and the *introspective* *evidence* is *not* *eliminated* by the *neuroscience*. (The *eliminativist* *reply*: the *introspective* *evidence* is *itself* a *construction* of the *brain*, and it is *compatible* with *eliminativism*.)

### 6.2 Instrumentalism

**The *thesis***. The *folk* *psychological* *states* *do* not *exist* as *entities*, but the *talk* of them is *a* *useful* *instrument* for *prediction* and *communication* (the *“intentional* *stance”*; Dennett 1987, 1991).

**The *statement*.** Dennett, *The Intentional Stance* (1987), and *Consciousness Explained* (1991).

**The *structure*.** The *intentional* *stance* is a *strategy*: *treat* the *system* as if it had *beliefs* and *desires*, and *predict* its *behaviour* from the *predictions*. The *stance* is *justified* by its *predictive* *success*, and *not* by the *existence* of the *entities*.

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *avoids* the *eliminativist* *consequence* (the *folk* *psychology* *survives* as an *instrument*) and the *realist* *consequence* (the *entities* are *real*).

**The *objection*.** The *view* *is* *unstable*: if the *entities* are *not* *real*, why does the *stance* *work*? (The *reply*: the *stance* *works* because the *brain* *realizes* the *functional* *roles*, and the *roles* are *real*; the *reply* *collapses* into *functionalism* or *interpretivism*, and the *collapse* is *contested*.)

### 6.3 The state of the debate

| Position | The *mental* *states* | The *strength* | The *weakness* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Realism** | Real; reducible or supervenient | Fits the success of psychology | The exclusion problem |
| **Instrumentalism** | Useful fictions | Fits the predictive success | The nature of the fictions |
| **Eliminativism** | Non-existent | Fits neuroscience | Self-refutation; explanatory success |

**The *assessment*.** Eliminativism is a *minority* *position*, and it is *taken* *seriously*. The *most* *defensible* *version* is a *qualified* *instrumentalism*: the *folk* *entities* are *real* to the *extent* that the *functional* *roles* are *realized*, and the *realization* is the *subject* of *neuroscience*.

## 7. Property Dualism and Epiphenomenalism

### 7.1 Property dualism

**The *thesis*.** The *mental* *properties* are *real*, *irreducible* to *physical* *properties*, and *distinct* from them. There is *one* *substance* (the *physical*), but *two* *kinds* of *property*.

**The *statement*.** Chalmers, *The Conscious Mind* (1996); Jackson, “Epiphenomenal Qualia” (1982), and *From Metaphysics to Ethics* (1998); the *contemporary* *defenders*.

**The *argument*** (Chalmers 1996, Ch. 4).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *phenomenal* *properties* (qualia) are *not* *reducible* to *physical* *properties* (the *knowledge* *argument*; Ch. 24, §3).} \\
2. & \text{The *phenomenal* *properties* are *real* (they are *the* *content* of *experience*).} \\
3. & \text{The *physical* *properties* are *real* (the *physical* *world* *exists*).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *phenomenal* *properties* are *distinct* from the *physical* *properties*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *preserves* the *irreducibility* of *consciousness*, and it *avoids* the *dualist* *ontology* (there is only one substance).

**The *objection*.** The *view* *faces* the *exclusion* *problem* (§5.2): if the *phenomenal* *properties* are *distinct* and *irreducible*, they are *causally* *idle* (epiphenomenalism).

### 7.2 Epiphenomenalism

**The *thesis*.** The *mental* *states* are *caused* by the *physical* *states*, but they do *not* *cause* the *physical* *states*.

**The *statement*.** Huxley, “On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata” (1874); Jackson (1982); the *contemporary* *literature*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *physical* *domain* is *causally* *closed* (§5.2).} \\
2. & \text{The *mental* *states* are *distinct* from the *physical* *states* (property *dualism*).} \\
3. & \text{If the *mental* *states* *caused* the *physical*, the *physical* *domain* would not be *closed*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *mental* *states* do *not* *cause* the *physical* *states*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *avoids* the *interaction* *problem*, and it *fits* the *causal* *closure* of *physics*.

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *epiphenomenalist* *objection* (the *“self-stultification”* *argument*). If the *mental* *states* are *causally* *idle*, then the *belief* that *epiphenomenalism* is *true* is *causally* *idle*, and the *belief* *cannot* be *justified* by the *evidence*. The *view* *undermines* the *justification* of *the* *view*.

**(ii) The *common-sense* *objection*. We *know* that *our* *thoughts* *cause* *our* *actions*: the *decision* to *raise* *my* *arm* *causes* the *arm* to *rise*. The *denial* is *counterintuitive*.

**(iii) The *evolutionary* *objection*. If the *mental* *states* are *causally* *idle*, *natural* *selection* *would* *not* have *produced* them (they would be *selected* *against*, because they *cost* *energy*). The *mental* *states* *must* have *causal* *efficacy* to be *selected*.

**The *state* of the debate.** Epiphenomenalism is *a* *consequence* of *property* *dualism* *combined* with the *causal* *closure*, and it is *contested*. The *most* *defensible* *version* is *a* *form* of *dual-aspect* *monism* (Ch. 20, §5.2), in which the *mental* and the *physical* are *two* *aspects* of a *single* *causal* *process*, and the *mental* *aspect* is *not* *causally* *idle* (it is *the* *same* *causal* *process* *described* *differently*).

### 7.3 The cost of each position

**The *table*.**

| Position | The *mental* *is* | The *causal* *efficacy* | The *main* *cost* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Substance dualism** | A distinct substance | Real, but interaction is mysterious | The interaction problem |
| **Type identity** | The brain state | Real (it is the physical) | Multiple realizability |
| **Functionalism** | A functional role | Real (it is the realization) | The liberality problem |
| **Non-reductive physicalism** | Supervenient | Real (via grounding) | The exclusion problem |
| **Eliminativism** | Non-existent | N/A | Self-refutation |
| **Property dualism** | A distinct property | Real (if emergent) | The exclusion problem |
| **Epiphenomenalism** | A distinct property | Idle | Self-stultification |
| **Panpsychism** | Fundamental | Real (it is the physical) | The combination problem |

**The *verdict*.** The *choice* is a *trade-off* among the *costs*, and *no* *position* is *free* of a *serious* *problem*. The *most* *defensible* *position* is *non-reductive* *physicalism* *combined* with the *interventionist* *response* to the *exclusion* *argument*, and the *combination* is *qualified* by the *admission* that *consciousness* (Ch. 24) is *not* *explained*.

## 8. The Map and the Criteria

### 8.1 The dialectical map

**The *map*.**

| Position | The *mental* is | The *strength* | The *weakness* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Substance dualism** | A distinct substance | Preserves the mental | Interaction problem |
| **Type identity** | The brain state | Simple; reductive | Multiple realizability |
| **Token identity** | A token of a brain state | Preserves physicalism | Too weak |
| **Machine functionalism** | A Turing state | Precise; computational | Liberality problem |
| **Causal-role functionalism** | A causal role | Fits common-sense psychology | The role is vague |
| **Non-reductive physicalism** | Supervenient | Preserves autonomy | The exclusion problem |
| **Eliminativism** | Non-existent | Fits neuroscience | Self-refutation |
| **Property dualism** | A distinct property | Preserves consciousness | The exclusion problem |
| **Epiphenomenalism** | A causally idle property | Fits physical closure | Self-stultification |
| **Panpsychism** | Fundamental | Fits consciousness | The combination problem |
| **Idealism** | Fundamental | Fits consciousness | The common-sense problem |
| **Neutral monism** | Construction | Avoids dualism | The neutral stuff |

**The *lesson*.** The *map* is a *space* of *positions*, and the *positions* differ on *two* *axes*: (i) the *number* of *fundamental* *kinds* (one, two, or many), and (ii) the *relation* between the *mental* and the *physical* (identity, realization, supervenience, causation, elimination).

### 8.2 The criteria

**The *criteria* *applied*.**

| Criterion | The *best* *position* |
|---|---|
| **Consistency** | Functionalism; non-reductive physicalism |
| **Explanatory power** | Non-reductive physicalism; panpsychism |
| **Simplicity** | Type identity; eliminativism |
| **Conservatism** | Substance dualism; common-sense functionalism |
| **Fertility** | Functionalism; non-reductive physicalism |
| **Adequacy** | Panpsychism; property dualism (for consciousness) |

**The *tension*.** The *criteria* *conflict*: the *simplest* *positions* (identity, eliminativism) *fail* on *adequacy*; the *most* *adequate* *positions* (panpsychism, substance *dualism*) *fail* on *simplicity* and *conservatism*. The *choice* is a *judgment* (Ch. 4, §3.2).

### 8.3 The honest verdict

**The *state* of the debate.** The *mind–body* *problem* is *unresolved*. The *most* *defensible* *position* is a *form* of *non-reductive* *physicalism* *qualified* by the *interventionist* *response* to the *exclusion* *argument* and by the *admission* that *consciousness* is *not* *explained*. The *position* is *defensible*, and it is *incomplete*.

**The *open* *questions*.**

1. The *nature* of *consciousness* (Ch. 24).
2. The *nature* of *intentionality* (Ch. 25).
3. The *nature* of the *self* (Ch. 26).
4. The *efficacy* of the *mental* (the *exclusion* *problem*).
5. The *status* of the *folk* *psychology* (the *eliminativist* *debate*).

**The *methodological* *lesson*. The *chapter* is a *case* *study* in the *method* of *Part* I.

- **Conceptual analysis** (§1 of Ch. 3) generated the *distinction* between the *mental* and the *physical*.
- **Thought experiments** (§2 of Ch. 3) — the *Chinese* *Room*, the *zombie* (Ch. 24), the *multiple* *realizability* *cases* — are the *engine* of the *debate*.
- **Formalization** (§3 of Ch. 3) — the *Turing* *machine*, the *causal* *role* — is *essential* for *functionalism*.
- **Reflective equilibrium** (§4 of Ch. 3) is the *method* by which the *intuitions* and the *theories* are *adjusted*.
- **Negative capability** (§7 of Ch. 3) is the *response* to the *residue*: the *humility* to *hold* the *question* *open*.

**The *honest* *verdict*.** The *theory* of *mind* is a *field* in *transition*. The *classical* *positions* are *untenable* in their *pure* forms; the *contemporary* *positions* are *promising* but *incomplete*; the *debate* is *unresolved*. The *unresolved* character is *instructive*: it *shows* what *philosophical* *progress* looks like — not a *settled* *answer*, but a *sharper* *understanding* of what the *question* is.

## Summary

- **The *mind–body* *problem* is the *problem* of the *relation* between the *mental* and the *physical*.** The *data* are *three*: the *mental* is *real*, the *physical* is *real*, and the *two* are *correlated*.
- **The *options* are a *space* of *positions*:** substance dualism, identity theory, functionalism, non-reductive physicalism, eliminativism, property dualism, panpsychism, idealism, neutral monism.
- **Substance dualism** *preserves* the *mental*, but it *faces* the *interaction* *problem* (how do the two substances *interact*?) and the *neural* *dependence* *objection*.
- **The identity theory** *avoids* the *interaction* *problem*, but it *faces* the *multiple* *realizability* *argument* (the same mental state is realized by different physical states).
- **Functionalism** *reconciles* the identity theory with multiple realizability, but it *faces* the *liberality* *problem* (too many systems count as minds) and the *Chinese* *Room* (syntax is not semantics).
- **Non-reductive physicalism** *preserves* the *autonomy* of the *mental*, but it *faces* the *causal* *exclusion* *argument* (the mental is causally idle).
- **Eliminativism** *takes* *neuroscience* *seriously*, but it *faces* the *self-refutation* *objection* and the *explanatory* *success* of *folk* *psychology*.
- **Property dualism** *preserves* *consciousness*, but it *faces* the *exclusion* *problem*; **epiphenomenalism** *avoids* the *interaction* *problem*, but it *faces* the *self-stultification* *objection*.
- **The *most* *defensible* *position* is a *qualified* *non-reductive* *physicalism*, *combined* with the *interventionist* *response* to the *exclusion* *argument*, and *qualified* by the *admission* that *consciousness* is *not* *explained*.
- **The *debate* is *unresolved*, and the *unresolved* character is *instructive*: it shows what philosophical progress looks like — not a settled answer, but a sharper understanding of what the question is.

> *A closing note.* This chapter has followed the *mind–body* *problem* through the *classical* *positions* — substance dualism, identity theory, functionalism, non-reductive physicalism, eliminativism, property dualism, epiphenomenalism — and it has *arrived* at a *conclusion* that is *less* a *theory* than a *diagnosis*: the problem is a *family* of *problems*, the *positions* are a *space* of *trade-offs*, and the *choice* is a *judgment* rather than a *calculation*. The *diagnosis* is *not* a *refutation* of *the* *positions*; it is a *description* of the *conditions* under which the *choice* is *made*.

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter turns to *consciousness* — the *hard* *problem* that the *chapter* has *deferred*. The *question* of this *chapter* was the *relation* between the *mental* and the *physical*; the *question* of the *next* is the *nature* of the *phenomenal* — the *“what* *it* *is* *like”* of *experience* — and the *question* is the *most* *difficult* in *philosophy*. The *mind–body* *problem* *presupposes* the *existence* of *consciousness*, and the *next* *chapter* *examines* the *presupposition*.
