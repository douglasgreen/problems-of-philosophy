> — Attributed to many; practiced by few.

## 1. Assertions, Questions, and the Pseudo-Question

Philosophy begins with a sentence that can be true or false. This sounds trivial. It is not. A great deal of bad philosophy, and a great deal of good philosophy gone astray, consists in treating a question as though it had a propositional answer when it does not, or treating an assertion as though it were a question when it already presupposes its own answer.

### 1.1 Three kinds of utterance

Consider three utterances:

1. *“The table is solid.”*
2. *“Is the table solid?”*
3. *“Please pass the salt.”*

Only (1) has a **truth value**. Utterance (2) is not true or false; it is a *request for information*, and it is answered by an assertion. Utterance (3) is an *action* — an order or a request — and it is assessed as appropriate or inappropriate, polite or rude, effective or ineffective, but not as true or false.

The distinction is not merely grammatical. It corresponds to a difference in **direction of fit** (Anscombe 1957; Searle 1969), which we will use repeatedly in this book (Ch. 28, §3). An assertion is supposed to fit the world: if the table is not solid, the assertion fails. A command is supposed to make the world fit it: if the salt is not passed, the command fails. Questions sit awkwardly between: they presuppose a possible answer and request the world-fitting direction.

For a working philosopher, the practical upshot is this: **before you argue, make sure you are arguing about a proposition.** Most philosophical disputes are disputes about which proposition is true. Some are disputes about which question to ask. A few are disputes about whether there is anything to dispute.

### 1.2 The pseudo-question

What is a **pseudo-question**? Not simply a question we cannot answer. “What is the mass of the universe?” is a question we may never answer; it is not thereby pseudo. A pseudo-question is a question that *appears* to have a propositional answer but in fact does not — because its presuppositions fail, or because it is a verbal dispute, or because the answer is stipulated by the question itself.

Four diagnostic types are worth distinguishing. Each is a tool, and each has its failure modes.

**(a) Presupposition failure.** “Why did the King of France invade Belgium?” presupposes that there is a King of France. If there is not, the question is not hard; it is *defective*. The right response is not to speculate about his motives but to reject the presupposition.

Russell’s theory of descriptions (Ch. 12, §2) was designed precisely to handle this. “The present King of France is bald” is analyzed not as a subject–predicate sentence whose subject fails to refer, but as a conjunction of three claims:

$$\exists x(\text{King}(x) \wedge \text{France}(x) \wedge \forall y((\text{King}(y) \wedge \text{France}(y)) \to y = x) \wedge \text{Bald}(x))$$

The sentence is false (not truth-valueless), because the existential conjunct fails. The technique generalizes: when a question seems unanswerable, ask **what it presupposes**, and then ask whether the presupposition is true. Often the honest answer is that the presupposition is a substantive thesis that has not been argued.

**(b) Category mistakes.** “What is the average color of the number seven?” The question is not hard; it applies a predicate to a category of thing to which the predicate does not apply. Gilbert Ryle (1949) diagnosed the mind–body problem partly in these terms: the question “Where is the mind?” is defective if minds are not the kind of thing that has a spatial location — just as “Where is the university?” is defective if universities are not buildings (though see Ch. 23, §1.3, for the objection that this is too quick).

The category-mistake diagnosis is powerful but dangerous. It is too easy to declare one’s opponents guilty of a category mistake instead of answering them. The burden is on the diagnostician to say *which* category is being confused and *why* the predicate does not apply. Wittgenstein’s later method (Ch. 3, §7) is a sustained exercise in this burden.

**(c) Verbal dispute.** A dispute is verbal when the parties agree on all the facts but disagree about the meaning or use of a word. “Is a tomato a fruit?” — if both parties agree that tomatoes are the seed-bearing ovaries of a flowering plant and also that they are used in salads, the question “Is it a fruit?” is settled by stipulating which sense of “fruit” is in play.

The hard case, and the philosophically important one, is the dispute that is *partly* verbal and partly substantive. David Chalmers (2011) offers a method for diagnosing these: **ask what the parties would say if they agreed about the word.** If the disagreement evaporates, it was verbal. If a residue remains, that residue is the substantive question, and it should be stated in neutral terms. The method does not always work (it presupposes that the parties can identify what they “would say,” which is exactly what is contested), but it is a useful first pass. We will apply it to several disputes in this book, notably in Ch. 20, §7 (what would settle idealism?), Ch. 22, §5 (is metaphysics a verbal dispute?), and Ch. 33, §4.3.

**(d) Stipulative or trivial questions.** “Is a bachelor an unmarried man?” If “bachelor” is *defined* as “unmarried man,” the question is answered by the definition, and there is nothing to investigate. The danger is that a question that looks substantive is in fact definitional, and the investigator wastes time trying to discover the answer to a definition. Conversely, a question that looks definitional may be substantive: “Is knowledge justified true belief?” cannot be settled by definition, as Gettier showed (Ch. 7, §2), because “knowledge” is a *philosophically loaded* term whose application is precisely what is at issue.

### 1.3 The test for a genuine question

The single best test is this:

> **Can you say, in advance, what would count as an answer — and what would count as evidence for it?**

This test does not require that the answer be *available*. It requires that the *shape* of an answer be specifiable. For “What is the mass of the universe?” the shape is clear (a number in kilograms, with error bars) even though the measurement is not available. For “Why is there something rather than nothing?” the shape is much less clear — is the answer a cause, a reason, a necessity, a brute fact? — and the question’s genuineness is correspondingly contested (Ch. 1, §3). For “Is the number seven blue?” there is no shape at all, which is why we call it a pseudo-question.

The test is a *heuristic*, not a decision procedure. Some genuinely deep questions have no antecedently specifiable shape, because part of the answer is a new conception of what an answer would look like. But when a question fails the test *and* there is no independent reason to expect a conceptual innovation, we should suspect a pseudo-question.

### 1.4 A diagnostic checklist

Before beginning work on a question, run it through the following:

| Test | Question to ask | If it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Truth-aptness** | Could the answer be true or false? | It is an order, an expression, or a performative |
| **Presupposition** | What must be the case for the question to arise? | Reject or examine the presupposition |
| **Category** | Is the predicate applicable to the subject? | Diagnose the category mistake |
| **Verbality** | Would agreement on the word settle it? | Restate substantively or declare it verbal |
| **Shape** | What would count as an answer? | Examine whether the question is well-formed |
| **Stakes** | What turns on the answer? | Distinguish intellectual from practical import |

The checklist is not a substitute for thinking; it is a way of avoiding the commonest errors in the first hour of work. We will return to it in Appendix E, where it becomes a worksheet.

## 2. The Premise–Conclusion Architecture

An **argument**, in the philosopher’s sense, is a set of propositions in which some (the **premises**) are offered as support for another (the **conclusion**). This is a technical sense of the word. In ordinary English, “argument” often means a quarrel. In philosophy, a quarrel may contain no arguments at all.

### 2.1 The standard form

We standardize arguments into a numbered list, with the conclusion clearly marked:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{All humans are mortal.} \\
2. & \text{Socrates is a human.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{Socrates is mortal.}
\end{array}
$$

Standardization is not pedantry. It forces the arguer to say *what exactly* is being claimed, and it exposes the *gaps*. An argument that cannot be standardized is usually an argument that has not been thought through.

**The premises may be any number, including one.** “It is raining; therefore the street is wet” has one premise and relies on an implicit generalization. **The conclusion is one proposition** — a common error is to write a multi-sentence “conclusion” that is really two conclusions, each needing separate support. **The order is not the argument**: premises may appear after the conclusion, and the argument’s validity does not depend on presentation order.

### 2.2 Validity

An argument is **valid** if and only if it is impossible for all the premises to be true and the conclusion false. Equivalently: the conclusion follows from the premises; the premises *entail* the conclusion.

Validity is a property of the **form**, not of the content. The following two arguments have the same form and are both valid:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{All cats are mammals.} \\
2. & \text{Felix is a cat.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{Felix is a mammal.}
\end{array}
\qquad\qquad
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{All unicorns are immortal.} \\
2. & \text{Shadowfax is a unicorn.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{Shadowfax is immortal.}
\end{array}
$$

The second argument is valid — *if* its premises were true, its conclusion would have to be true. It is also unsound, because its premises are (probably) false. The point is worth dwelling on: **validity is cheap.** Any argument can be made valid by adding a premise. The interesting questions are whether the premises are true and whether the validity is non-trivial.

**Formal validity** is validity in virtue of logical form alone, where the form is expressed in a formal language with a semantics (Ch. 35, §2). **Material validity** (sometimes called *semantic* validity, or validity in the wide sense) is validity that depends on the meanings of the non-logical terms. “A is a bachelor; therefore A is unmarried” is materially valid, because of what “bachelor” means, but it is not formally valid in the language of first-order logic without adding a meaning postulate.

There is a live debate about whether all good arguments are formally valid, or whether material validity is a genuine and irreducible kind (see Brandom 1994, 2000; and the discussion of inferentialism in Ch. 31, §3). This book treats both as legitimate, but insists that formal validity is the clearer and more testable notion, and should be used wherever possible.

### 2.3 Soundness

An argument is **sound** if and only if it is valid **and** all of its premises are true. Soundness is the property you want. It is much harder to achieve than validity, because it requires you to *establish the premises*, and establishing a premise typically requires a further argument.

This generates the **regress of justification** (Ch. 8, §1). Every argument rests on premises; premises need support; support is further argument; and so on. The regress is not a refutation of the possibility of knowledge — foundationalists, coherentists, and infinitists offer competing accounts of where it stops (Ch. 8) — but it is a permanent structural feature of argument, and the philosopher must always be prepared to say *why* the premises are acceptable.

There is a stronger notion, **demonstrative soundness** (or *cogency*): a sound argument whose premises are *known* to be true, or at least *justified* for the person advancing it. This is what a good philosophical argument should aim at. A valid argument from premises that are merely asserted is not a proof; it is a sketch of a proof.

### 2.4 Deductive and ampliative inference

The distinction between **deductive** and **ampliative** inference is fundamental and frequently blurred.

| Feature | Deductive | Ampliative (inductive, abductive) |
|---|---|---|
| Conclusion follows with **necessity** | Yes | No |
| Possible for premises true and conclusion false | No | Yes |
| Information in conclusion | Contained in premises | Goes beyond premises |
| Defeasible by new evidence | No | Yes |
| Canonical example | *Modus ponens* | Enumerative induction, IBE |
| Goodness is | Validity | Strength / support |

The distinction is sometimes put as follows: deductive inference is **truth-preserving**, ampliative inference is **truth-extending**. Deduction guarantees that you will not lose truth; amplification allows you to gain it, at the cost of a risk of error.

**Induction** is the inference from observed instances to unobserved ones. *All observed emeralds are green; therefore the next emerald is green.* **Statistical syllogism**: *90% of Fs are G; a is an F; therefore (probably) a is G.* **Abduction** (inference to the best explanation): *the hypothesis H would explain the data; no available alternative explains it as well; therefore H is true.* We will treat abduction in detail in §5 below and in Ch. 10.

The classical empiricist tradition (Hume, Mill, Russell in Ch. VI) treated induction as the central ampliative inference. The twentieth century added abduction and probability theory, and the twenty-first century added a great deal of formal machinery. The structural point for now:

> **Most interesting philosophical arguments are ampliative.** They offer considerations that *support* a conclusion — that make it more probable, or more reasonable, or the best available hypothesis — without entailing it.

This has a consequence that beginning students often miss: **objecting to an ampliative argument does not require showing that the conclusion is false.** It is enough to show that the support is weaker than claimed — that the inference is not as strong as the arguer needs it to be. Conversely, defending an ampliative argument does not require showing that the premises entail the conclusion; it requires showing that the conclusion is *well-supported* relative to the alternatives.

### 2.5 The forms worth knowing

The following table lists the inference forms that appear most often in philosophical argument. Learn them; they are the grammar of the subject.

| Form | Pattern | Valid? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Modus ponens* | $p \to q;\ p;\ \therefore q$ | Valid | The workhorse |
| *Modus tollens* | $p \to q;\ \neg q;\ \therefore \neg p$ | Valid | Often confused with denying the antecedent |
| Hypothetical syllogism | $p \to q;\ q \to r;\ \therefore p \to r$ | Valid | |
| Disjunctive syllogism | $p \vee q;\ \neg p;\ \therefore q$ | Valid | |
| Affirming the consequent | $p \to q;\ q;\ \therefore p$ | **Invalid** | Very common in bad philosophy |
| Denying the antecedent | $p \to q;\ \neg p;\ \therefore \neg q$ | **Invalid** | Equally common |
| Reductio ad absurdum | Assume $p$; derive $\bot$; $\therefore \neg p$ | Valid | The engine of most metaphysical argument |
| *Ex falso* | $\bot;\ \therefore q$ | Valid (classically) | Paraconsistent logicians demur (Ch. 35, §5) |
| Constructive dilemma | $p \vee q;\ p \to r;\ q \to s;\ \therefore r \vee s$ | Valid | |
| Universal instantiation | $\forall x Fx;\ \therefore Fa$ | Valid | |
| Existential generalization | $Fa;\ \therefore \exists x Fx$ | Valid | |

Two forms deserve special comment because they are so often misused.

**Affirming the consequent** is the form of many bad arguments from success. “If the theory were true, it would make accurate predictions; it makes accurate predictions; therefore it is true.” This is invalid — the predictions could be accurate for other reasons. The valid version, *modus tollens*, is much weaker: “If the theory were true, it would make accurate predictions; it does not; therefore it is false.” This is why falsification (Ch. 40, §2) is logically cleaner than verification, and why the *no-miracles* argument for scientific realism (Ch. 40, §5) requires careful formulation: it is an inference to the best explanation, not a deductive argument, and must be assessed as such.

**Reductio** is the engine of most *a priori* philosophy, but it has a characteristic danger: the derived absurdity is often only absurd *given further assumptions*. When a philosopher says “this leads to absurdity,” ask: (i) what exactly is the absurdity? (ii) is it really absurd, or merely surprising? (iii) does the absurdity follow from the position alone, or from the position plus something the opponent need not accept? The best *reductios* derive a formal contradiction, or an implication that violates a principle the opponent has explicitly endorsed. The weakest derive a consequence the audience finds distasteful.

### 2.6 What validity does not give you

Three cautions, each of which will recur.

**(i) Validity is not persuasiveness.** A valid argument with premises your audience rejects persuades no one; a fallacious argument with premises your audience accepts often persuades everyone. The philosopher’s task is not to win but to be right (see Ch. 4, §4, on the ethics of discourse), and the gap between the two is where most rhetoric lives.

**(ii) Validity is not relevance.** An argument can be valid and irrelevant to the question at issue. This happens constantly in philosophy: a valid argument is produced for a conclusion that is not the one under dispute. The motte-and-bailey (Ch. 4, §2) is the pathological form.

**(iii) Validity is not soundness, and soundness is not knowledge.** You can have a sound argument whose premises you believe for bad reasons, and a valid argument whose conclusion you cannot know because the premises are merely probable. The epistemologist’s question — *is this a good way to form the belief?* — is not answered by logic alone (Ch. 8).

## 3. Hidden Premises and the Principle of Charity

### 3.1 Enthymemes

Most real arguments are **enthymematic**: they leave premises unstated because they are obvious, or because the arguer has not noticed them, or because stating them would invite objection. The classic example:

> “Socrates is a man; therefore he is mortal.”

The suppressed premise is *(all men are mortal)*. Without it, the argument is invalid. With it, the argument is valid — and the question becomes whether the premise is true, which is a different question from whether the conclusion follows.

The task of reconstruction is therefore twofold: **make the argument valid** (supply the missing premises), and **make it assessable** (state the premises in a form whose truth can be evaluated). These two aims can conflict. A premise sufficient to make the argument valid may be so weak that it is uninteresting, or so strong that it is obviously false. A premise sufficient to make the argument interesting may be too weak to make it valid.

**Example.** Consider:

> “If determinism is true, no one could have done otherwise. Therefore determinism is incompatible with free will.”

Reconstruction 1 (minimal): *(If determinism is true, no one could have done otherwise; if no one could have done otherwise, determinism is incompatible with free will; therefore …)* — valid, but the second premise simply restates the conclusion. Useless.

Reconstruction 2 (substantive): *(Free will requires the ability to do otherwise; determinism excludes the ability to do otherwise; therefore free will and determinism are incompatible.)* — valid, and the premises are substantive and contestable. This is the reconstruction to work with, because it exposes the *real* dispute: whether free will requires the ability to do otherwise (Ch. 27, §2).

The skill is choosing the reconstruction that makes the argument **strongest but not stronger than the evidence allows** — and then saying which reconstruction you are using.

### 3.2 The underdetermination of reconstruction

Here is a hard truth: **the missing premises are usually underdetermined by the text.** Many different premise sets will make the argument valid. Which one “the” argument is cannot be read off the words; it requires an interpretive judgment about what the arguer *meant* or *would have accepted*.

This is a special case of the general problem of interpretation (Ch. 31, §6). It has three practical consequences.

1. **State your reconstruction explicitly**, and mark it as a reconstruction. “On the most charitable reading, the argument is …” is honest; “The argument is …” is often a fiction.
2. **Consider multiple reconstructions** before settling. The best objection to an argument may target a reconstruction other than the one you first saw.
3. **Check the reconstruction against the arguer’s other commitments.** If the reconstruction requires a premise the arguer explicitly denies elsewhere, it is probably wrong.

### 3.3 The principle of charity

The **principle of charity** (Wilson 1959; Quine 1960; Davidson 1974) says: interpret a speaker so as to maximize the truth or reasonableness of what she says, consistent with the evidence of what she actually said. The rationale is partly semantic — radical interpretation is only possible if we assume mostly-true beliefs and mostly-valid inferences — and partly methodological: the point of engaging with an argument is to engage with its *best* version.

Charity operates at three levels:

| Level | What is charitably interpreted | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Semantic** | The meanings of the words | Reading “free” as “uncoerced” rather than “uncaused” when context suggests it |
| **Logical** | The inferential connections | Supplying the missing premise rather than declaring the argument invalid |
| **Substantive** | The plausibility of the premises | Choosing the reading under which the premises are defensible |

Charity is not the same as **agreement**, and it is not the same as **weakness**. To interpret an argument charitably is to engage with its strongest form, which is the opposite of making it easy to refute. A charitable reconstruction often makes an argument *harder* to answer.

### 3.4 The limits of charity

Charity has limits, and they are important, because a principle without limits becomes a license.

**Limit 1: fidelity to the text.** Charity cannot license a reconstruction that the arguer’s words will not bear. If the text says “all men are mortal” and you reconstruct it as “most men are mortal,” you have not interpreted; you have replaced. The principle is *charitable interpretation*, not *charitable invention*.

**Limit 2: the arguer’s own standards.** An argument must be assessed by the standards the arguer accepts, or by standards that can be argued for. If a philosopher claims deductive certainty, it is not charitable to reconstruct her argument as a probabilistic one; it is a change of subject.

**Limit 3: the possibility of error.** Sometimes the charitable reading is not the correct one, because the arguer really did make a mistake. Over-charity obscures real errors, and real errors are worth finding. The historian of philosophy has a special obligation here: the point of reading Leibniz is partly to see *what he actually thought*, including what he got wrong.

**Limit 4: the strategic use of charity.** Charity is a virtue of the interpreter, not a demand the arguer can make. “You’re not reading me charitably” is sometimes a legitimate complaint and sometimes a way of avoiding a real objection. The test is whether the uncharitable reading is *available* to a competent reader of the text.

The balanced position, which this book adopts, is what we might call **strong charity**: reconstruct the argument in its strongest form that the text will bear, state the reconstruction explicitly, and then criticize it — and, having criticized it, ask whether the arguer had a reply available.

## 4. Argument Maps, Dialectical Trees, and Steel-Manning

### 4.1 Argument maps

Prose is a poor medium for arguments. It hides structure, encourages irrelevance, and makes it hard to see where the weight of an argument lies. **Argument mapping** is the remedy.

An argument map represents:

- **Nodes**: propositions (premises, sub-conclusions, conclusions).
- **Support links**: one node supports another (drawn as an arrow from premise to conclusion).
- **Attack links**: an objection attacks a premise or an inference.
- **Co-premises**: premises that support *jointly* rather than independently.

The last distinction is the most important and the most often missed. Consider:

> “The butler was in the house at 10 p.m. And he had a motive. So he probably did it.”

The two premises support the conclusion *independently*: either alone raises the probability. Now consider:

> “All ravens are black. This bird is a raven. So this bird is black.”

The premises support the conclusion *jointly*: neither alone gives any support, and the support exists only in combination. In a map, joint premises are bracketed together; independent premises are separate arrows.

**Why it matters.** If premises are independent, refuting one leaves the argument standing (weakened). If they are joint, refuting one destroys the argument entirely. A great deal of philosophical confusion consists in attacking a joint premise as though it were independent, or defending an independent premise as though it were joint.

```
                 [Butler had opportunity]
                            \
                             v
                    [Butler probably did it]
                             ^
                            /
                    [Butler had motive]
```
*(Independent support)*

```
                 [All ravens are black] ─┐
                                        ├─> [This bird is black]
                 [This bird is a raven] ─┘
```
*(Joint support)*

The technique generalizes to disagreements: a **dialectical map** adds the opponent’s counterarguments, the replies to those, and so on. The result is a tree whose depth is the depth of the debate.

### 4.2 The Toulmin model and its limits

Stephen Toulmin (*The Uses of Argument*, 1958) proposed a six-part analysis of argument: **claim**, **grounds**, **warrant**, **backing**, **qualifier**, **rebuttal**. The model is well known in rhetoric and informal logic, and it is useful for noticing things that the premise–conclusion model obscures — especially the *warrant* (the general principle licensing the inference) and the *qualifier* (how strongly the conclusion is claimed).

But the Toulmin model has two problems for philosophical purposes. First, it is not a formal model: there is no semantics, no definition of validity, no decision procedure. Second, it obscures the fact that warrants are themselves premises, and can be attacked as premises. For most philosophical work, the premise–conclusion model with explicit hidden premises is clearer. Toulmin’s contribution is best treated as a **checklist** — have you stated the warrant? have you qualified the conclusion? have you considered rebuttals? — rather than as an alternative logic.

### 4.3 Steel-manning

The **straw man** is the fallacy of refuting a weaker version of an opponent’s position than the one she holds. Its epidemiology is a chapter in the psychology of persuasion (Ch. 4, §2), not of logic; but its philosophical form is important because it can be committed in good faith.

**Steel-manning** is the deliberate practice of constructing the strongest version of the opponent’s position before criticizing it. It has a structure:

1. **Reconstruct** the position in its most defensible form, using the charitable method of §3.
2. **Strengthen** it: add premises or distinctions that the opponent did not state but would accept; remove irrelevant or unfortunate formulations; repair obvious slips.
3. **State it back** to the opponent, or to the reader, as the target of criticism.
4. **Criticize the strengthened version**, and only then return to the actual text.

The fourth step is essential. A steel man is not a substitute for reading the text; it is a *testing procedure*. The philosophical value of steel-manning is that it converts a disagreement into a genuine test: if the strongest version survives your objection, your objection was not good enough.

**The limits of steel-manning.** Steel-manning can become a form of avoidance, if the strengthened position is so far from the actual one that criticizing it is irrelevant. The rule of thumb: *the steel man should be a position the opponent would recognize as hers.* If she would say “that’s not what I meant,” you have gone too far.

### 4.4 The motte-and-bailey (preview)

A pattern to watch for, discussed fully in Ch. 4, §2: the arguer defends a **bailey** (a strong, interesting, controversial claim) in good times, and retreats to a **motte** (a weak, obvious, easily defended claim) when attacked — then returns to the bailey once the attack is over. Example: “Consciousness is a fundamental feature of the universe” (bailey) / “We don’t know how consciousness arises” (motte). The pattern is not always dishonest — sometimes the bailey is genuinely uncertain and the motte is all that can be supported — but the transition must be argued, not assumed.

## 5. Explanation versus Argument

Much philosophical writing confuses two different activities. The confusion is worth a section of its own, because the diagnostic payoff is large.

### 5.1 The distinction

An **argument** aims to establish the truth of a proposition. An **explanation** aims to make a phenomenon intelligible — to say *why* it is the case, or *how* it came to be, or *what* it consists in.

| | Argument | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Target | A proposition (the conclusion) | A phenomenon (the explanandum) |
| Success condition | Truth-preservation / support | Intelligibility / understanding |
| Canonical question | “Is it true?” | “Why is it so?” |
| Structure | Premises → conclusion | Explanans → explanandum |
| Failure mode | Invalidity, false premise | Circularity, redescription, vacuity |
| Assessed by | Logic + epistemology | Explanatory virtues (§5.3) |

The distinction is not absolute. An explanation can be offered *as* an argument (the fact that theory $T$ explains the data is a premise for the truth of $T$), and an argument can have explanatory force. But the criteria of assessment are different, and confusing them produces two characteristic errors.

**Error 1: offering an explanation where an argument is required.** “Why do you believe in God? — Because belief in God explains the religious experience.” This may or may not be a good explanation; but as an argument for the *existence* of God, it needs the further premise that the explanation is *better* than the alternatives (Ch. 50, §2), and that premise is doing all the work.

**Error 2: demanding an argument where an explanation is what is on offer.** Moral philosophy often offers *explanations* of why we have the moral beliefs we do (Gibbard, Haidt, Joyce). These are not arguments against morality; they are natural histories. Whether they are *debunking* explanations depends on further normative premises (Ch. 44, §6; Ch. 3, §5).

### 5.2 Explanans and explanandum

In the standard terminology:

- The **explanandum** is the thing to be explained — a fact, event, regularity, or phenomenon.
- The **explanans** is the set of propositions or factors that do the explaining.

Two structural points about the relation:

**(i) The asymmetry of explanation.** Explanations are asymmetric: the height of the mercury explains the reading on the thermometer, not vice versa; the past explains the present, not vice versa (though final causes make trouble for this, Ch. 42, §2). Arguments are not asymmetric in the same way: if $p$ entails $q$, and $q$ entails $p$, both entailments are valid, whereas only one direction of explanation is correct. This is one reason explanation is a genuinely different notion from inference.

**(ii) The contrast-class problem.** Why-questions are always implicitly contrastive: *why this rather than that?* “Why did the bridge collapse?” can mean: why did *this* bridge collapse (rather than standing), why did it collapse *now* (rather than later), why did it collapse *from metal fatigue* (rather than from an earthquake)? Each contrast generates a different explanandum and a different explanation (van Fraassen 1980; Ch. 38, §5). Most bad explanations in philosophy answer a different contrast question from the one that was asked.

### 5.3 Inference to the best explanation

**Abduction**, or **inference to the best explanation** (IBE), is the inference from the fact that a hypothesis would explain some body of evidence to the conclusion that the hypothesis is true (or probably true, or worthy of belief). Formally:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & H_1 \text{ explains the evidence } E \text{ better than any available alternative.} \\
2. & \text{If a hypothesis explains the evidence better than any available alternative, it is probably true.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore H_1 \text{ is probably true.}
\end{array}
$$

The scheme is notoriously contestable. The second premise is the **principle of IBE**, and it is not a logical truth; it is a substantive methodological claim. Much of the philosophy of science in the last fifty years has been an attempt to say what “better explanation” means (Lipton 2004; Harman 1965; Psillos 2002).

The standard **explanatory virtues** are the following:

| Virtue | What it means | Warning |
|---|---|---|
| **Explanatory scope** | Explains more of the data | Can be gamed by vacuous generality |
| **Simplicity** | Fewer entities, posits, or parameters | “Simple” is theory-relative |
| **Conservatism** | Fits with what we already believe | Conservatism can entrench error |
| **Fertility** | Suggests new predictions or lines of inquiry | Speculative fertility is cheap |
| **Precision** | Makes definite, testable claims | Vague claims are unfalsifiable |
| **Mechanism** | Says *how* the explanation works | “Mechanism” is contested in the special sciences (Ch. 38, §4) |
| **Unification** | Subsumes many phenomena under one principle | Unification without truth is a fiction |

The virtues can conflict. A simple hypothesis may be less explanatory; a fertile one may be less conservative. The choice of weights is a substantive methodological decision, and there is no algorithm for it — which is precisely why IBE is both indispensable and vulnerable.

**The circularity worry.** IBE is used to justify scientific realism (the no-miracles argument); but the no-miracles argument is an application of IBE; and if IBE is not truth-conducive, the argument fails. The worry is that the justification of IBE cannot itself be an IBE without circularity (Ch. 40, §5). The best current reply is that IBE is justified pragmatically or by its track record, not by a further IBE (Psillos; Douven); the debate is live.

**The van Fraassen challenge.** Bas van Fraassen (1980) argues that the explanatory virtues are *pragmatic*, not *epistemic*: they make a theory useful or attractive, not more likely to be true. If he is right, IBE is not an inference to truth at all, but a heuristic for theory choice. This is one of the deepest disagreements in the philosophy of science, and we shall return to it in Ch. 38 and Ch. 40.

**The methodological point for the rest of this book.** Many of the most important arguments in philosophy are IBE arguments in disguise: the argument from consciousness, the argument from evil, the argument from religious experience, the no-miracles argument, the argument from the success of mathematics. Each can be reconstructed as an IBE, and each is vulnerable to the same questions: *Is the explanatory comparison fair? Are all the alternatives on the table? Are the virtues being applied consistently?* The skill of reconstructing an IBE — and of attacking one — is one of the most valuable in the subject.

### 5.4 Possibility arguments

A special and under-discussed form of argument deserves mention here, because it is everywhere in philosophy.

A **possibility argument** aims to establish a conclusion about what is *possible* or *conceivable*, not about what is actual. Its form is:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{It is possible that } p \text{ (or: } p \text{ is conceivable, or not ruled out).} \\
2. & \text{If } p \text{ is possible, then } q \text{ is false (or: } q \text{ is not necessary).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore q \text{ is false (or: } q \text{ is not necessary).}
\end{array}
$$

The **zombie argument** (Chalmers 1996; Ch. 24, §3) is the canonical example: it is conceivable that there be a being physically identical to me with no conscious experience; what is conceivable is possible; therefore physicalism is not a necessary truth. The argument’s force depends entirely on the second premise of the inner inference — the **conceivability–possibility thesis** — and on the controversial claim that we can establish conceivability (Ch. 9, §5).

The general lesson: **possibility arguments are not arguments for the actuality of anything.** They are arguments against necessity, or against the impossibility of alternatives. Conflating the two is a common error, and a common source of illegitimate probative weight.

## 6. Workshop: Three Classic Arguments

We now apply the machinery. Three arguments, chosen because they illustrate three different kinds of reconstruction — a deductive argument with a contested premise, an inference to the best explanation in deductive dress, and an argument about the logic of a transition.

### 6.1 The ontological argument (Anselm, *Proslogion* II)

**The text** (paraphrased): God is that than which nothing greater can be conceived. But that than which nothing greater can be conceived cannot exist in the understanding alone; for if it existed only in the understanding, it could be conceived to exist in reality as well, and that would be greater. Therefore that than which nothing greater can be conceived exists in reality.

**Standard reconstruction** (following Plantinga’s version of *Proslogion* II; *The Nature of Necessity*, 1974, ch. 10):

Let $G$ = “a being than which no greater can be conceived.” Let “exists in the understanding” mean “is conceived,” and “exists in reality” mean “actually exists.”

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & G \text{ is understood (exists in the understanding).} \\
2. & \text{If } G \text{ existed only in the understanding, we could conceive of a being greater than } G \text{ (namely, one that also exists in reality).} \\
3. & \text{But by definition, no being greater than } G \text{ can be conceived.} \\
4. & \text{Therefore } G \text{ cannot exist only in the understanding.} \\
5. & G \text{ exists in the understanding (1).} \\
6. & \text{Therefore } G \text{ exists in reality.}
\end{array}
$$

**Assessment.** The argument is valid — steps 2–4 are a *reductio* of the supposition that $G$ exists only in the understanding, and 1 and 6 are an application of excluded middle to the two modes of existence. The contestable premises are (2) and the definition of $G$.

- **Gaunilo’s objection** (the “perfect island”): the same form of argument would prove the existence of a perfect island. Reply: the argument works only for a being whose *greatness* includes existence essentially — i.e., only for a being with all perfections, including necessary existence. Whether the notion of a “greatest conceivable being” is coherent is the crux (see below).
- **Kant’s objection**: existence is not a predicate, so “exists in reality” does not add a *property* to a concept; hence a being that exists is not “greater” than one that does not. Reply: Kant’s claim is about *first-order* existence; the argument can be reformulated using *necessary* existence, which arguably is a property (Ch. 16, §1).
- **The coherence objection**: the concept of a being than which no greater can be conceived may be incoherent, like the concept of the largest integer. If it is incoherent, the argument’s premise (3) is unavailable (Ch. 50, §3).

**The modal version** (Plantinga 1974) is more interesting and more instructive:

Let $\Box$ mean “it is necessary that” and $\Diamond$ “it is possible that.” Let $Gx$ mean “$x$ is a maximally great being” (roughly: omnipotent, omniscient, morally perfect in every possible world).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \Diamond \exists x\, Gx \quad \text{(it is possible that a maximally great being exists).} \\
2. & \Box(\exists x\, Gx \to \Box \exists x\, Gx) \quad \text{(if a maximally great being exists, it exists necessarily — by maximal greatness).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \Box \exists x\, Gx \quad \text{(it is necessary that a maximally great being exists).}
\end{array}
$$

The inference from 1 and 2 to 3 is valid in $S5$ modal logic (Ch. 35, §3). The contestable premise is (1). The famous **symmetry objection** (Oppy, Sobel): the same form proves the necessity of *atheism* if it is possible that no maximally great being exists:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1'. & \Diamond \neg \exists x\, Gx. \\
2'. & \Box(\neg\exists x\, Gx \to \Box \neg \exists x\, Gx). \\
\hline
3'. & \therefore \Box \neg \exists x\, Gx.
\end{array}
$$

Since (1) and (1′) cannot both be true, the argument establishes at most that the question turns on which possibility premise is correct — and that is a substantive question, not a logical one. Plantinga’s own view is that (1) is *warranted* for the theist, but not for the atheist; the argument is therefore a *defense* of theism against the charge of inconsistency, not a proof. This is an honest and instructive result: a valid argument whose probative force is exhausted by the epistemic status of one premise.

**The lesson.** The ontological argument is the paradigm case of an argument whose validity is uncontested and whose premises are the whole game. Learning to see this — to stop arguing about the logic and start arguing about the premise — is a milestone in philosophical maturity.

### 6.2 The argument from illusion

**The text** (Russell, *Problems*, Ch. I; Ayer, *Foundations of Empirical Knowledge*, 1940). A straight stick half-submerged in water looks bent. A coin looks elliptical from an angle. A distant tower looks small. In each case, the object *appears* to have a property it does not have. But appearing is a relation to something; what we are directly aware of, in perception, must be the appearance — the **sense-datum**. Therefore we are never directly aware of material objects, only of sense-data.

**Standard reconstruction** (Ayer’s version):

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{In illusion, the subject is directly aware of something that has the illusory property (a bent-looking thing, an elliptical-looking thing).} \\
2. & \text{The material object does not have that property.} \\
3. & \text{Hence what the subject is directly aware of is not the material object.} \\
4. & \text{But the experience in illusion is of the same kind as the experience in veridical perception.} \\
5. & \text{Hence, by the same reasoning, in veridical perception the subject is directly aware of something other than the material object.} \\
6. & \text{That something is a sense-datum.} \\
\hline
7. & \therefore \text{We are never directly aware of material objects.}
\end{array}
$$

**Assessment.** The argument is valid if premise 1 is interpreted as a claim about *direct awareness* rather than about *the object of experience*. The whole weight of the argument falls on that interpretation, which is the **phenomenal principle**: *whenever something looks $F$ to a subject, there is something of which the subject is aware that is $F$.* (Robinson 1994.)

The phenomenal principle is not a logical truth, and it has been extensively criticized:

- **Adverbialism** (Chisholm, Ducasse): “I sense redly” is not a relation to a red object; it is a mode of sensing. On this view, premise 1 is false. (Ch. 5, §6.)
- **Intentionalism / representationalism** (Ch. 5, §4): experience represents the world as being a certain way, but representation does not require an object that *is* that way. A photograph can represent a unicorn without there being a unicorn. Premise 1 is false as stated; the correct premise (that experience *represents* a bent thing) does not yield the sense-datum conclusion.
- **Disjunctivism** (Hinton, McDowell, Martin): veridical perception and illusion are not experiences of the same fundamental kind. Premise 4 is false. The argument’s symmetry is an illusion. (Ch. 5, §6.)

**The meta-point: what kind of argument is this?** On the surface, the argument is deductive. But its real force is **abductive**: the sense-datum theory is offered as the *best explanation* of the phenomena of illusion and hallucination. Once we see this, the assessment changes. We should ask: *is the sense-datum theory the best explanation?* Does it explain more than representationalism, with fewer costs? The sense-datum theory has well-known costs: it posits a private realm of objects; it faces a regress of appearing (what explains the *appearance* of the sense-datum?); it makes perception systematically misleading. Representationalism is simpler and has better prospects. The argument from illusion, properly reconstructed as an IBE, is probably not a good argument — which is why the philosophical consensus has moved away from sense-data, though not decisively (Ch. 5, §3).

**The lesson.** The surface form of an argument can mislead. Many deductive arguments in philosophy are compressed IBEs, and the right way to attack them is to compare explanations, not to hunt for formal fallacies.

### 6.3 The is–ought gap

**The text** (Hume, *Treatise* III.i.1, the famous “is–ought” paragraph): “In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remark’d, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning, and establishes the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when of a sudden I am surpriz’d to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, *is*, and *is not*, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an *ought*, or an *ought not*. This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence. For as this *ought*, or *ought not*, expresses some new relation or affirmation, ’tis necessary that it shou’d be observ’d and explain’d; and at the same time that a reason should be given, for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it.”

**Standard reconstruction** (the “no-ought-from-is” thesis):

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{No set of purely descriptive premises entails a normative conclusion.} \\
2. & \text{If a normative conclusion is to be established by argument, its premises must include at least one normative premise.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{No purely descriptive argument can establish a normative conclusion.}
\end{array}
$$

**Assessment.** The argument is valid *if* (1) is true. Is it? The question is surprisingly delicate.

- **Hume’s own claim** is narrower than the slogan suggests: he says the transition from *is* to *ought* is “inconceivable” as a *deduction*, and that the new relation must be explained. He does not say that descriptive facts are irrelevant to normative conclusions; indeed his whole moral psychology is an account of how the descriptive facts about human nature generate moral sentiment. The “gap” is logical, not psychological.
- **Searle’s counterexample** (1964): from “Jones uttered the words ‘I hereby promise to pay you $5’” (a descriptive fact), together with the constitutive rules of promising, it seems to follow that “Jones ought to pay you $5” (a normative conclusion). Searle concluded that the gap is bridged by institutional facts.
- **The standard reply**: the constitutive rules of promising are themselves normative premises. The inference is valid only if we add a premise like “one ought to keep one’s promises,” which is normative. So Searle’s argument does not refute the gap; it illustrates it. The descriptive fact that a promise was uttered generates an obligation only given a normative rule. (A similar reply applies to attempts to derive ought from function, from convention, or from the nature of action.)
- **The recent literature** (Pigden, Prior, and the “automatic” transition debate) has explored whether there are *analytic* entailments from is to ought — e.g., whether “this is a knife” entails “this is good for cutting,” and whether that is normative. The consensus is that the interesting cases turn on whether the descriptive predicate is *thick* (Ch. 44, §7) — that is, whether it already contains an evaluative component. If it does, the gap is not crossed; it is presupposed.
- **The companions-in-guilt argument** (Ch. 44, §5): if the is–ought gap refutes moral realism, it equally refutes epistemic and mathematical realism, since “is justified” cannot be derived from “is believed” either. The argument is therefore either too strong or irrelevant.

**The lesson.** The is–ought gap is not a single argument but a family of theses, and the differences between them matter:

| Thesis | Content | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Logical** | No descriptive premises entail a normative conclusion | Widely accepted (with thick-concept caveats) |
| **Epistemic** | Descriptive evidence cannot justify a normative belief | Much stronger; probably false (moral perception, Ch. 44, §6) |
| **Motivational** | Descriptive beliefs cannot motivate action | Humean; contested (Ch. 28, §2) |
| **Semantic** | Normative predicates do not mean descriptive ones | Widely accepted; does not settle the metaphysics |
| **Metaphysical** | There are no normative facts | Does **not** follow from any of the above |

This last row is the most important. The is–ought gap is often cited as though it refuted moral realism. It does not. It constrains the *form* of a realist theory — a realist must hold that normative facts are not identical to descriptive facts, and must explain the relation — but it leaves the metaphysics open. Confusing a logical thesis with a metaphysical one is one of the commonest errors in metaethics, and the discipline of separating them is a paradigm of the method this chapter teaches.

## Summary

- Philosophy operates on **propositions**. Before arguing, establish that you have a genuine question: check truth-aptness, presuppositions, category applicability, verbality, and shape. Pseudo-questions are not answered; they are diagnosed.
- An **argument** is premises offered in support of a conclusion. **Validity** is truth-preservation; **soundness** is validity plus true premises; cogency adds justification. Validity is cheap; the philosophical work is in the premises.
- **Deductive** inference is truth-preserving; **ampliative** inference (induction, abduction) is truth-extending and defeasible. Most interesting philosophical arguments are ampliative, and should be assessed as such.
- Most real arguments are **enthymematic**. Reconstruction is underdetermined by the text; state your reconstruction explicitly, and choose the strongest version the text will bear. **Charity** is a virtue with limits: fidelity, the arguer’s own standards, the possibility of error.
- **Argument maps** distinguish independent from joint premises; **dialectical trees** represent the depth of a debate; **steel-manning** converts disagreement into a genuine test. A steel man must be a position the opponent would recognize.
- **Explanation** is not **argument**. Explanation aims at intelligibility; argument aims at truth. The two have different success conditions and different failure modes, and confusing them is a common source of philosophical error.
- **Inference to the best explanation** is the most important ampliative form in philosophy. Its explanatory virtues are real but contestable, and its use raises the circularity worry (is IBE itself justified abductively?) and the van Fraassen challenge (are explanatory virtues epistemic or pragmatic?).
- **Possibility arguments** establish possibility, not actuality. Their weight depends on the conceivability–possibility thesis, which is itself contested.
- The **workshop** showed three reconstructions: the ontological argument (valid; premise is the whole game), the argument from illusion (deductive in form, abductive in substance), and the is–ought gap (a family of theses that must be kept distinct).

## Study Questions

1. Is “Why is there something rather than nothing?” a genuine question by the test of §1.3? If not, is that a criticism of the question or of the test?
2. Give an argument that is valid but not sound, and one that is sound but not cogent. What does the difference show about the relation between logic and epistemology?
3. Can a valid argument have a false conclusion? If so, what follows for the claim that validity is the core of good reasoning?
4. Reconstruct the following argument charitably, then criticize it: “You can’t criticize someone’s culture. Morality is relative to culture. So we should tolerate all cultural practices.” Identify at least two hidden premises and two distinct errors.
5. Under what conditions is it legitimate to attribute a hidden premise to an arguer rather than declaring the argument invalid? Give a case where charity goes too far.
6. Consider the claim that the argument from illusion is really an IBE. Does this reconstruction make the argument stronger or weaker? Why?
7. Is the is–ought gap a logical thesis, a metaphysical thesis, or both? What difference does it make to the debate about moral realism?
8. Steel-man a position you reject. Then state the strongest objection you can to your steel man. Did the objection survive?
9. Explain the difference between the *symmetry objection* to Plantinga’s modal argument and the *parody objection* (Gaunilo) to Anselm’s. Do they attack the same premise?
10. “Philosophy is just the art of constructing valid arguments from premises no one has established.” Assess.
