# Chapter 4 — How to Read, Write, and Evaluate Philosophical Work

> “The aim of philosophy, abstractly formulated, is to understand how things in the broadest possible sense of the term hang together in the broadest possible sense of the term.”
> — Wilfrid Sellars, “Philosophy and the Scientific Image of Man” (1962)

> “Do not block the way of inquiry.”
> — C. S. Peirce, *Collected Papers* 1.135

The first three chapters gave us a conception of the subject, an anatomy of the argument, and a repertoire of instruments. This chapter turns to the craft — the practices by which philosophical work is actually done, read, and judged. It is the most practical chapter in the book, and in some ways the most important, because the difference between good and bad philosophy is less often a difference in raw intelligence than a difference in **discipline**: the discipline of stating a question precisely, of reconstructing an argument fairly, of calibrating confidence to evidence, and of seeking out the objection that hurts.

We proceed in five parts: the anatomy of a paper, the taxonomy of fallacies, the criteria of a good theory, the ethics of intellectual discourse, and the practice of self-examination.

## 1. The Anatomy of a Philosophical Paper

### 1.1 The five parts

Almost every good philosophical paper — article, chapter, dissertation section, seminar essay — has the same skeleton. The proportions vary; the presence of the parts does not.

| Part | Function | Typical length | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Question** | Isolates the issue | 1–2 paragraphs | What exactly are we asking? |
| **Thesis** | States the answer | 1 paragraph | What do you claim? |
| **Argument** | Supports the thesis | 60–70% of the paper | Why should we believe you? |
| **Objection** | States the strongest challenge | 10–20% | What is the best case against you? |
| **Reply** | Answers the objection | 5–10% | Why does the challenge fail? |

The proportions are diagnostic. A paper that is 95% argument and 5% objection is either a paper about a question no one disputes — rare in philosophy — or a paper that has not looked for trouble. A paper that is 50% objection is often a paper that has not decided what it thinks.

The **conclusion** is not a sixth part; it is a *summary* of the five, plus an honest statement of what has not been established and what follows.

### 1.2 From topic to question to thesis

The commonest failure in student (and not only student) work is to write about a **topic** rather than answer a **question**. “Consciousness” is a topic. “Is phenomenal consciousness entailed by the complete physical truth?” is a question. “Free will” is a topic; “Is the ability to do otherwise required for moral responsibility?” is a question.

The progression is a funnel:

```
TOPIC
  └─> QUESTION (specific, answerable, contestable)
        └─> THESIS (a proposition that could be false)
              └─> ARGUMENT (why it is true)
                    └─> OBJECTION (why it might not be)
                          └─> REPLY (why the objection fails)
```

Three tests for a good question:

1. **Specificity.** Can a single paper answer it? “What is justice?” cannot; “Does Rawls’s difference principle permit inequalities in health care?” can.
2. **Answerability.** Is there a shape that an answer would take? (Ch. 2, §1.3.) A question with no specifiable shape is a candidate for dissolution, not for a paper.
3. **Contestability.** Is the answer genuinely in doubt? A question whose answer is already settled by definition (“Is a bachelor unmarried?”) is not a philosophical question, however it is dressed.

The **thesis** must satisfy a parallel set of conditions:

- It must be a **proposition** — something that can be true or false.
- It must be **specific** enough that the argument can bear on it. “Consciousness is mysterious” is not a thesis; “Phenomenal properties are not identical to any functional properties” is.
- It must be **contestable** — someone informed and intelligent could deny it. If no one could deny it, you have not said anything.
- It must be **matched** to the argument — no stronger than the evidence supports, and no weaker than the evidence allows. (This is the *matching principle*, and it will return in §4.1.)

A useful exercise: state your thesis in one sentence, then ask, of that sentence, *what would someone who denies it say?* If you cannot answer, your thesis is probably too vague to be denied, and therefore too vague to be defended.

### 1.3 The argument section

The argument section is the body of reasoning, not a single deduction. In practice it does four things, usually in this order:

1. **Framing.** It sets out the alternatives and says which one the paper will defend. (“There are three views: identity theory, functionalism, and dualism. This paper defends a version of functionalism against the other two.”)
2. **Distinctions.** It introduces the distinctions that the argument needs. (“We must distinguish *access* consciousness from *phenomenal* consciousness; the argument concerns only the latter.”) Distinctions are the philosopher’s scalpel: most bad arguments fail because a distinction that should have been drawn was not.
3. **The core argument.** It presents the premises and the inference, with the support for each premise. This is where the standard form of Ch. 2, §2.1 earns its keep, even if the paper does not present its argument in that form.
4. **The cases.** It brings in examples, thought experiments, and empirical findings, each doing specific work. (On the discipline of cases, Ch. 3, §2.)

A good argument section has **signposts**: “I will argue first that …, then that …, and finally that …”; “This premise may seem obviously true, but it is not; here is why”; “The argument so far has established only X, not Y.” Philosophy is hard enough without making the reader guess where she is.

### 1.4 Objection and reply

The objection–reply pair is what distinguishes a philosophical paper from an opinion piece. The rule is simple and rarely followed: **state the strongest objection, not the easiest.**

A taxonomy of replies is useful, because each has a characteristic cost:

| Reply | Form | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Deny a premise** | “The objection assumes P; P is false, for reasons R.” | Requires an independent argument for $\neg P$ |
| **Distinguish** | “The objection works against the strong version; my version is weaker in respect W, which is what matters.” | Can look like retreat (cf. motte-and-bailey, §2.3) |
| **Reject the inference** | “Grant the premises; the conclusion does not follow, because …” | Requires showing the gap |
| **Bite the bullet** | “The objection’s consequence is indeed a consequence, and it is acceptable, because …” | The most honest and the most expensive |
| **Reframe** | “The objection presupposes a framework the paper rejects.” | Risks begging the question against the objector |
| **Cost-accounting** | “The objection costs my view X, but the alternatives cost more.” | Requires a comparative case |
| **Concede** | “The objection is decisive; here is the modified thesis.” | Requires rewriting the paper |

The **cost-accounting** reply is the most philosophically sophisticated, and the most often abused. It is legitimate when the costs are *real*, *comparative*, and *paid by every alternative*. It is illegitimate when it is used to wave away an objection that the view incurs and the alternatives do not.

The discipline of the objection section is the discipline of **writing your opponent’s brief**. If a reader who holds the opposing view would not recognise her own position in your statement of it, you have not done the work. (This is the steel-manning requirement of Ch. 2, §4.3, applied to writing.)

### 1.5 Reading: the three passes

Reading philosophy is a skill distinct from reading literature or journalism. The text is an argument, and the argument has to be reconstructed before it can be assessed. Three passes, in order, with decreasing speed and increasing depth:

**Pass 1 — Survey (10–15 minutes).** Read the title, abstract, introduction, section headings, and conclusion. Do not read the body. At the end, answer three questions in writing:

- What is the question?
- What is the thesis?
- What kind of paper is this — constructive (defending a view), critical (refuting one), expository (explaining one), or synthetic (reconciling two)?

If you cannot answer the three questions, the paper is badly written, or you have not read the introduction carefully enough. Either way, do not proceed until you can.

**Pass 2 — Reconstruction (30–60 minutes).** Now read the body. Your job is not to agree or disagree; it is to reconstruct. Produce:

- A **standardised argument** (Ch. 2, §2.1) for the main thesis.
- An **argument map** (Ch. 2, §4.1) distinguishing joint from independent premises.
- A list of the **key distinctions** the paper introduces or relies on.
- A list of the **load-bearing moves** — the premises or inferences that, if false, would collapse the paper.
- A list of the **unstated premises** the argument needs (Ch. 2, §3.1).

The test of pass 2 is the **one-sentence summary**: can you state the paper’s thesis and its main argument in one sentence each? If not, you have not yet understood it.

**Pass 3 — Evaluation (30–60 minutes).** Now, and only now, assess. For each load-bearing move, ask:

- Is the premise true? What is the evidence?
- Is the inference valid, or at least strong?
- What would the strongest opponent say? Has the author addressed it?
- Is there an alternative explanation of the same data?
- What is the *cost* of the thesis, and is it acknowledged?

**Annotation protocol.** A consistent set of margin marks saves enormous time on rereading:

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `T` | Thesis |
| `P1, P2, …` | Premises |
| `O` | Objection |
| `R` | Reply |
| `D` | Key distinction |
| `Q` | Passage worth quoting |
| `?` | Do not understand; return later |
| `!` | Disagree; note why |
| `→` | Cross-reference to another text |

**Reading against the grain.** After pass 2, before pass 3, write a paragraph in the voice of the author’s strongest opponent. If the paper has anticipated it, the paragraph is a test of the reply. If it has not, the paragraph is the paper’s gap. This is the single most efficient reading exercise I know.

**Reading the classics.** The three-pass method works on the *Republic* and the *Critique of Pure Reason*, but it needs two additional disciplines. First, **historical charity**: the author did not have our distinctions, our science, or our politics; reconstruct the argument in *her* terms before assessing it in ours. Second, **anachronism-check**: distinguish what the author actually argued from what the tradition says she argued. The secondary literature is a guide, not an authority.

### 1.6 Writing: the craft

Writing is not the transcription of thought; it is the *instrument* of thought. This is not a metaphor. Most philosophical problems become tractable only when you try to write them down, because writing forces you to specify what you mean. The philosopher who says “I know what I think, I just can’t put it into words” is usually a philosopher who does not know what she thinks.

Six principles, in descending order of importance:

**(i) One idea per paragraph.** The paragraph is the unit of argument. Its first sentence — the **topic sentence** — states the idea; the remaining sentences elaborate, support, or qualify it. If a paragraph has two ideas, it is two paragraphs. If it has none, it is filler.

**(ii) Given-to-new.** Within a paragraph, begin with what the reader already knows (the given) and move to what is new. This is the “given–new contract” (Halliday; Clark & Haviland), and violating it is the commonest cause of unreadable prose. “This distinction, which we owe to Frege, is …” is easier to read than “A distinction we owe to Frege, between sense and reference, which is …”

**(iii) Signpost.** Tell the reader where the argument is going, and where it has been. “I have argued that X; I now turn to Y” is not padding; it is navigation. The reader who is lost will not be persuaded.

**(iv) Define terms, and then use them consistently.** A term introduced with a stipulative definition and then used in its ordinary sense is an equivocation waiting to happen (§2.3). If you introduce “physical” as “described by fundamental physics,” do not slide into “physical” as “material” three pages later.

**(v) Quote sparingly and precisely.** A quotation is evidence; it should be introduced, interpreted, and cited. A block quote with no commentary is a way of avoiding the argument. Paraphrase where you can; quote only when the exact wording matters.

**(vi) Cut ten percent.** Every draft is too long. The first ten percent you cut is usually the throat-clearing; the second ten percent is usually the repetition; the third ten percent is usually the sentence you were proudest of. Cut all three. (The rule of thumb: if you cannot bear to cut a sentence, it is probably doing rhetorical rather than philosophical work.)

**The introduction.** A good introduction answers, in order: What is the question? Why does it matter? What have others said? What is wrong or missing in what they have said? What does this paper argue? How is it structured? This is not a formula to be followed mechanically; it is a checklist to be satisfied, in whatever order serves the argument.

**The abstract.** 100–200 words. Question, thesis, method, contribution. No throat-clearing, no “this paper will attempt to explore,” no promises the paper does not keep.

**The conclusion.** What has been established? What has not? What follows? What remains open? The conclusion is not a summary of the introduction; it is the *accounting* — the ledger of what the reader now has, and what she does not.

### 1.7 Workshop: a paragraph and its rewrite

Here is a paragraph of the kind one sees in first drafts. It is a composite, not a quotation from any real author.

> It is often said that consciousness is a mystery. Many philosophers have argued that the hard problem is insoluble, while others disagree. In this paper I will argue that consciousness is not something that can be reduced to physical processes. This is because, as Nagel argues, there is something it is like to be a bat. Therefore, physicalism is false.

**Diagnosis.** Six problems, in order of seriousness:

1. **The thesis is vague.** “Not something that can be reduced to physical processes” could mean (a) not *identical* to physical processes, (b) not *caused* by them, (c) not *explained* by them, or (d) not *exhausted* by them. The argument supports at most (c) or (d), and the paper needs to say which.
2. **The inference is too strong.** Nagel’s bat argument is about the limits of *objective description*; it does not by itself establish that physicalism is false. The “therefore” leaps a gap that the paper has not bridged.
3. **No engagement with the opposition.** The physicalist has replies (phenomenal concepts, the distinction between explanation and identity, illusionism), and they are not mentioned.
4. **The first two sentences are filler.** They inform no one who is reading the paper, and they delay the thesis.
5. **The citation is doing too much work.** “As Nagel argues” is not an argument; it is an appeal to authority, and it is not even clear *which* of Nagel’s claims is being invoked.
6. **No signposting.** The reader has no idea where the paper is going after this paragraph.

**Rewrite.**

> This paper argues that phenomenal consciousness — the “what it is like” character of experience — is not *entailed* by the complete physical truth about the world. The claim is not that consciousness is non-physical, but that the physical facts, however complete, leave open whether there is something it is like to be the subject of those facts. §2 reconstructs Nagel’s (1974) argument for this conclusion, distinguishing the epistemic claim (we cannot derive the phenomenal from the physical) from the metaphysical claim (the phenomenal is not the physical). §3 considers the strongest physicalist reply — that the phenomenal is entailed by the physical but not *a priori* derivable from it (Chalmers 2003; Papineau 2002) — and argues that this reply either concedes the epistemic gap or relies on an ad hoc restriction on what counts as a physical fact. §4 draws out the consequences for the metaphysics of mind.

The rewrite is longer, but every sentence does work: it states the thesis precisely, marks the distinction that the argument needs, signposts the structure, and names the opponent. It does not *assert* the conclusion; it says what the paper will argue.

## 2. A Taxonomy of Fallacies

A **fallacy** is a pattern of reasoning that is *tempting* but *bad*. The “tempting” is essential: there are infinitely many invalid forms, but only some of them are fallacies in the diagnostic sense — the ones that people are actually inclined to commit, and that look persuasive.

The taxonomy has three tiers: **formal** (bad in virtue of logical form), **informal** (bad in virtue of content or context), and **dialectical** (bad in virtue of the structure of the exchange). The third tier is the one most characteristic of philosophy, because philosophy is a dialectical practice, and it is the one least often taught.

### 2.1 Formal fallacies

| Fallacy | Pattern | Why it fails | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Affirming the consequent** | $p \to q;\ q;\ \therefore p$ | $q$ may have other causes | “If the theory is true, it predicts X; it predicts X; so it is true” |
| **Denying the antecedent** | $p \to q;\ \neg p;\ \therefore \neg q$ | $q$ may obtain without $p$ | “If it rained, the ground is wet; it did not rain; so the ground is not wet” |
| **Undistributed middle** | All $A$ are $B$; all $C$ are $B$; $\therefore$ all $A$ are $C$ | The middle term is not distributed | “All dogs are mammals; all cats are mammals; so all dogs are cats” |
| **Illicit process** | A term (major or minor) distributed in the conclusion but not in the premises | The conclusion claims more than the premises support | “All squares are rectangles; all rectangles are parallelograms; so all parallelograms are squares” |
| **Affirming a disjunct** | $p \vee q;\ p;\ \therefore \neg q$ | Inclusive disjunction | “He is in Paris or Rome; he is in Paris; so he is not in Rome” |
| **Scope fallacy** | $\forall x \exists y Rxy \therefore \exists y \forall x Rxy$ | Quantifier order | “Everyone loves someone; so someone is loved by everyone” (Ch. 3, §3.2) |
| **Modal fallacy** | $\Diamond p\ \therefore\ p$ | Possibility does not entail actuality | “It could have been otherwise; so it was otherwise” (Ch. 3, §3.2 on the modal operators; Ch. 2, §5.4 on possibility arguments) |

The formal fallacies are the *easy* ones: they can be detected mechanically once the argument is formalised. The hard cases are the ones where the formalisation is contested — where the surface grammar conceals a different logical form (Ch. 3, §3.3). A student who has learned to formalise is in a position to detect these; a student who has not will be persuaded by them.

### 2.2 Informal fallacies

The informal fallacies are patterns of bad reasoning that depend on content or context. The important point, often lost in textbook lists, is that **almost none of them is always a fallacy.** Each has legitimate uses, and the legitimate uses are as important to know as the abuses.

| Fallacy | Pattern | When legitimate |
|---|---|---|
| **Ad hominem** | Attacking the person rather than the argument | When the person’s credibility is the issue (testimony, Ch. 11, §1) |
| ***Tu quoque*** | “You do it too” | When the charge is hypocrisy *and* hypocrisy is relevant to credibility |
| **Appeal to authority** | “Expert E says $p$, so $p$” | When E is a genuine expert in the relevant domain, and there is consensus (Ch. 11, §1) |
| **Appeal to popularity** | “Most people believe $p$, so $p$” | Almost never; but widespread belief is evidence of *something* (see below) |
| **Appeal to ignorance** | “No one has proved $\neg p$, so $p$” | When the domain is one in which absence of evidence is evidence of absence (closed worlds) |
| **Appeal to emotion** | “$p$ makes me angry, so $p$ is false” | When the emotion is *about* a morally relevant feature (Ch. 30, §2) |
| **False dilemma** | “Either $p$ or $q$” when there is a third option | When the options really are exhaustive |
| **Slippery slope** | “If $p$, then … then disaster” | When the causal chain is empirically supported |
| **Hasty generalisation** | “Two cases, so all” | When the sample is representative (statistical inference, Ch. 10, §7) |
| **Composition** | “Parts have $F$, so the whole has $F$” | When $F$ is preserved under composition |
| **Division** | “Whole has $F$, so parts have $F$” | When $F$ is distributive |
| **Loaded question** | “Have you stopped beating your wife?” | When the presupposition has been established |
| **No true Scotsman** | “No real $X$ would do $Y$” | When “real $X$” has an established definition |
| **Genetic fallacy** | “The belief originated in $Z$, so it is false” | When the origin is truth-tracking-relevant (Ch. 3, §5) |
| **Argument to moderation** | “The truth is in the middle” | When the extremes are equally supported |
| **Cherry-picking** | Selecting confirming evidence | Never, in principle; but all inquiry selects |

Two of these deserve special comment, because they are so often misapplied.

**Appeal to authority.** There is a real fallacy — “Einstein said it, so it is true” — and there is a legitimate form of epistemic dependence: in a domain where you are not an expert, the consensus of experts is the best evidence available (Ch. 11, §1). The fallacy is not citing authorities; it is citing them *as though their say-so were an argument*, or citing them outside their competence, or citing a minority against a consensus without argument.

**Appeal to popularity.** The *argumentum ad populum* is a fallacy when it is offered as a *proof*. But widespread belief is not nothing: it is evidence that the belief has *some* basis, and in a domain where most people have some access to the evidence (the existence of pain, the wrongness of cruelty), it is weak but real evidence. The fallacy is treating weak evidence as strong.

The general lesson is the one stated at the head of the section: **a fallacy is a diagnosis, not a refutation.** Naming a pattern does not establish that the argument fails, because the pattern may be legitimate in context. The diagnosis must be supplemented by an account of *why* the context does not license the move.

### 2.3 Dialectical fallacies

The fallacies in this tier are not (primarily) defects of individual arguments. They are defects of the **structure of the exchange** — of how the arguments are deployed. They are the fallacies most characteristic of philosophy, because philosophy is a conversation, and conversations can be rigged.

#### (a) Straw man

**Pattern.** The arguer refutes a weak version $S'$ of the opponent’s position $S$, and concludes that $S$ is refuted. The unstated inference is:

$$\neg S' \ \therefore\ \neg S$$

which is valid only if $S' = S$. The fallacy is the *substitution* — the silent replacement of the opponent’s view with a weaker one.

**Formal core.** The argument against $S'$ may be perfectly valid. The defect is in the *identification* of $S'$ with $S$. The fallacy is therefore not in the argument but in the *claim that the argument bears on the opponent’s position*.

**Diagnostic test.** State the opponent’s position in a form she would recognise as her own. If the argument does not touch that form, it is a straw man. If it does, it is legitimate — and a great deal of good philosophy consists in showing that a position *reduces to* a form that can be refuted.

**When legitimate.** Simplification is unavoidable in criticism. The test is not whether the version is simplified, but whether the simplification is *faithful* — whether the omitted complexities are irrelevant to the objection. A straw man is a simplification that omits the very feature that would answer the objection.

#### (b) Motte-and-bailey

**Pattern** (Shackel 2005, naming a medieval castle: the *motte* is the defensible tower, the *bailey* the open courtyard). The arguer advances a strong, interesting, controversial claim — the **bailey** — in the absence of challenge. When challenged, she retreats to a weak, obvious, easily defended claim — the **motte** — and defends that. When the challenge passes, she returns to the bailey.

**Formal core.** Let $B$ be the bailey and $M$ the motte, with $M$ strictly weaker than $B$ (often $B \vdash M$, but not conversely). The dialectical structure is:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Assert } B. \\
2. & \text{Challenge } B. \\
3. & \text{Defend } M \text{ (which is easy).} \\
4. & \text{Treat the defence of } M \text{ as if it were a defence of } B.
\end{array}
$$

The fallacy is not in any single step (each step may be valid), but in step 4: the **illicit transfer** of the credit of defending $M$ to the claim $B$. The inference $M \therefore B$ is never asserted (it would be obviously invalid), but is *relied upon* in the structure of the exchange.

**Diagnostic test.** Ask two questions:

1. **Are there one position or two?** If the bailey and the motte are offered as the same claim under different descriptions, the argument equivocates. If they are offered as distinct claims, which one is the paper defending?
2. **Is the relation between them argued?** If $M$ is claimed to be *evidence* for $B$, the evidential link must be made explicit. If it is not, the move from the motte to the bailey is unsupported.

**When legitimate.** All positions have a core and a periphery; all defences distinguish the defensible core from the dispensable periphery. A *legitimate* retreat from bailey to motte is an **admission** — “I cannot defend the strong claim, so I now defend the weaker one” — and it should be marked as such. The fallacy is the *unmarked* retreat, the transfer of credit without the admission.

**The philosophical example** (composite, not a quotation): “Consciousness is a fundamental feature of the universe, on a par with space and time” (bailey) → “We do not currently have a reductive explanation of consciousness” (motte) → “So consciousness is fundamental” (bailey resumed). The middle step is true; the first and third are not supported by it.

#### (c) Equivocation

**Pattern.** The same word or phrase is used in different senses in the same argument, so that the argument appears valid but is not.

The classic example:

> “Plato says the end of a thing is its perfection; I say that death is the end of life; hence, death is the perfection of life.” (A traditional example; the fallacy is in “end” = purpose vs. “end” = termination.)

**Formal core.** An argument is valid only if its terms have the same meaning throughout. Equivocation is the failure of **univocity** — the same expression with different semantic values. Formally, let $T$ be the equivocal term, with senses $T_1$ and $T_2$. The argument is valid only on a reading where $T$ has a single sense; the premises are true only on a reading where it has two. There is no reading on which the argument is both valid and sound.

**Diagnostic test.** Identify the term; state the two (or more) senses; check whether the same sense is required for the truth of each premise. A useful technique is **subscripting**: rewrite the argument with $T_1$ and $T_2$ explicitly, and see whether it still goes through.

**Philosophical examples.** The literature is a catalogue of equivocations on *natural*, *free*, *explain*, *cause*, *information*, *physical*, *representation*, *knowledge*, *justified*, *real*. The “is–ought gap” argument (Ch. 2, §6.3) is largely a debate about whether *good* and *ought* equivocate across the descriptive/normative divide. The *knowledge* debate turns on whether “knows” in the sceptical context means the same thing as “knows” in the ordinary context (Ch. 6, §4; Ch. 7, §6).

**When legitimate.** Polysemy is a feature of natural language, not a defect. The fallacy is not using a word in two senses; it is *relying on the slide* between them. Context-sensitivity (where the sense is fixed by context) is not equivocation, provided the context fixes it.

#### (d) Begging the question

**Pattern** (*petitio principii*). The argument assumes what it purports to prove. The premise is one that only someone who already accepts the conclusion would accept, or the premise’s justification depends on the conclusion.

The classic example:

> “God exists, because the Bible says so; and the Bible is the word of God.”

**Two notions.** It is worth distinguishing:

- **Epistemic circularity**: the warrant for the premise depends on the warrant for the conclusion. Formally, let $W(p)$ be the warrant for $p$; the argument is viciously circular iff $W(c) \subseteq W(p)$ for some premise $p$ used essentially.
- **Dialectical circularity**: the argument is useless *as a means of persuasion* to an interlocutor who doubts the conclusion, because the premise is not available to her. (This is the notion Aristotle has in *Prior Analytics* II.16 and *Topics* VIII.13, and it is the one that matters for the ethics of discourse.)

**The triviality problem.** Every valid argument is “circular” in the trivial sense that the conclusion is *contained in* the premises. The vice is *vicious* circularity — the conclusion doing the justificatory work. The diagnostic test is therefore not “is the conclusion contained in the premises?” but:

> **Would someone who doubted the conclusion have any reason to accept the premise?**

If the answer is no, the argument is question-begging *for her*. If the answer is yes, the argument may be question-begging *for you* — if your only reason for accepting the premise is the conclusion.

**The meta-question.** Is question-begging always a fallacy? There is a respectable tradition (Chisholm, and the “particularist” tradition in epistemology, Ch. 13, §2) that holds that some circularity is unavoidable: you cannot justify all your beliefs without eventually using some of them. The question is which circularities are *virtuous* — the ones that display the structure of a position — and which are *vicious*. That question is itself philosophical.

**When legitimate.** In *exposition*, circularity is fine: you may set out a position by showing how its parts support each other. In *persuasion*, it is not. The same argument can be a legitimate exposition and a vicious attempt at persuasion. The difference is the audience and the purpose.

#### (e) Deepity

**Pattern** (Dennett 2009; developed in *Intuition Pumps*, 2013). A statement that has two readings: on one, it is trivially true; on the other, it is profound-sounding but false, ill-formed, or unsupported. The appearance of profundity comes from the conflation: the truth of the trivial reading is borrowed to lend credibility to the profound one.

**Formal core.** Let $S$ be the sentence, with readings $R_1$ and $R_2$. On $R_1$, $S$ is true but banal. On $R_2$, $S$ would be interesting, but it is not supported (or not even well-formed). The hearer slides from “$S$ is true” (on $R_1$) to “$S$ is profound” (on $R_2$). The fallacy is the **illicit transfer of truth across readings**.

**Examples.**

- “Love is just a word.” On $R_1$: true — the English word “love” is a word. On $R_2$: false — love is not just a word, and no one who has been in love believes otherwise. The profundity is entirely the product of the slide.
- “The self is an illusion.” On $R_1$: true — the self is not a simple, unchanging substance (Hume, Parfit). On $R_2$: false — there is a person, with a continuous psychological life, who is reading this sentence. The Buddhist *anattā* doctrine is not a deepity, because it does the work of specifying *which* self is denied and *what* replaces it (Ch. 26, §7.3). The bumper-sticker version is a deepity.
- “Everything is connected.” On $R_1$: true — everything is causally related to something. On $R_2$: a substantive metaphysical claim about the nature of reality, unsupported by $R_1$.

**Diagnostic test.** Ask: *what does this sentence mean on each reading? Is the truth of the first reading any evidence for the second?* If the answer is no, you have a deepity. A useful heuristic: **a deepity is a sentence that sounds better than it argues.**

**When legitimate.** Some genuinely deep claims *look* like deepities because they trade on a distinction that has not yet been made explicit. The Buddhist *anattā*, Spinoza’s *deus sive natura*, and the idealist’s “the world is my idea” are all candidates — and the difference between a deepity and a profound truth is whether the work is done. A profound truth earns its paradox; a deepity borrows it.

#### A note on the dialectical fallacies as a family

The five fallacies share a structure. Each is a **fallacy of transfer**: the credit earned by one thing is illicitly transferred to another.

| Fallacy | Credit earned by | Transferred to |
|---|---|---|
| **Straw man** | Refuting $S'$ | Refuting $S$ |
| **Motte-and-bailey** | Defending $M$ | The claim $B$ |
| **Equivocation** | The true reading $T_1$ | The needed reading $T_2$ |
| **Begging the question** | Accepting $p$ (which presupposes $c$) | Establishing $c$ |
| **Deepity** | The trivial reading $R_1$ | The profound reading $R_2$ |

This is why the formal-fallacy taxonomy is insufficient for philosophy. The dialectical fallacies are not defects of individual inferences; they are defects of the *bookkeeping* — of what is being claimed, on which reading, at which point in the exchange. And bookkeeping is what philosophy, at its best, is.

### 2.4 The fallacy fallacy

The most important caution in this chapter: **the detection of a fallacy does not establish the falsehood of the conclusion.**

This is the **fallacy fallacy** (sometimes *argumentum ad logicam*): “Your argument is fallacious, therefore your conclusion is false.” The conclusion may be true, and supported by a better argument that you have not considered. The most that a fallacy establishes is that *this* argument fails to support the conclusion.

There is a second, subtler version: the **fallacy-hunting fallacy** — treating the detection of fallacies as the goal of philosophical reading, rather than as a diagnostic step. A reader who comes to a text looking for fallacies will find them (there are always some, in any text), and will miss the argument. The taxonomy is a set of instruments, not a set of weapons.

And a third: the **rhetorical use of the fallacy label**. Naming a fallacy is an accusation, and it can be a form of bullying — a way of dismissing an argument without engaging it. “That’s a slippery slope” is not an argument; it is a claim that requires the specific diagnosis (which slope? which causal link? what evidence?). The ethics of discourse (§4) applies to the use of the taxonomy itself.

### 2.5 Workshop: a composite passage

Here is a composite passage — *not* a quotation from any single author — of the kind one meets in popular and semi-popular philosophy. Diagnose it.

> Contemporary physicalism is untenable. Its defenders claim that everything is physical, but when pressed they admit that they cannot explain consciousness. Either consciousness is physical or it is not; if it is, then it must be locatable in the brain, but no one has found it there. If it is not, then physicalism is false. The physicalist’s usual reply — that consciousness is an “emergent” property — is mere hand-waving: to say that something emerges is just to say that it appears, which is exactly what needs explaining. Moreover, the whole physicalist research programme has been a failure: after a century, we still have no theory. It is time to take dualism seriously.

**Diagnosis.**

1. **Equivocation on “physical.”** Sense 1: *described by physics* (plausibly true of everything, including consciousness). Sense 2: *reducible to the entities of fundamental physics* (contested). The argument’s plausibility comes from Sense 1; its conclusion needs Sense 2. The argument is valid only on a reading where “physical” means the same thing throughout; on the reading the conclusion needs, the two senses come apart. No single reading makes it both valid and sound.

2. **False dilemma / suppressed alternative.** “Either physical or not” is exhaustive only on the disputed sense of “physical.” The argument ignores non-reductive physicalism, functionalism, panpsychism, neutral monism, and mysterianism — each of which is a distinct option. The “if it is, it must be locatable in the brain” premise is simply asserted; it is precisely what the functionalist denies.

3. **Straw man.** The “usual reply” — emergence — is not the strongest physicalist reply. The strongest replies (phenomenal concepts, higher-order theories, illusionism, global workspace) are not considered. The argument refutes a weak version and concludes against the strong one.

4. **Motte-and-bailey.** The bailey is “physicalism is false.” The motte is “physicalists cannot *explain* consciousness.” The motte is true (in the sense that there is no consensus explanation); the bailey does not follow. The middle of the passage is a defence of the motte; the end is a resumption of the bailey.

5. **Equivocation on “explain.”** To explain can mean (a) to give a mechanism, (b) to identify with something else, (c) to give an account of why the explanandum seems mysterious. The argument assumes (a), and the assumption is not argued.

6. **Appeal to ignorance / argument from lack of imagination.** “No one has found it in the brain” and “we have no theory” are claims about the current state of inquiry, not about the nature of the thing. The history of science is a history of things not yet found.

7. **The “mere hand-waving” move.** This is tonal, not argumentative. It dismisses a position by describing it dismissively. If emergence is vacuous, the argument must show that it is — by showing that the emergentist cannot specify what emerges, or how, or why the emergence is not a restatement. That is a real argument, and it is not given.

8. **Non sequitur in the conclusion.** Even if physicalism is incomplete, dualism is not the only alternative. The conclusion (“it is time to take dualism seriously”) does not follow from the premises; it follows only given a further premise that dualism is the sole remaining option.

**What a charitable version would look like.** The passage has a real argument inside it. Reconstructed charitably (Ch. 2, §3): *If physicalism is true, then every fact is entailed by the physical facts. The facts of phenomenal consciousness are not entailed by the physical facts (as the various anti-physicalist arguments purport to show). Therefore physicalism is false.* This is a valid argument; the work is in the second premise, and the passage gestures at it without stating it. The lesson: **the charitable reconstruction is not a courtesy; it is the only way to find out whether the passage has an argument.** And in this case, it does — a much better one than the passage’s rhetoric suggests.

## 3. The Criteria of a Good Theory

### 3.1 The six criteria

Philosophy produces theories — of knowledge, of mind, of value, of reality. What makes one theory better than another? The following criteria are the standard ones. None is a decision procedure; all are contested; together they constitute the vocabulary of theory assessment.

| Criterion | What it demands | How to test | Warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Consistency** | The theory does not contradict itself, or the best-established background beliefs | Derive consequences; look for contradictions | Consistency is necessary, not sufficient; a consistent theory can be empty |
| **Fertility** | The theory generates new questions, predictions, or lines of inquiry | Has it led to progress? | Speculative fertility is cheap |
| **Simplicity** | The theory posits fewer entities, principles, or parameters | Count the posits; compare formulations | “Simple” is theory-relative (§3.3) |
| **Conservatism** | The theory requires fewer revisions of established belief | Compare the revisions required | Conservatism can entrench error (§3.4) |
| **Explanatory power** | The theory explains the phenomena it is meant to explain, and more | Scope, precision, mechanism | Explanatory power can be bought with vacuity |
| **Adequacy to ordinary thought** | The theory does not do excessive violence to the data of pre-theoretic judgment | Reflective equilibrium (Ch. 3, §4) | Ordinary thought can be wrong (§3.5) |

**Consistency** has two forms. *Internal* consistency: no contradiction among the theory’s claims. *External* consistency: no contradiction with the best-established background beliefs — logic, mathematics, empirical science, and the most secure deliverances of ordinary experience. External consistency is not absolute (the background can be revised), but it is a real constraint: a theory that contradicts quantum mechanics, or the existence of the external world, owes us an argument.

**Fertility** is the criterion most often honoured in the breach. A theory that explains the existing data but generates no new questions is a *closed* theory; a theory that opens new lines of inquiry is *live*. The history of philosophy suggests that the live theories are the ones that survive, not because they are truer but because they are *worked on*.

**Explanatory power** is not a single thing. It includes *scope* (how much is explained), *precision* (how specific the explanations are), *mechanism* (whether the theory says how the explanation works), and *unification* (whether disparate phenomena are brought under one principle). The criteria can conflict: a theory with great scope may be vague; a precise theory may explain little.

### 3.2 The no-algorithm problem

The criteria conflict. A simple theory may be less conservative; a fertile theory may be less simple; an explanatorily powerful theory may be less adequate to ordinary thought. There is **no algorithm** for weighing them, and the attempt to produce one — a formal scoring system — usually produces a false precision.

This is not a defect of the criteria; it is a feature of the epistemic situation. Theory choice in philosophy is *comparative* and *holistic*: you compare the total package of virtues, against the background of the alternatives actually on the table, and you make a judgment that is itself arguable. The judgment can be criticised — “you are weighting simplicity too heavily,” “you are ignoring the cost in adequacy” — and the criticism is itself a philosophical argument. That is the method.

The following matrix is a *heuristic for comparison*, not a measurement. It is useful for making the weighting explicit, and dangerous if treated as arithmetic.

| Criterion | Physicalism | Property dualism | Idealism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consistency | High | Medium | Medium |
| Fertility | High | Low | Low |
| Simplicity | High | Low | Medium |
| Conservatism | High | Low | Low |
| Explanatory power | Medium | Medium | Low |
| Adequacy to ordinary thought | Medium | High | Low |

The matrix is not an argument. It is a *representation of an argument* — a way of showing where the disagreement lies. If someone rejects the verdict, the matrix tells you which cell to argue about.

### 3.3 Simplicity

Simplicity is the most abused criterion in philosophy. The abuse takes the form of treating “simple” as though it were a single, obvious property, when in fact there are at least four distinct notions:

1. **Syntactic simplicity**: the theory can be stated with fewer symbols or shorter formulas. (A formal notion; there is no canonical measure, but Kolmogorov complexity is one attempt.)
2. **Ontological simplicity**: the theory posits fewer entities. (Ockham’s razor in its classic form: *entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem*.)
3. **Explanatory simplicity**: the theory uses fewer independent principles, or unifies more phenomena under one principle. (This is the notion at work in “elegance” in physics.)
4. **Parameter simplicity**: the theory has fewer free parameters. (The statistician’s notion; it connects to the bias–variance tradeoff.)

These notions come apart. A theory can be ontologically parsimonious but syntactically complex (fewer entities, more complicated laws); or syntactically simple but ontologically profligate (one short formula, many entities). When a philosopher says “my theory is simpler,” the first question is: *simpler in which sense?*

**The truth-conduciveness question.** Is simplicity evidence of truth? The traditional answer is yes (Ockham, and most of the scientific-realist tradition). The modern answer is more cautious: simplicity is a *virtue* of theories, but its connection to truth is not obvious. The statistical literature has shown that simpler models are not always more accurate out of sample (the “no free lunch” theorems; the bias–variance tradeoff means that *some* complexity is necessary for accuracy). The philosophical literature (Sober 1975, 2015) has explored the conditions under which parsimony is truth-conducive, and the conditions are not trivial.

**The rhetorical danger.** “Simple” is often a cover for “what I already believe.” A theory is called simple when its posits are familiar, and complex when its posits are unfamiliar. The test is to ask: *would this theory be called simple by someone who did not already accept it?* If not, the appeal to simplicity is doing ideological work.

### 3.4 Conservatism

The **principle of conservatism**: a theory that requires fewer revisions of established belief is, other things equal, preferable. The rationale is Quinean: our beliefs form a web, and revising a central strand is costly (Ch. 8, §3). The principle is a counsel of intellectual economy.

Its danger is obvious: conservatism can entrench error. Every revolutionary theory was, at the time, non-conservative. The heliocentric theory, the theory of evolution, and the rejection of natural slavery all required revising beliefs that were deeply established. A conservatism that is too strong would have blocked them.

The resolution is not to abandon conservatism but to *contextualise* it. Conservatism is a *default*, not an absolute: it tells you what to believe in the absence of a strong argument for revision. When the argument for revision is strong enough, the cost of revision is worth paying. The question is always *how strong is strong enough*, and that is a judgment, not a calculation.

There is a further point: conservatism is a virtue only if the established beliefs have some track record. In domains where the track record is poor — moral beliefs about out-groups, beliefs about the capacities of women and minorities, beliefs about the nature of consciousness — conservatism should have little weight. The criterion is not neutral across domains; it is a function of the reliability of the background.

### 3.5 Adequacy to ordinary thought

The **adequacy criterion**: a theory should not do excessive violence to the data of pre-theoretic judgment. This is the reflective-equilibrium requirement (Ch. 3, §4) in its evaluative form.

But what counts as “excessive”? The criterion is not that a theory must preserve *all* ordinary judgments; it is that the theory must account for them, or explain them away, or show that they are not what they seem. A theory that implies that no one ever knows anything is not thereby refuted — it is a *revisionary* theory, and revisionary theories are legitimate (Ch. 22, §4) — but it owes an account of why the ordinary judgment is so persistent, and what function it serves.

The distinction between the **data** of ordinary thought and the **theory** of ordinary thought is crucial. The data are the particular judgments (“I know that I have hands,” “torturing a child for fun is wrong”). The theory is the general framework that the folk use to explain those judgments (a faculty of intuition, a moral sense, a soul). A theory can revise the folk theory while preserving the data; it cannot simply ignore the data.

**The revisionary option.** Some of the best philosophy is revisionary: it tells us that our ordinary concepts are confused, or that our ordinary judgments are systematically mistaken. The revisionary philosopher owes two things: an argument that the revision is *necessary* (that no conservative theory can accommodate the data), and an argument that the revision is *worth the cost* (that the theoretical gains outweigh the losses in ordinary thought). The burden is heavy, and it is often not met.

### 3.6 The meta-criterion: honesty

A good theory is not only one that satisfies the criteria; it is one whose **author is honest about which criteria it satisfies and which it does not**.

This means, at a minimum:

- **Specifying the costs.** Every theory has costs. The honest theorist names them.
- **Specifying the defeat conditions.** What would count against the theory? A theory for which nothing counts against it is not a theory; it is a commitment.
- **Specifying the alternatives.** The theory is better than which alternatives, on which criteria? A theory that is not compared is not assessed.
- **Specifying the confidence.** How confident is the author, and why? (§4.1.)

The meta-criterion is the one that connects this section to the next. A theory that satisfies the six criteria but is presented with false confidence is a *bad* theory in the ethical sense, even if it is a good theory in the logical sense. And in philosophy, the ethical sense matters: the reader is being asked to trust the author’s judgment, and the trust is warranted only if the author is honest about the limits of the judgment.

## 4. The Ethics of Intellectual Discourse

> “Be a philosopher; but, amidst all your philosophy, be still a man.”
> — Hume, *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, §1

### 4.1 Calibrated confidence

The first ethical demand on a philosopher is **honesty about the strength of her evidence**. This is harder than it sounds, because the language of philosophy is the language of assertion, and assertion carries an implicit claim of confidence that may not be warranted.

The demand has two parts.

**Part one: the credence ladder.** Philosophical claims come in different strengths, and the language should match the strength. The following table is conventional, not precise; its purpose is to make the *discipline of matching* explicit.

| Locution | Rough credence | Appropriate use |
|---|---|---|
| “It is certain that $p$” | $\geq 0.99$ | Logical truths, definitions, direct observation |
| “It is nearly certain” | $0.9$–$0.99$ | Well-established empirical facts |
| “It is highly probable” | $0.75$–$0.9$ | Strong evidence, converging arguments |
| “It is probable” | $0.6$–$0.75$ | Better supported than not |
| “It is plausible” | $0.4$–$0.6$ | A live option, worth taking seriously |
| “It is possible” | $> 0$ | Not ruled out |
| “It is speculative” | low | A hypothesis to explore, not to rely on |

The numbers are not measurements; they are expressions of *comparative* confidence. The point is not to quantify, but to **force the question**: *how confident am I, and does my language say so?*

**Part two: the matching principle.** The strength of the conclusion must match the strength of the argument. A valid argument from merely probable premises yields a merely probable conclusion; a conclusion asserted as certain from such premises is a *non sequitur* of confidence. The vice has two forms:

- **Overclaiming**: “This proves that …” when the argument merely suggests. The commonest form in student writing, and in popular philosophy.
- **Underclaiming**: “This might perhaps suggest, in some sense, that …” when the argument is actually strong. The commonest form in anxious academic writing, and it is also a vice: it hides the argument behind a hedge.

The matching principle is the ethical core of philosophical writing. It is also the hardest to internalise, because the temptation to overclaim is the temptation to be *interesting*, and the temptation to underclaim is the temptation to be *safe*. Both are temptations to be something other than honest.

### 4.2 The vice of cleverness

There is a vice specific to philosophy, and it is not stupidity. It is **cleverness**: the disposition to value the striking, the paradoxical, the ingenious over the true.

The vice takes several forms.

**(i) Novelty for its own sake.** The desire to say something that has not been said, whether or not it is true. The result is a literature of ingenious positions that no one would hold if the incentive were truth rather than originality.

**(ii) The love of paradox.** Paradoxes are exciting; they promise a thrill of the counterintuitive. The temptation is to embrace the paradox before checking whether the appearance of paradox is an artifact of a confused formulation. The best response to a paradox is not to solve it but to *diagnose* it: is it a genuine contradiction, or a confusion in the setup? (Ch. 33, §1.)

**(iii) The elegant refutation.** There is an aesthetic pleasure in the decisive objection, the *tu quoque* that turns the argument against itself, the *reductio* that collapses the opponent’s position. The pleasure is real, and it is dangerous, because it can make a bad objection feel good. The test is not whether the refutation is elegant; it is whether it is *sound*.

**(iv) Distinction-mongering.** The multiplication of distinctions can be a way of avoiding a problem: each new distinction creates a space in which the problem can be deferred. The distinctions may all be valid, and the problem may still be there. The test is whether the distinctions are *doing work* — whether they change what can be said.

**(v) The clever sentence.** A sentence that is technically true but misleading; a formulation that is defensible but not honest; the lawyer’s move. The paradigm is the hedge that protects the author while suggesting the claim: “It is not implausible to suppose that, in some sense, …” The sentence is true if the supposition is not implausible; the reader is meant to hear the stronger claim.

The remedy is not the suppression of cleverness — philosophy needs intelligent, inventive minds — but its *subordination*. The question to ask of any philosophical move is not “Is this clever?” but “Is this true, and is the cleverness serving the truth or replacing it?” The **boring-truth test**: a true but boring observation is worth more than a false but exciting one, and the philosopher who cannot tolerate boredom is a philosopher who will be tempted by the false.

The vice of cleverness has a social dimension. Cleverness is rewarded: the clever paper is cited, the clever seminar remark is remembered, the clever dismissal is quoted. The incentives of the discipline are not always aligned with the incentives of truth. The ethical response is not to pretend that the incentives do not exist, but to be *conscious* of them, and to build practices — the diary, the steel man, the best objection — that counteract them.

### 4.3 The ethics of criticism

The ethics of criticism can be summarised in four norms.

**(i) Criticise the argument, not the person.** The *ad hominem* is the paradigm violation. But the norm is subtler than it looks: the argument is the person’s, and criticism is a social act that can wound. The norm does not require indifference to the person; it requires that the force of the criticism be directed at the argument, and that the person be treated as a participant in inquiry rather than an obstacle to it.

**(ii) Engage the strongest version.** This is the charity requirement of Ch. 2, §3, applied to public criticism. The obligation is not merely epistemic (you will understand the position better) but ethical (you owe the author the respect of engaging her actual view). The straw man is not only a logical fallacy; it is a failure of respect.

**(iii) Be specific.** “This is confused” is not a criticism; it is an expression of irritation. A criticism must identify the premise, the inference, the distinction, or the passage that fails, and say *why* it fails. Specific criticism is useful; vague criticism is merely discouraging.

**(iv) Be constructive where possible.** Where a criticism can be paired with a suggestion — a distinction that would resolve the problem, a premise that would repair the argument, a literature that would address the objection — the criticism is more useful. This is not a demand that every criticism be constructive (some arguments are beyond repair, and saying so is a contribution); it is a demand that the critic consider whether the repair is possible before declaring the position dead.

The **referee report** is the test case. A good report does four things: summarises the paper accurately (this is the test of whether the referee has read it); identifies the paper’s contribution (this is the test of whether the referee has understood it); states the principal objections, in order of importance, with reasons; and recommends a decision, with an explanation of what would change it. A bad report does one thing: it expresses a verdict. The difference is the difference between a reader’s advocate and a gatekeeper’s ego.

### 4.4 Citation, attribution, and the duty to read

The steel-manning requirement has a corollary that is rarely stated: **you cannot steel-man a text you have not read.** The straw man is often the product of reading the summary rather than the text, or the critique rather than the original.

The ethical norms of citation follow:

- **Cite the actual text**, with page numbers, not the secondary summary (unless the summary is the object of criticism).
- **Distinguish your view from the views you discuss.** The reader should always be able to tell whose claim is whose.
- **Acknowledge the strongest version.** If you are criticising a position, say which version you are criticising, and why it is the strongest.
- **Do not cite authorities as arguments.** A citation is evidence that someone said something; it is not evidence that what they said is true. (The appeal-to-authority fallacy, §2.2.)
- **Acknowledge the limits of your reading.** If you have not read the whole literature, say so.

The **secondary literature** question is a real one. Engaging the secondary literature is a sign of scholarly responsibility, but it can also be a way of avoiding the primary text, or of substituting the conversation for the argument. The rule of thumb: engage the secondary literature *where it bears on the argument*, and not as a ritual of deference.

### 4.5 The ethics of belief

The question of how we ought to form beliefs is not new. **Clifford’s principle** (1877): “It is wrong, always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence.” **James’s reply** (1896): in some cases — where the option is forced, live, and momentous — the evidence cannot be independent of the belief, and the refusal to believe is itself a decision.

The debate is often framed as a dispute about *doxastic voluntarism* (whether we can choose our beliefs). The framing is misleading: the ethics of belief is not primarily about the voluntary control of belief, but about the *norms* that govern belief-formation, and about the *practices* that shape it. We cannot choose to believe that $2+2=5$, but we can choose what to read, whom to trust, and how much to inquire — and these choices shape what we believe.

The modern position is a *middle way*, and it has three parts:

1. **Evidence is the primary norm.** Clifford is right that belief should be proportioned to evidence, and that the deliberate cultivation of credulity is a vice.
2. **The stakes matter.** James is right that the *cost* of error and the *cost* of missing the truth are both relevant, and that in some contexts the rational response to uncertainty is action rather than suspension. (This is the insight behind pragmatic encroachment, Ch. 8, §5.)
3. **The norm is social.** The ethics of belief is not only about the individual believer; it is about the *epistemic environment* — the institutions, the media, the testimonial practices — that shape what beliefs are available and credible (Ch. 11).

The application to philosophy is direct. The philosopher’s beliefs are formed by reading, by conversation, by the pressure of the discipline, and by temperament. The ethical demand is to be *aware* of these influences, to *test* the beliefs against the strongest objections, and to *calibrate* the confidence to the evidence. The philosophical diary (§5.1) is the technology for doing so.

### 4.6 Disagreement and humility

The final ethical demand is the hardest: **to hold a view while acknowledging that reasonable people disagree.**

The epistemology of disagreement (Ch. 11, §3) offers three broad positions: the *equal-weight* view (when an epistemic peer disagrees, you should reduce your confidence), the *steadfast* view (you may retain your confidence if you have good reason to think your assessment is better), and the *total-evidence* view (the disagreement is evidence, but its weight depends on the details). The debate is unresolved, and this book does not pretend to settle it.

The ethical demand is less theoretical. It is the demand for **humility without cowardice**:

- **Humility**: the recognition that your view may be wrong, that the disagreement is evidence, and that the confidence you feel is not a guarantee of truth.
- **Without cowardice**: the willingness to state the view, defend it, and act on it, despite the disagreement. The cowardly response to disagreement is to withhold judgment indefinitely; the arrogant response is to dismiss the disagreement as irrational. The virtuous response is to hold the view *with the confidence the evidence warrants*, and to be open to revision.

The test of humility is not what you say about your fallibility; it is what you do when you encounter a serious objection. The test of courage is not what you say about your convictions; it is what you do when the convictions are unpopular. A philosopher who passes both tests is rare, and worth reading.

## 5. Self-Examination

### 5.1 The philosophical diary

The single most useful practice in philosophy is also the least discussed: **keep a diary of your philosophical views.**

The rationale is psychological and epistemological. Memory is reconstructive: we do not retrieve our past beliefs; we reconstruct them, and the reconstruction is biased toward our present beliefs. We *believe* we have always thought what we now think; the diary is the evidence that we have not. Without it, the illusion of consistency is almost impossible to break.

**Format.** A dated entry for each significant philosophical question, with the following fields:

```
Date:
Question:
My current answer:
My confidence (0–1):
Best argument for my answer:
Best objection to my answer:
What would change my mind:
What I need to read / think about next:
```

The diary is not a journal of feelings; it is a record of *positions*, with their arguments and their confidence levels. The confidence field is essential: it is the technology that makes it possible to see, over time, which views have *earned* their confidence and which have merely persisted.

**Frequency.** Weekly, or after each significant reading or conversation. The discipline is more important than the frequency.

**Review.** Reread the diary annually. The review has three purposes: to see which views have changed and why; to see which views have *not* changed despite the accumulation of objections (a warning sign); and to see which questions have remained open for years (a sign that the question is either deep or badly posed — Ch. 1, §3).

### 5.2 Writing out your own best objection

The second practice is the one that most improves philosophical work: **write the strongest objection to your own view, in the voice of someone who holds it.**

The rules:

1. **The objection must be fair.** It must engage your actual view, not a caricature of it.
2. **The objection must be strong.** It must be the best version you can construct, not the version you can answer.
3. **The objection must be unanswered.** Do not smuggle in the reply. Write the objection as though you were its author, and let it stand.

Then, and only then, attempt the reply.

The value of the exercise is not that it produces an answer (sometimes it does not). The value is that it converts the *fear* of objection into *familiarity* with it. The philosopher who has written her own best objection is no longer surprised by it; she has already seen it, and she knows what she thinks. The philosopher who has not is vulnerable to the first competent critic — and, worse, to the first *incompetent* one, because she has no way of distinguishing them.

**The devil’s advocate as a discipline.** The exercise is a form of what the tradition calls the *advocatus diaboli*: the deliberate construction of the case against one’s own position. In the Catholic canonisation process, the *promotor fidei* was appointed to argue against the candidate; the institution was designed to make the case *for* the candidate stronger by forcing it to survive the strongest case *against*. Philosophy could use more of this.

### 5.3 The five-year test and steel-manning yourself

Two further tests, both simple, both useful.

**The five-year test.** Ask: *will I still hold this view in five years, after more reading and more objections?* If the answer is no, the view is not yet stable — which is not a reason to abandon it, but a reason to hold it with *calibrated* confidence, and to keep working on the objections. If the answer is yes, ask *why*: is it because the view is well-supported, or because it has become part of your identity? The distinction is not always easy to draw, and drawing it is part of the work.

**Steel-manning yourself.** Ask: *can I state my view in a form that my opponents would recognise as their target?* If not, you have not understood your own view. The test is not whether the opponents *agree* with your formulation; it is whether they would say, “yes, that is what I am denying.” A view that cannot be stated in a form its opponents recognise is a view that has not been formulated clearly enough to be denied — and, therefore, clearly enough to be defended.

### 5.4 The confession of ignorance

The next practice is the *confession of ignorance*: the explicit statement of what one does not know.

This is the Socratic move, and it is the condition of inquiry. The philosopher who cannot say “I do not know” is condemned to defend whatever she has said, because she has made her views into her identity. The philosopher who can say it is free to learn.

The practice has a concrete form: keep a running list of **open questions** in your area. Not the questions you have answers to, but the ones you do not. The list is the seed of the next paper, the next reading, the next conversation. It is also the antidote to the vice of cleverness: the clever philosopher is full of answers; the honest philosopher is full of questions.

The **Socratic paradox** is relevant here: Socrates claimed to know only that he did not know, and this was the beginning of wisdom. The paradox is not a puzzle to be solved; it is a description of the epistemic position of the finite inquirer. The philosopher who claims to know everything is not wise; she is merely unacquainted with her own limits.

### 5.5 The communal dimension

Self-examination is not solitary. The best check on self-deception is **the other person** — the colleague who does not share your assumptions, the student who asks the naive question, the opponent who is smarter than you.

The institutions of philosophy are, at their best, technologies of self-correction:

- The **seminar**: a space in which arguments are tested by people who are not you.
- The **reading group**: a space in which texts are read carefully, by several people, so that no one’s reading goes unchallenged.
- The **workshop and conference**: a space in which work is exposed to criticism before it is published.
- The **referee report**: a space in which the work is tested by someone who has no stake in it.
- **Teaching**: the most underrated form of self-examination, because the student’s question exposes the assumption you did not know you had.

The virtue that these institutions require is **teachability**: the willingness to be changed by an argument. It is not the same as agreeableness (the agreeable philosopher changes her view to avoid conflict); it is not the same as stubbornness (the stubborn philosopher never changes). It is the disposition to be *moved by reasons*, and to accept the cost of being moved.

### 5.6 A curriculum for oneself

The practices of §5.1–5.5 are the elements of a **lifelong curriculum** — a programme of self-directed philosophical formation. The curriculum has four components:

1. **Read outside your area.** The specialist’s knowledge is deep and narrow; the generalist’s is shallow and broad. The best philosophers are both, and the way to become both is to read outside the area of expertise, regularly, with the same care you give to your own field.
2. **Write regularly.** Not only papers; also notes, objections, diary entries, and letters. Writing is the instrument of thought (§1.6).
3. **Seek the strongest critics.** Not the ones who will agree, and not the ones who will attack you personally, but the ones who will engage your argument at its strongest and show you where it fails.
4. **Teach.** Teaching forces clarity, and clarity exposes confusion. The best way to find out whether you understand an argument is to explain it to someone who does not.

The ancient model for this curriculum is the **philosophical exercise** (*askēsis*): the daily practice of reflection, argument, and self-examination, aimed not only at knowledge but at *wisdom* (Hadot 1981/1995; Foucault 1984). The modern academy has largely abandoned the model, to its cost. The practices in this chapter are an attempt to recover a version of it — adapted to the conditions of contemporary philosophy, and stripped of the metaphysics of the ancient schools, but retaining the core insight: **philosophy is a way of life, not only a body of doctrine.**

> <details><summary><b>Advanced thread: the institution of philosophy</b></summary>
>
> Three further questions, for the reader who wants to think about the social conditions of the discipline.
>
> **1. The incentive structure.** Publication, citation, and prestige reward novelty and polemic more than careful, boring, true work. The individual philosopher can resist the incentives, but the discipline as a whole is shaped by them. The question is whether the institutions can be redesigned — and, if so, how.
>
> **2. Gatekeeping and epistemic injustice.** Who gets published, who gets cited, who gets hired, and whose questions are treated as central are not neutral facts. The literature on epistemic injustice (Fricker 2007; Ch. 11, §4) applies to philosophy itself. The discipline’s demographics are not a side issue; they shape what counts as a problem and what counts as a solution.
>
> **3. Philosophy and the public.** The public role of philosophy — in policy, in education, in public deliberation — is contested. The Socratic model (the gadfly) and the academic model (the specialist) are different conceptions of the philosopher’s social function. The book’s own conception — philosophy as the study of the conditions of intelligibility — has implications for both: the philosopher is the person who asks what a practice presupposes, and that question is as relevant to public life as it is to the seminar room.
> </details>

## Coda: The End of the Beginning

This chapter completes Part I. The reader now has a conception of the subject (Ch. 1), an anatomy of the argument (Ch. 2), a repertoire of instruments (Ch. 3), and a practice — reading, writing, evaluating, and self-examining (Ch. 4).

Part II turns to the terrain: knowledge and its limits. The order is deliberate. The problems of epistemology are intoxicating — the sceptic’s challenge, the Gettier problem, the regress of justification — and a reader who encounters them without the instruments of Part I will be swept along by whichever argument she reads last. A reader who has the instruments will be able to *reconstruct* the arguments, *diagnose* their failures, and *assess* their strength. That is the difference between reading philosophy and *doing* it.

The chapter’s closing advice is the one that matters most: **the practices are more important than the doctrines.** The diary, the best objection, the steel man, the confession of ignorance — these are not preliminaries to philosophy; they are philosophy. The doctrines change; the practices are what make the change possible.

## Summary

- A philosophical paper has **five parts**: question, thesis, argument, objection, reply. The proportions are diagnostic; a paper without a serious objection section is a paper that has not looked for trouble.
- **From topic to question to thesis**: the funnel is specific, answerable, contestable. The thesis must be a proposition, specific, contestable, and *matched* to the argument.
- **Reading** is a three-pass skill: survey, reconstruct, evaluate. The one-sentence summary is the test of pass 2; reading against the grain is the bridge to pass 3. Reading the classics requires historical charity and anachronism-check.
- **Writing** is the instrument of thought. One idea per paragraph; given-to-new; signpost; define and use terms consistently; quote precisely; cut ten percent.
- **Fallacies** come in three tiers: formal (bad form), informal (bad content or context), dialectical (bad structure of the exchange). The dialectical fallacies — straw man, motte-and-bailey, equivocation, begging the question, deepity — are all **fallacies of transfer**: credit earned by one thing is illicitly transferred to another.
- **The fallacy fallacy** is the most important caution: detecting a fallacy does not establish the falsehood of the conclusion, and fallacy-hunting can itself be a vice.
- **The criteria of a good theory** — consistency, fertility, simplicity, conservatism, explanatory power, adequacy to ordinary thought — conflict, and there is no algorithm for weighing them. **Simplicity** is the most abused criterion; **conservatism** is a default, not an absolute; **adequacy** distinguishes the data from the theory of ordinary thought. The **meta-criterion** is honesty about which criteria the theory satisfies and what would count against it.
- **The ethics of discourse** demands calibrated confidence, the matching principle, the subordination of cleverness to truth, the criticism of arguments rather than persons, the engagement of the strongest version, and humility without cowardice.
- **Self-examination** is a set of practices: the philosophical diary, writing out one’s own best objection, the five-year test, steel-manning oneself, the confession of ignorance, and the communal institutions of correction. The practices are more important than the doctrines.

## Study Questions

1. Take a paper you admire and reconstruct it using the five-part anatomy (§1.1). Which part is strongest? Which is weakest? Does the weakness affect the thesis?
2. Write a one-sentence summary of a paper you have read recently. Then write a one-sentence summary of its *argument*. If you cannot do both, what does that tell you about the paper — or about your reading of it?
3. Rewrite the composite paragraph in §1.7 in your own words, improving the thesis and the argument. What did you have to add?
4. Identify a fallacy in an argument you have recently encountered (in a paper, a conversation, or the media). Diagnose it using the three-tier taxonomy. Is it a formal, informal, or dialectical fallacy? What would the charitable version of the argument look like?
5. Construct a deepity of your own. Then construct a sentence that *looks* like a deepity but is genuinely deep, and explain the difference.
6. Is the motte-and-bailey always a fallacy? Give a case in which the retreat from bailey to motte is legitimate, and explain why it is not the fallacy.
7. Apply the six criteria of §3.1 to a theory you hold. Which criteria does it satisfy? Which does it fail? Is the failure a reason to abandon it, or a cost to be paid?
8. Is simplicity truth-conducive? Argue both sides, then state your view with a calibrated confidence.
9. “The ethics of belief is impossible, because beliefs are not under our control.” Assess. What follows for the practices of §5 if the objection is correct?
10. Write your own best objection to a view you hold. Then write the reply. Which is stronger, and what would it take to change your mind? (This is the exercise of §5.2, and it is the most important question in the chapter.)

## Further Reading

- **On philosophical writing**: Eric Hayot, *The Elements of Academic Style* (2014), for the craft; Helen Sword, *Stylish Academic Writing* (2012), for the sentence; Joseph Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* (12th ed., 2016), for the given–new contract; Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014), for the cognitive science of prose. On the philosophical essay specifically: A. P. Martinich, *Philosophical Writing* (4th ed., 2015).
- **On reading**: Mortimer Adler and Charles Van Doren, *How to Read a Book* (1940/1972), for the general method; the advice in *The Philosophers’ Magazine* and in departmental writing guides is often more specific. On reading the classics: Bernard Williams, “The Legacy of Greek Philosophy” (1993), and the methodological essays in Richard Sorabji, *Time, Creation and the Continuum* (1983).
- **On fallacies**: Douglas Walton, *Informal Logic: A Handbook for Critical Argumentation* (1989), and *Appeal to Expert Opinion* (1997), for the nuanced treatments; Charles Hamblin, *Fallacies* (1970), for the history; the *Stanford Encyclopedia* entries on “Fallacies,” “Begging the Question,” and “The Straw Man Fallacy” (by Walton and others). On deepity: Daniel Dennett, “Deepities,” *Boston Review* (2009), and *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking* (2013), Ch. 12 (“What Is a Deepity?”). On the motte-and-bailey: Nicholas Shackel, “The Vacuity of Postmodernist Methodology,” *Metaphilosophy* 36(3) (2005): 295–320.
- **On theory choice**: Thomas Kuhn, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962), for the paradigm framework; W. V. O. Quine and J. S. Ullian, *The Web of Belief* (2nd ed., 1978), for the conservative/holistic picture; Elliott Sober, *Simplicity* (1975) and *Ockham’s Razors* (2015), for the analysis of parsimony; Paul Thagard, *Computational Philosophy of Science* (1988), for the attempt to formalise theory choice.
- **On the ethics of belief**: W. K. Clifford, “The Ethics of Belief” (1877); William James, “The Will to Believe” (1896); the essays in *The Ethics of Belief* (ed. Jonathan Matheson and Rico Vitz, 2014); on epistemic virtue, Linda Zagzebski, *Virtues of the Mind* (1996), and Jason Baehr, *The Inquiring Mind* (2011).
- **On the vice of cleverness**: the theme is pervasive in the tradition — see Kierkegaard’s critique of the “brilliant” Hegelians in *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (1846), and Wittgenstein’s remarks on the fly in the fly-bottle and on the “rough” uses of ordinary language (*Philosophical Investigations* §23; *Culture and Value*, 1980). For a contemporary treatment, see the essays on intellectual humility, intellectual vice, and creativity in *The Routledge Handbook of Virtue Epistemology* (ed. Battaly, 2018), Part 2.
- **On self-examination and the philosophical life**: Pierre Hadot, *Philosophy as a Way of Life* (1981/1995), and *What Is Ancient Philosophy?* (2002); Michel Foucault, *The Care of the Self* (1984/1988); Pierre Hadot’s critique of Foucault is itself instructive. On the diary as a practice, see the Stoic *Meditations* of Marcus Aurelius, and, in a different register, Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Culture and Value* (1980), and the *Diaries* of Simone Weil.
- **On the institution of philosophy**: Kristie Dotson, “How Is This Paper Philosophy?” (2012), on disciplinary norms and exclusion; Miranda Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice* (2007), on testimonial and hermeneutical injustice; the essays in *The Good Place* (ed. Samantha Brennan and Tracy Isaacs, 2018), on the ethics of academic life.

> *A closing note.* This chapter has been about the craft — the disciplines that turn intelligence into philosophy. The crafts are unglamorous: the diary, the objection, the rewrite, the cut. They do not produce the thrill of the great idea, and they are not much discussed in the seminar room. But they are where the work is done. The reader who adopts them will find, over time, that her views have changed — not because she has been persuaded by a single argument, but because she has built a practice that makes it possible to be persuaded. That is the beginning of philosophical independence.

> *And a final note on the book’s method.* This chapter has applied the book’s thesis to the book itself: it has specified what it claims, what it does not, what would count against it, and what the reader should do with it. The reader who finds the chapter dogmatic should ask whether the dogmatism is in the chapter or in her reading of it. The reader who finds it obvious should ask whether the obviousness is in the content or in the familiarity. Either way, the chapter has done its work if it has made the question unavoidable.
