# Appendix D — Who's Who: A Chronological Map

**What this appendix is.** A navigational instrument, not a history. Each entry gives a figure, dates, a one-line statement of the thesis or contribution for which the book uses them, and the chapters where they appear. The one-liners are deliberately lossy: they are handles, not summaries, and any figure reduced to a sentence has been wronged. Where a thinker's position changed materially (Jackson on the knowledge argument, Wittgenstein between the two books), the entry flags it.

**Three ways to use it.** *By name*: find the figure, get the thesis, get the chapter. *By problem*: use §D.11, which re-sorts the same figures by question rather than by date. *By route*: use §D.12 for a primary-reading shelf, and the "How the Book Is Meant to Be Used" table for the order in which to read the chapters themselves.

**Conventions.** Dates are approximate for antiquity and given as *b.*/*d.* where only one is known. "c." marks genuine uncertainty, not vagueness about the century. Figures are placed by the period of their principal work, not their birth. A thinker who straddles two eras appears in the earlier one only, with a cross-reference. The canon represented here is the canon this book actually argues with; §D.13 says something about what that selection does and does not claim.

## D.1 — The Shape of the Map: Six Shifts

Before the tables, the spine. The history of philosophy as this book uses it turns on six transitions, each of which created a problem that the next period inherited.

**1. The invention of the argument (c. 6th–4th c. BCE).** Thales and the Milesians replace mythic with naturalistic explanation; Parmenides and Zeno show that reasoning can produce conclusions no one wants; Plato invents the systematic dialogue and the theory of Forms; Aristotle invents formal logic and the research programme. Everything after is a response to the standards set here. (*Ch. 1, 2*)

**2. The turn inward (c. 4th c. BCE – 5th c. CE).** Hellenistic schools make philosophy a therapy for living; Scepticism turns the criterion against itself; Plotinus and then Augustine relocate the fundamental questions to the interior — the will, the self, time as the mind's distension. The Buddhist and Jain traditions conduct an independent, equally technical version of this turn, with a more developed epistemology and a more radical metaphysics of the self. (*Ch. 6, 13, 17, 26*)

**3. The medieval synthesis and its fracturing (c. 9th–14th c.).** Islamic, Jewish, and Latin scholasticism integrate Aristotle with revelation and produce the technical apparatus — essence and existence, modality, univocity, intentionality — that early modernity inherits. Ockham's nominalism and Scotus's univocity break the synthesis from within. (*Ch. 15, 16, 50*)

**4. The epistemological turn (17th–18th c.).** Descartes makes the first-person standpoint foundational; the empiricist–rationalist dispute structures two centuries; Kant responds with the Copernican turn, making the conditions of experience the primary topic. (*Ch. 5–9, 20, 36*)

**5. The linguistic turn (late 19th–mid 20th c.).** Frege, Russell, and Wittgenstein make language the royal road to philosophical problems; logical analysis, ordinary-language analysis, and logical positivism are three answers to the same question about what philosophy should be. (*Ch. 31–37, 52*)

**6. The naturalistic and pluralistic turn (mid 20th c.–present).** Quine, Sellars, and Kuhn dissolve the analytic-synthetic distinction and the autonomy of philosophy; cognitive science gives philosophy of mind an empirical partner; the canon itself is contested and widened; applied ethics becomes a serious field. The discipline is now plural in method and increasingly global in sources. (*Ch. 21–30, 39–43, 51–54*)

## D.13 — Dating, Attribution, and Canon Cautions

Three warnings, in descending order of importance.

**Attribution is harder than the tables suggest.** The Socrates of the early dialogues is probably close to the historical Socrates; the Socrates of the *Republic* is probably Plato's mouthpiece. The *Laozi* is a composite text of uncertain date and multiple hands. The *Analects* is a stratified compilation. Nāgārjuna's authorship of the *Vigrahavyāvartanī* is secure; that of several attributed works is not. Kripke's rule-following argument is presented *as* Wittgenstein's, and the exegetical question is open. Where the book attributes a thesis to a figure, it means "the view standardly associated with the name in the secondary literature," not a claim about what the historical person thought. When the distinction matters, the text says so.

**"Central thesis" is a compression that sometimes falsifies.** Hume's philosophy is not "causation is constant conjunction" — that is one premise in a much larger naturalistic project. Spinoza's is not "everything is one" — the *conatus* doctrine and the geometrical method do at least as much work. The one-liners are indexes, and an index is not a summary. The same warning applies to the by-problem tables, which necessarily sort thinkers into camps they would often have rejected.

**The canon represented here is this book's argumentative canon, not a claim about the whole of philosophy.** The selection is weighted toward what the book's chapters actually use. That produces three visible biases: (i) the analytic tradition dominates the twentieth century because the book's method is analytic; (ii) non-Western traditions appear as systematic positions rather than as historical contexts, which is a deliberate choice and also a limitation; (iii) some fields — aesthetics, philosophy of law, philosophy of history, non-Western medieval logic — are represented thinly relative to their importance. A reader who wants the fuller history should treat this appendix as a set of entry points, not a survey. The optional threads in each part name the alternatives that the main text could not accommodate.

**A note on the living.** Figures marked as contemporary are included for the influence of their work, not for the finality of their views. Where a position is still in motion — conceptual engineering, grounding, illusionism, the epistemology of AI — the entry names the position and the chapter, and leaves the verdict to the reader.
