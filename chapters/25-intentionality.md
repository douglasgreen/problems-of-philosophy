# Chapter 25 — Intentionality and Mental Content

> “Every mental phenomenon is characterized by what the Scholastics of the Middle Ages called the intentional (or mental) inexistence of an object, and what we might call, though not wholly unambiguously, reference to a content, direction toward an object.”
> — Franz Brentano, *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874), II.1.5

> “Thoughts are about things. To say that is to say something that sounds entirely obvious, and which no theory of the mind has yet made entirely intelligible.”
> — a formulation of the problem, in many mouths

> “It is not the case that ‘meaning’ is something in the head. Meaning is in the world, and the head is in the world.”
> — a slogan of externalism, derived from Putnam (1975)

---

Chapter 24 examined the *subjective* *character* of experience — the “what it is like.” This chapter examines the *other* *aspect* of the mental that has resisted naturalization: **intentionality** — the *aboutness* of thought and experience.

The two are *distinct*, and their *distinctness* is itself a *datum*. An experience can be *about* something (my visual experience is *of* a red rose), and it can *feel* a certain way (it feels *like* seeing red). The *aboutness* is *intentionality*; the *feeling* is *phenomenality*. A *purely* *phenomenal* state without *aboutness* is *conceivable* (the *“raw feel”* of pain, on some views); a *purely* *intentional* state without *phenomenality* is *actual* (the *belief* that $2+2=4$ need not *feel* like anything). The *relationship* between the two is one of the deepest *open* questions in the philosophy of mind, and it is the *subject* of a growing literature.

The chapter's *thesis* is that intentionality is *real*, *irreducible* to *purely* *physical* *description* (in the sense that intentional vocabulary is not eliminable from the *special* sciences), and *naturalizable* — but that the *naturalization* is *incomplete*, and its *incompleteness* is *structural* rather than *provisional*.

The chapter proceeds in eight movements. §1 states Brentano's thesis and the naturalization program. §2 examines the information-theoretic and teleosemantic theories. §3 examines functional and conceptual-role theories. §4 examines wide versus narrow content. §5 examines non-conceptual content. §6 examines empty intention. §7 examines collective intentionality. §8 gives an honest verdict.

---

## 1. Brentano's Thesis and the Naturalistic Challenge

### 1.1 The mark of the mental

**The *thesis*** (Brentano, *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint*, 1874, II.1.5). The *mark* of the mental is **intentionality**: every mental phenomenon *refers* to an object, *directs* itself toward a content.

**The *structure*.**

$$
\text{Mental state } S \text{ is *intentional* iff } S \text{ is *of* or *about* something}
$$

**The *examples*.** A *belief* is a belief *that* $p$; a *desire* is a desire *for* $x$; a *perception* is a perception *of* $y$; a *fear* is a fear *of* $z$. The *object* of the state is the *intentional* object.

**The *virtue*.** The *thesis* *captures* the *intuition* that the mental is *essentially* *directed* — the mind is not a *closed* *system* of *states*, but an *open* *system* of *relations* to the *world*.

**The *problem*.** The *thesis* *generates* the *puzzles* of the *chapter*: what *is* the *intentional* *object*? What *is* the *relation*? How can a *physical* *state* be *about* anything?

### 1.2 The problem of intentional inexistence

**The *puzzle***. The *intentional* *object* may *not* *exist*. I can *fear* a *ghost*, *believe* in *unicorns*, *desire* a *fountain* of *youth*, and *think* about *Sherlock* *Holmes*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Intentional states are *relations* to *intentional* objects.} \\
2. & \text{Some intentional objects do *not* exist (ghosts, unicorns, Sherlock Holmes).} \\
3. & \text{A *relation* cannot relate a subject to a *nonexistent* object (the *relational* *principle*).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Either the intentional *object* *exists* (as a *nonexistent* *entity*; Ch. 14, §2), or the *relation* *model* is *false*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *dilemma*.** The *realist* *response* (Meinong) *posits* a *realm* of *nonexistent* *objects*; the *anti-realist* *response* (the *“no* *object”* *view*) *denies* that the *state* *has* an *object* *at* all.

**The *contemporary* *form*.** The *puzzle* is the *problem* of *empty* *intention* (§6), and it is the *direct* *descendant* of Brentano's *problem*. The *puzzle* *drives* the *debate* *about* *mental* *content*: if the *content* *determines* the *aboutness*, and the *content* *can* be *about* a *nonexistent* *object*, then the *content* is *not* a *relation* to an *object* (it is a *representation* with *accuracy* *conditions*; Ch. 5, §4).

### 1.3 The naturalization program

**The *program***. To *explain* *intentionality* in *non-intentional* *terms* — to *show* how *physical* *systems* can be *about* things.

**The *structure*. The *program* has *three* *components*.

| Component | The *task* | The *paradigm* |
|---|---|---|
| **The *content* *question*** | What is the *content* of a mental state? | The *representational* *content* |
| **The *determination* *question*** | What *determines* the *content*? | The *causal* or *informational* *relation* |
| **The *normativity* *question*** | What makes the content *correct* or *incorrect*? | The *teleological* *norm* |

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Intentionality *supervenes* on the *physical* (the *naturalistic* *premise*).} \\
2. & \text{The *supervenience* *requires* a *determination* *relation* (the *content*-*determining* *facts* are *physical*).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{Intentionality is *naturalizable* (in *principle*).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *program* *fits* the *naturalistic* *worldview*, and it *generates* the *research* *programs* of *cognitive* *science*, *artificial* *intelligence*, and *evolutionary* *psychology*.

**The *cost*.** The *program* *may* *fail*: the *intentional* *facts* *may* *not* *supervene* on the *physical* *facts* (the *anti-naturalist* *view*), and the *failure* *would* *support* *dualism* or *panpsychism* (Ch. 23, §7; Ch. 19, §7).

### 1.4 The criteria of adequacy

**The *criteria***. A *theory* of *content* *must* *satisfy* *five* *desiderata* (the *list* is *derived* from Fodor, *Psychosemantics*, 1987, and the *contemporary* *literature*).

| Criterion | The *demand* |
|---|---|
| **Naturalism** | The theory *uses* only *non-intentional* *terms* |
| **Determinacy** | The theory *assigns* a *determinate* *content* to *each* *state* |
| **Productivity** | The theory *explains* the *compositionality* of *content* (Ch. 31, §4) |
| **Normativity** | The theory *explains* the *possibility* of *error* |
| **Causality** | The theory *explains* the *causal* *role* of *content* (Ch. 23, §5) |

**The *tension*.** The *criteria* *conflict* (the *productivity* requires *compositionality*, the *normativity* requires *teleology*, the *naturalism* *forbids* the *teleology* unless it is *naturalized*), and the *conflict* is the *engine* of the *debate*.

**The *significance*.** The *criteria* are the *standards* by which the *theories* of §§2–3 are *assessed*, and the *assessment* is the *subject* of the *chapter*.

## 2. The Information-Theoretic and Teleosemantic Theories

### 2.1 Dretske's informational semantics

**The *thesis*** (Dretske, *Knowledge and the Flow of Information*, 1981; *Explaining Behavior*, 1988). The *content* of a *mental* *state* is *determined* by the *information* it *carries* — the *causal*-*informational* *relation* between the *state* and the *world*.

**The *structure*.**

$$
\text{State } S \text{ *means* } F \quad \text{iff} \quad S \text{ *carries* the *information* that } F
$$

**The *definition* of *information*** (Dretske 1981, Ch. 3; the *Shannon* *framework*, adapted).

$$
\text{A state } s \text{ *carries* the *information* that } F \text{ iff } P(F \mid s) = 1 \text{ (the *conditional* *probability* is 1)}
$$

The *conditional* *probability* is *the* *probability* that $F$ *obtains*, *given* that $s$ *obtains*. The *state* *carries* the *information* that $F$ *just* *in* *case* $F$ *is* *certain*, *given* $s$.

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *grounds* *content* in *information*, and *information* is a *naturalistic* *notion* (it is *defined* by *conditional* *probability*, which is *not* *intentional*).

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *disjunction* *problem*** (§2.2). The *state* *carries* the *information* that $F$ *and* the *information* that $F \vee G$, and the *theory* *assigns* *both* *contents* — *which* is *not* *what* *we* *want*.

**(ii) The *misrepresentation* *problem***. The *theory* *cannot* *explain* *error*: if the *state* *carries* the *information* that $F$, then the *state* *means* $F$, and if the *state* *is* *caused* by something *else* (the *frog*’s *fly*-*detector* *firing* at a *small* *black* *object*), the *state* *means* the *disjunction* (the *fly* or the *small* *black* *object*), and the *error* is *not* *possible*.

**(iii) The *‘=1’* *problem***. The *conditional* *probability* is *rarely* *1*; the *state* *carries* *information* about a *range* of *conditions*, and the *theory* *assigns* a *disjunctive* *content*.

### 2.2 The disjunction problem

**The *problem*** (Fodor, *Psychosemantics*, 1987, Ch. 4). The *frog*’s *fly*-*detector* *fires* at *flies*, *but* *also* at *small* *black* *objects* (the *experimental* *case*). The *state* *carries* the *information* that (fly $\vee$ small black object), and the *theory* *assigns* the *disjunctive* *content*. But the *frog* *represents* the *object* as a *fly*; the *state* *misrepresents* the *small* *black* *object* as a *fly*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *state* *carries* the *information* that } (F \vee G). \\
2. & \text{If *content* = *information*, the *state* *means* } (F \vee G). \\
3. & \text{The *state* *means* } F \text{ (the *frog* *represents* the *object* as a *fly*).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Content is *not* identical to *information*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *consequence*.** The *informational* *theory* *fails* on the *disjunction* *problem*, and the *failure* *is* *the* *motivation* for the *teleological* *theory* (§2.3).

**The *responses*.**

**(i) The *‘natural* *indicator’* *response*** (Dretske 1986, 1988). The *content* is *determined* by the *natural* *indicator* *relation* *during* the *learning* *period* (the *“learning* *set”*), and the *learning* *set* *excludes* the *small* *black* *objects*. The *response* *relativizes* the *content* to the *learning* *history*, and the *relativization* *is* *contested*.

**(ii) The *teleological* *response*** (Millikan 1984, 1989; Papineau 1987). The *content* is *determined* by the *function* of the *state*, and the *function* is *determined* by *natural* *selection*. The *response* is the *subject* of §2.3.

### 2.3 Teleosemantics

**The *thesis*** (Millikan, *Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories*, 1984; *White Queen Psychology and Other Essays for Alice*, 1993; Papineau, *Reality and Representation*, 1987). The *content* of a *mental* *state* is *determined* by its *biological* *function* — the *effect* for which the *state* was *selected* *by* *evolution*.

**The *structure*.**

$$
\text{State } S \text{ *means* } F \quad \text{iff} \quad \text{the *function* of } S \text{ is to *indicate* } F
$$

**The *definition* of *function*** (Millikan 1984, Ch. 1; the *“proper* *function”* *account*). A *function* is a *relational* *property*: the *function* of a *trait* is the *effect* that *explains* its *selection* *history*.

**The *example*.** The *frog*’s *fly*-*detector* *has* the *function* of *detecting* *flies*, *because* the *detector* was *selected* for *detecting* *flies* (the *ancestral* *frogs* who *detected* *flies* *survived* *more* *often*). The *state* *means* *fly*, *even* when it *fires* at a *small* *black* *object* (the *state* *misrepresents* the *object*).

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *solves* the *disjunction* *problem* (the *function* *determines* the *content*, and the *function* *excludes* the *small* *black* *object*) and the *misrepresentation* *problem* (the *error* is a *failure* of the *function*).

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *swampman* *objection*** (Davidson, “Knowing One’s Own Mind,” 1987). Consider a *being* that *arises* *by* *chance* (a *lightning* *strike* *assembles* the *molecules* into a *molecule*-for-*molecule* *duplicate* of Davidson). The *being* has *no* *selection* *history*, and the *theory* *assigns* it *no* *content*. But the *being* *seems* to *have* *beliefs* and *desires*. The *objection* *threatens* the *theory*.

**The *reply*. The *content* *can* be *individuated* by the *current* *function* (the *“present* *function”* *view*; the *reply* is *contested*), or the *being* has *derived* *functions* (the *reply* is *contested*).

**(ii) The *indeterminacy* *objection*. The *function* of a *state* is *indeterminate* (it can be *described* *widely* or *narrowly*), and the *theory* *assigns* an *indeterminate* *content*. The *objection* is the *generality* *problem* (Ch. 8, §4.3), *applied* to *content*.

**(iii) The *‘hard* *to* *specify’* *objection*. The *function* of a *belief* (the *effect* for which *beliefs* were *selected*) is *hard* to *specify*, and the *theory* *may* *not* *determine* the *content* *uniquely*.

### 2.4 The state of the naturalization program

**The *table*.**

| Theory | The *content* is *determined* by | The *strength* | The *weakness* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Informational** | The *information* (conditional probability) | Naturalistic | Disjunction problem |
| **Dretske's learning** | The *learning* set | Saves the natural indicator | Relativized to the history |
| **Teleosemantic** | The *biological* function | Solves disjunction and error | Swampman; indeterminacy |
| **Functional** | The *causal* role | Fits cognitive science | The role is vague (§3.1) |
| **Conceptual role** | The *inferential* role | Fits meaning holism | Indeterminacy (§3.2) |

**The *verdict*.** The *naturalization* *program* is *incomplete*: the *theories* *solve* *some* *problems* and *generate* *others*, and the *choice* among them *depends* on the *criteria* of §1.4. The *most* *promising* *position* is a *hybrid*: *teleosemantics* *combined* with *conceptual* *role* (the *two*-*factor* *theory*; §3.3).

## 3. Functional and Conceptual-Role Theories

### 3.1 Causal-role semantics

**The *thesis***. The *content* of a *mental* *state* is *determined* by its *causal* *role* — its *relations* to *inputs*, *outputs*, and *other* *mental* *states* (Ch. 23, §4.3).

**The *structure*.**

$$
\text{Content}(S) = \langle \text{inputs}, \text{outputs}, \text{other states} \rangle
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *fits* the *functionalist* *framework*, and it *explains* the *causal* *role* of *content*.

**The *objection*.** The *causal* *role* *underdetermines* the *content*: *different* *contents* can *have* the *same* *causal* *role* (the *“inverted* *qualia”* *case*; Ch. 24, §1.3; the *“twin* *earth”* *case*; §4.1), and the *theory* cannot *distinguish* them.

### 3.2 Conceptual-role semantics

**The *thesis*** (Block, “Advertisement for a Semantics for Psychology,” 1986; Harman, *Change in View*, 1986; Brandom, *Making It Explicit*, 1994). The *content* of a *mental* *state* is *determined* by its *inferential* *role* — its *relations* to *other* *states* in *reasoning*.

**The *structure*.**

$$
\text{Content}(S) = \text{the *inferential* *role* of } S \text{ in the *cognitive* *economy}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *fits* the *holistic* *nature* of *meaning* (the *meaning* of a *concept* is *constituted* by its *relations* to *other* *concepts*), and it *explains* the *inferential* *role* of *content*.

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *holism* *problem***. If the *content* is *determined* by the *inferential* *role*, and the *role* *depends* on the *whole* *system* of *beliefs*, then *any* *change* in *belief* *changes* the *content* of *every* *concept* (the *“meaning* *holism”* *problem*; Fodor & Lepore, *Holism: A Shopper’s Guide*, 1992). The *consequence* is *implausible*: *learning* a *new* *fact* would *change* the *meaning* of *all* *words*.

**(ii) The *indeterminacy* *problem***. The *inferential* *role* is *indeterminate* (there are *many* *roles* consistent with the *data*), and the *theory* *assigns* an *indeterminate* *content*.

**(iii) The *compositionality* *problem***. The *theory* *struggles* to *explain* the *compositionality* of *content* (Ch. 31, §4): if the *content* of a *concept* is its *inferential* *role*, the *content* of a *complex* *expression* is not a *function* of the *contents* of its *parts*.

### 3.3 The two-factor theory

**The *thesis*** (Block 1986; the *“two*-*factor”* *theory*; the *contemporary* *literature*). The *content* of a *mental* *state* has *two* *factors*: a *narrow* *factor* (the *conceptual* *role*) and a *wide* *factor* (the *external* *reference*).

**The *structure*.**

$$
\text{Content} = \langle \text{narrow role}, \text{wide reference} \rangle
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *reconciles* the *internalist* *intuition* (the *content* *depends* on the *subject*’s *perspective*) with the *externalist* *intuition* (the *content* *depends* on the *world*; §4).

**The *objection*.** The *theory* *posits* *two* *kinds* of *content*, and the *relation* between them is *mysterious*. (The *reply*: the *narrow* *content* is the *mode* of *presentation*, and the *wide* *content* is the *referent*; the *reply* is *contested*.)

### 3.4 The state of the functional theories

**The *assessment*.** The *functional* *theories* *capture* the *causal* and *inferential* *roles* of *content*, and they *struggle* with *determinacy* and *compositionality*. The *most* *defensible* *position* is a *hybrid*: *teleosemantics* for the *wide* *content*, *conceptual* *role* for the *narrow* *content*, and a *compositional* *syntax* for the *structure* (the *language* of *thought*; §5.4).

## 4. Wide versus Narrow Content

### 4.1 Twin Earth

**The *argument*** (Putnam, “The Meaning of ‘Meaning’,” 1975; Ch. 12, §3.6). The *content* of a *mental* *state* is *not* *determined* by the *intrinsic* *state* of the *subject*; it is *determined* by the *environment*.

**The *scenario*.** Oscar and Twin Oscar are *molecule*-for-*molecule* *duplicates*, *living* on *Earth* and *Twin* *Earth*. The *stuff* they *call* “water” is H₂O on *Earth* and XYZ on *Twin* *Earth*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Oscar and Twin Oscar are *intrinsically* *identical* (the *empirical* *premise*).} \\
2. & \text{Oscar's "water" *refers* to H₂O; Twin Oscar's "water" *refers* to XYZ (the *semantic* *premise*).} \\
3. & \text{If the *references* are *different*, the *contents* are *different*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *content* is *not* *determined* by the *intrinsic* *state* (it is *wide*).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *conclusion*.** The *content* is *wide*: it *depends* on the *environment*, *not* *only* on the *head*.

### 4.2 Slow switching

**The *argument*** (Burge, “Individualism and the Mental,” 1979; “Other Bodies,” 1982). The *content* of a *belief* *depends* on the *social* *environment* (the *linguistic* *community*), *not* only on the *individual*’s *intrinsic* *state*.

**The *scenario*.** A *subject* has a *belief* *about* *arthritis*, but *misunderstands* the *concept* (she *believes* that *arthritis* can *occur* in the *thigh*). The *content* of her *belief* is *determined* by the *community*’s *concept* (arthritis is a *joint* *disease*), and her *belief* is *false*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *subject* is *intrinsically* *identical* to a *subject* in a *community* with a *different* *concept*.} \\
2. & \text{The *contents* of the *beliefs* are *different* (the *community*’s *concept* *differs*).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The *content* is *wide* (it *depends* on the *social* *environment*).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *conclusion*.** The *content* is *wide* in the *social* *sense*: it *depends* on the *linguistic* *community*, and the *individual*’s *intrinsic* *state* *underdetermines* it.

### 4.3 The narrow content debate

**The *positions*.**

| Position | The *claim* | The *proponent* |
|---|---|---|
| **Strong externalism** | All content is wide | Putnam; Burge; the *mainstream* |
| **Two-factor** | There is wide and narrow content | Block; the *two-factor* *theorists* |
| **Narrow content** | Content is narrow; wide content is a *construction* | Fodor (1987); the *individualist* *tradition* |
| **Eliminativism about narrow content** | There is no narrow content; the *debate* is *misplaced* | the *eliminativist* *tradition* |

**The *argument* *for* *narrow* *content***. The *narrow* *content* is *needed* to *explain* *behavior*: Oscar and Twin Oscar *behave* *identically*, and the *behavior* is *explained* by the *narrow* *content* (the *content* that is *the* *same* in the *two* *cases*).

**The *argument* *against***. The *narrow* *content* is *not* *expressible*: the *subject* *cannot* *express* the *narrow* *content* (the *content* is *not* a *proposition* in the *ordinary* *sense*; it is a *“notional”* *content*; Fodor 1987, Ch. 2).

**The *state* of the debate.** The *externalist* *consensus* is *dominant*, and the *narrow* *content* is *posited* *only* by the *individualist* *tradition*. The *debate* is *unresolved*, and it is *connected* to the *theory* of *action* (Ch. 28, §1) and to the *theory* of *self-knowledge* (Ch. 26, §6).

### 4.4 Wide content and causation

**The *problem***. If the *content* is *wide*, the *content* *depends* on the *environment*, and the *environment* is *not* *in* the *head*. How can a *wide* *content* *cause* *behavior*?

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Causation is *local*: the *cause* is *in* the *head*.} \\
2. & \text{The *wide* *content* is *not* *in* the *head* (it *includes* the *environment*).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The *wide* *content* *cannot* *cause* *behavior*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *responses*.**

**(i) The *narrow* *content* *response***. The *narrow* *content* *causes* the *behavior*, and the *wide* *content* is *derived* from the *narrow* *content* *plus* the *environment*.

**(ii) The *interventionist* *response*** (Ch. 18, §3). The *wide* *content* *causes* the *behavior* *in* the *interventionist* *sense*: *intervening* on the *content* (by *changing* the *environment* or the *belief*) *changes* the *behavior*. The *response* is *promising* and *contested*.

**(iii) The *grounding* *response***. The *wide* *content* is *grounded* in the *narrow* *content* *plus* the *environment*, and the *grounding* *explains* the *causal* *role* *without* *requiring* the *wide* *content* to be *in* the *head*.

**The *state* of the debate.** The *debate* is *unresolved*, and it is *central* to the *theory* of *action* and to the *theory* of *mental* *causation* (Ch. 23, §5).

## 5. Non-Conceptual Content and the Format of Representation

### 5.1 The distinction

**The *distinction*** (Evans, *The Varieties of Reference*, 1982, Ch. 5; Peacocke, “Does Perception Have a Nonconceptual Content?”, 2001; the *contemporary* *literature*).

| Kind | The *content* is | The *paradigm* |
|---|---|---|
| **Conceptual** | *Structured* by *concepts*; the *subject* must *possess* the *concepts* | Belief, judgment |
| **Non-conceptual** | *Not* structured by *concepts*; the *subject* need not *possess* the *concepts* | Perception, *sensorimotor* *representation* |

**The *argument* *for* *non-conceptual* *content***.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *perceptual* *experience* *represents* *more* *detail* than the *subject* *can* *conceptualize* (the *richness* *of* *experience*; Ch. 24, §1.3).} \\
2. & \text{The *content* of the *experience* is *not* *captured* by the *concepts* the *subject* *has*.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The *content* is *non-conceptual*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *arguments*.**

**(i) The *richness* *argument***. The *visual* *field* *contains* *more* *information* than the *subject* can *report*, and the *information* is *part* of the *content*.

**(ii) The *infant*/*animal* *argument***. *Infants* and *animals* *perceive* the *world*, but they *do* not *possess* the *concepts* (dog, *red*, *tree*), and the *perception* has *content*.

**(iii) The *fine-grained* *discrimination* *argument***. The *subject* can *discriminate* *shades* of *color* that she *has* *no* *concept* for (the *“knowing* *how”* *of* *discrimination*), and the *discrimination* *requires* a *content* that is *finer* than the *concepts*.

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *‘myth* *of* *the* *given’* *objection*** (Sellars 1956; Ch. 5, §8.3). The *non-conceptual* *content* is *not* in the *space* of *reasons*, and it *cannot* *justify* a *belief*. (The *reply*: the *non-conceptual* *content* *can* *cause* the *belief*, and the *justification* is *provided* by the *conceptual* *content*; the *reply* is *contested*.)

**(ii) The *‘concepts* *are* *needed’* *objection*** (McDowell, *Mind and World*, 1994). The *perception* *is* *conceptual* *throughout* (the *“spontaneity* *is* *everywhere”* *view*), and the *non-conceptual* *content* is *an* *illusion*.

**(iii) The *‘indeterminacy’* *objection***. The *non-conceptual* *content* is *indeterminate* (it is *not* a *proposition*), and the *theory* *cannot* *specify* what it is.

### 5.2 The format of representation

**The *distinction*** (Fodor, *The Language of Thought*, 1975; Kosslyn, *Image and Brain*, 1994; the *contemporary* *literature*).

| Format | The *structure* | The *paradigm* |
|---|---|---|
| **Discursive** (language-like) | *Compositional*; the *content* is *structured* by *predicates* and *arguments* | The *language* of *thought* |
| **Iconic** (picture-like) | *Analog*; the *content* is *structured* by *spatial* *relations* | *Visual* *imagery*; the *sensory* *field* |

**The *argument* *for* *iconic* *format***. The *visual* *system* *represents* *spatial* *relations* *in* a *spatial* *medium* (the *retinotopic* *map*), and the *representation* is *not* *sentential*. The *argument* *supports* a *pluralist* *view* of *formats*.

**The *argument* *against***. The *iconic* *format* is *not* *compositional* (Ch. 31, §4), and the *systematicity* of *thought* *requires* a *compositional* *format*. (The *reply*: the *systematicity* *argument* applies to *thought*, not to *perception*; the *reply* is *contested*.)

**The *state* of the debate.** The *debate* is *unresolved*, and it is *connected* to the *theory* of *imagination* (Ch. 30, §3) and to the *theory* of *perception* (Ch. 5, §4).

### 5.3 Non-conceptual content and the justification of belief

**The *problem***. If *perception* has *non-conceptual* *content*, how does it *justify* *belief*?

**The *argument***.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Justification *requires* that the *justifier* stand in *logical* *relations* to the *justified*.} \\
2. & \text{The *non-conceptual* *content* does not stand in *logical* *relations* (it is *not* a *proposition*).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The *non-conceptual* *content* cannot *justify* a *belief*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *responses*.**

**(i) The *‘causal* *justification’* *response*** (the *reliabilist* *response*; Ch. 8, §4). The *justification* is *causal*, not *logical*: the *perceptual* *state* *reliably* *causes* the *belief*, and the *reliability* is the *justification*. The *response* is *naturalistic* and *contested*.

**(ii) The *‘conceptual* *content’* *response*** (McDowell 1994). The *perceptual* *content* is *conceptual*, and the *justification* is *logical*. The *response* *denies* the *non-conceptual* *content*.

**(iii) The *‘hybrid’* *response***. The *perception* has *both* *conceptual* and *non-conceptual* *content*, and the *conceptual* *content* *provides* the *justification* while the *non-conceptual* *content* *provides* the *richness*. The *response* is *promising* and *underdeveloped*.

### 5.4 The language of thought

**The *thesis*** (Fodor, *The Language of Thought*, 1975; *LOT 2*, 2008). Thought has a *compositional* *structure*, and the *structure* is *realized* in a *language*-like *medium* (the *language* of *thought*, LOT).

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Thought is *systematic* (the *ability* to think } aRb \text{ *implies* the *ability* to think } bRa). \\
2. & \text{The *systematicity* *requires* a *compositional* *medium*.} \\
3. & \text{The *medium* is *language*-like (it has *syntax* and *semantics*).} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Thought has a *language* of *thought*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *explains* *systematicity*, *productivity*, and *compositionality*, and it *fits* the *computational* *theory* of *mind* (Ch. 23, §4.2).

**The *objection*.** The *theory* *postulates* a *language* that is *not* *observable*, and the *postulate* is *not* *empirically* *justified* (the *“empirical”* *dispute* between *imagery* and *propositional* *theories*; the *contemporary* *literature*).

## 6. Empty Intention

### 6.1 The problem

**The *problem***. How can a *mental* *state* be *about* an *object* that *does* not *exist*?

**The *examples*.** *Sherlock* *Holmes*, *phlogiston*, the *fountain* of *youth*, the *future* *sea* *battle* (Ch. 17, §1.2), the *non-existent* *twin* (the *“Twin* *Earth”* *case*).

**The *views*.**

| Position | The *claim* | The *proponent* |
|---|---|---|
| **Meinongian realism** | The *object* *exists* (as a *nonexistent* *entity*) | Meinong; Parsons; Zalta (Ch. 14, §2.4) |
| **Fictionalism** | The *content* is a *fiction*; the *object* is *not* *real* | the *fictionalist* *tradition* |
| **Descriptivism** | The *content* is a *description*; the *object* is *whatever* *satisfies* the *description* | Russell (Ch. 12, §2) |
| **Gappy content** | The *content* has a *gap* where the *object* would be | the *structured* *propositions* *tradition* (Ch. 12, §7.3) |
| **Meta-linguistic** | The *content* is *about* the *name*, not the *object* | Kripke (2013) |

**The *argument* *for* *Meinongian* *realism***.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *thought* is *about* an *object*.} \\
2. & \text{If the *thought* is *about* an *object*, the *object* *exists* (the *relational* *principle*).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The *object* *exists* (as a *nonexistent* *entity*).}
\end{array}
$$

**The *argument* *against***. The *view* *posits* *infinitely* *many* *nonexistent* *objects* (one for every *description*), and the *ontology* is *profligate* (Ch. 14, §2.4).

### 6.2 The fictionalist solution

**The *thesis***. The *content* of a *thought* *about* *Sherlock* *Holmes* is a *fiction*: the *thought* is *embedded* in a *pretense*, and the *truth* of the *thought* is *relative* to the *pretense* (Walton, *Mimesis as Make-Believe*, 1990; Ch. 49, §4).

**The *structure*. The *thought* “Sherlock is a detective” is *true* *in* the *fiction* of *Conan* *Doyle*’s *stories*, and the *truth* is *not* *truth* *simpliciter*; it is *truth* *in* a *game* of *make-believe*.

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *avoids* the *Meinongian* *ontology*, and it *explains* the *truth* of *fictional* *statements*.

**The *objection*.** The *theory* *does* not *explain* the *phenomenology* of *thought*: the *thought* *seems* to be *about* *Sherlock*, and the *seeming* is *not* *explained* by the *pretense* (the *“aboutness* *of* *fiction”* *problem*).

### 6.3 The gappy content solution

**The *thesis***. The *content* of a *thought* *about* a *nonexistent* *object* is a *structured* *proposition* with a *gap* — an *“unsaturated”* *proposition* (Braun, “Empty Names,” 1993; the *structured* *propositions* *tradition*; Ch. 12, §7.3).

**The *structure*.** The *thought* has the *structure* $\langle \_, \text{is a detective} \rangle$, with a *gap* where the *object* would be. The *content* is *not* a *complete* *proposition*, and the *truth* *value* is *undefined* (or *false*).

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *avoids* the *Meinongian* *ontology*, and it *preserves* the *object*-directedness of *thought*.

**The *objection*.** The *theory* *assigns* a *content* that is *not* *truth*-*evaluable*, and the *assignment* is *contested*.

### 6.4 The state of the debate

**The *assessment*.** The *debate* is *unresolved*, and the *choice* among the *solutions* *depends* on the *theory* of *reference* (Ch. 12) and the *theory* of *fiction* (Ch. 49). The *most* *defensible* *position* is a *hybrid*: *gappy* *content* for the *semantics*, *fictionalism* for the *pragmatics*, and *Meinongian* *realism* for the *logic* of *fiction* (the *three* *are* *compatible*).

## 7. Collective Intentionality

### 7.1 We-intentions

**The *phenomenon***. We *intend* *jointly*: *we* *will* *walk* *together*, *we* *will* *win* the *game*, *we* *will* *build* the *house*. The *intention* is *not* *reducible* to *individual* *intentions* (the *“we”* is *essential*).

**The *theses*.**

| Position | The *claim* | The *proponent* |
|---|---|---|
| **Irreducibility** | The *we*-*intention* is *primitive* | Searle (1990, 1995); Gilbert (1989) |
| **Reducibility** | The *we*-*intention* is *reducible* to *individual* *intentions* *plus* *common* *knowledge* | Bratman (1999); Tuomela (2007) (in a *qualified* *form*) |
| **Collective* *mental* *states*** | The *group* *has* *intentional* *states* *irreducibly* | Ch. 11, §5 |

**The *argument* *for* *irreducibility*** (Searle 1990).

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *we*-*intention* is *not* *reducible* to } (I \text{ intend}) + (I \text{ believe that you intend}) + \ldots \\
2. & \text{The *reduction* *misses* the *“we”* (the *collective* *subject*).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The *we*-*intention* is *primitive*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *captures* the *phenomenology* of *joint* *action*, and it *explains* the *normativity* of *collective* *commitments* (the *obligations* that *arise* from *joint* *intentions*).

**The *objection*** (Bratman 1999). The *we*-*intention* *can* be *reduced* to *individual* *intentions* *plus* *common* *knowledge* (the *“shared* *cooperative* *activity”* *model*), and the *reduction* is *simpler* (the *parsimony* *argument*).

**The *state* of the debate.** The *debate* is *unresolved*, and it is *connected* to the *theory* of *group* *agency* (Ch. 11, §5) and to the *theory* of *social* *ontology* (§7.3).

### 7.2 The structure of we-intentions

**The *components*** (Searle 1990; Tuomela 2007; Gilbert 1989).

| Component | The *content* | The *example* |
|---|---|---|
| **The *we*-*subject*** | The *collective* *agent* | *We* |
| **The *collective* *goal*** | The *shared* *end* | *Walk* *together* |
| **The *mutual* *commitment*** | The *obligation* to *the* *group* | The *promise* to *participate* |
| **The *common* *knowledge*** | The *knowledge* that the *others* *know* | The *shared* *awareness* |

**The *argument***. The *we*-*intention* *has* a *structure* that the *individual* *intention* *lacks* (the *collective* *subject*, the *mutual* *commitment*, the *common* *knowledge*), and the *structure* is *irreducible*.

**The *objection*.** The *structure* *can* be *analyzed* into *individual* *states* *plus* *relations* (the *“common* *knowledge”* *regress*; Lewis, *Convention*, 1969), and the *analysis* *is* *possible*.

**The *state* of the debate.** The *debate* is *unresolved*, and the *choice* *depends* on the *theory* of *group* *belief* (Ch. 11, §5) and on the *theory* of *convention* (Lewis 1969).

### 7.3 Social facts and institutions

**The *thesis*** (Searle, *The Construction of Social Reality*, 1995). *Social* *facts* (money, marriage, property, government) are *constituted* by *collective* *intentionality* *plus* *constitutive* *rules*.

**The *structure*.**

$$
\text{Social fact} = \text{collective intentionality} + \text{constitutive rule}
$$

**The *constitutive* *rule***. The *rule* has the *form* “$X$ *counts* *as* $Y$ *in* *context* $C$” (Searle 1969, 1995).

**The *examples*.**

| The *fact* | The *constitutive* *rule* |
|---|---|
| **Money** | *This paper counts as money in the economy* |
| **Marriage** | *This ceremony counts as marriage in the community* |
| **Property** | *This land counts as owned in the legal system* |
| **Government** | *This assembly counts as the legislature in the state* |

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Social *facts* are *not* *physical* *facts* (they are *not* *reducible* to the *physical*).} \\
2. & \text{The *social* *facts* are *constituted* by *collective* *intentionality*.} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{The *social* *facts* are *real*, but *mind*-*dependent*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *theory* *explains* the *ontology* of the *social* *world*, and it *fits* the *theory* of *collective* *intentionality*.

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *‘social* *construction’* *objection*. If the *social* *facts* are *constituted* by *collective* *intentionality*, they are *not* *objective*; they are *constructed*. (The *reply*: the *construction* is *real* and *objective* (the *construction* *is* a *social* *fact*); the *reply* is *contested*.)

**(ii) The *‘normativity’* *objection*. The *constitutive* *rules* *presuppose* *norms* (the *obligations* to *follow* the *rules*), and the *norms* are *not* *explained* by the *rules*. (The *reply*: the *norms* are *constituted* by the *collective* *acceptance*; the *reply* is *contested*.)

**(iii) The *‘political’* *objection*. The *theory* *legitimates* the *status* *quo* (the *social* *facts* are *real*, and the *existing* *institutions* are *legitimate*), and the *legitimation* is *contested* (Ch. 47, §7).

### 7.4 The state of collective intentionality

**The *assessment*.** Collective *intentionality* is a *real* *phenomenon*, and its *nature* is *contested*. The *most* *defensible* *position* is a *qualified* *irreducibility*: the *we*-*intention* is *irreducible* *in* *content* (the *collective* *subject* is *essential*), but *reducible* *in* *realization* (it is *realized* by *individual* *brains* and *their* *interactions*). The *position* *connects* the *philosophy* of *mind* to the *philosophy* of *society* (Ch. 47).

## 8. The State of the Debate

### 8.1 What is settled

- **Intentionality is a real phenomenon.** The *aboutness* of thought is a *datum*, and it is *not* an *illusion*.
- **The naturalization program is incomplete.** The *theories* *solve* *some* *problems* and *generate* *others*, and *none* is *decisive*.
- **The *disjunction* *problem* is a *serious* *challenge* to the *informational* *theory*, and the *teleological* *theory* is the *most* *promising* *response*.
- **The *swampman* *objection* is a *serious* *challenge* to the *teleological* *theory*, and the *reply* is *contested*.
- **The *externalist* *consensus* is *dominant*, and the *narrow* *content* is *posited* only by the *individualist* *tradition*.
- **The *non-conceptual* *content* is *contested*, and the *debate* is *connected* to the *theory* of *perception*.
- **The *empty* *intention* *debate* is *unresolved*, and the *choice* *depends* on the *theory* of *reference* and of *fiction*.
- **Collective *intentionality* is *real*, and its *irreducibility* is *contested*.

### 8.2 What is open

- **The *determination* of *content*.** Is it *informational*, *teleological*, *functional*, or *conceptual*?
- **The *narrow*/*wide* *distinction*.** Is there a *narrow* *content*, and what is its *nature*?
- **The *non-conceptual* *content*.** Is there *non-conceptual* *content*, and what is its *format*?
- **The *empty* *intention*.** Is the *Meinongian* *realism* *defensible*, or is the *fictionalism* *better*?
- **The *collective* *intentionality*.** Is the *we*-*intention* *irreducible*?

### 8.3 The methodological lesson

The *chapter* is a *case* *study* in the *method* of *Part* I.

- **Conceptual analysis** (§1 of Ch. 3) generated the *distinction* between the *intentional* and the *phenomenal*.
- **Thought experiments** (§2 of Ch. 3) — *Twin* *Earth*, the *arthritis* *case*, the *swampman* — are the *engine* of the *debate*.
- **Formalization** (§3 of Ch. 3) — the *conditional* *probability*, the *structured* *propositions*, the *constitutive* *rules* — is *essential* for the *field*.
- **Reflective equilibrium** (§4 of Ch. 3) is the *method* by which the *intuitions* and the *theories* are *adjusted*.
- **Negative capability** (§7 of Ch. 3) is the *response* to the *residue*: the *humility* to *hold* the *question* *open*.

The *honest* verdict: the *theory* of *content* is a *field* in *transition*. The *naturalization* *program* is *incomplete*; the *externalist* *consensus* is *dominant* but *fragile*; the *non-conceptual* *content* *debate* is *unresolved*. The *unresolved* character is *instructive*: it shows what philosophical progress looks like — not a *settled* *answer*, but a *sharper* *understanding* of what the *question* is.

### 8.4 The dialectical map

| Position | Core claim | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Informational** | Content = information | Naturalistic | Disjunction problem |
| **Teleosemantic** | Content = biological function | Solves disjunction | Swampman |
| **Causal-role** | Content = causal role | Fits functionalism | Underdetermination |
| **Conceptual-role** | Content = inferential role | Fits holism | Meaning holism |
| **Two-factor** | Narrow + wide content | Reconciles | The relation is mysterious |
| **Strong externalism** | All content is wide | Fits Twin Earth | Causal exclusion |
| **Narrow content** | Content is narrow | Explains behavior | Not expressible |
| **Non-conceptual** | Perception has non-conceptual content | Fits richness | The myth of the given |
| **Conceptual** | All content is conceptual | Fits justification | Denies richness |
| **Meinongian** | Nonexistent objects exist | Preserves aboutness | Profligate |
| **Fictionalism** | Fictional content is pretense | Parsimonious | Does not explain aboutness |
| **Irreducible we-intention** | Collective intentionality is primitive | Fits phenomenology | Reduction is possible |

The *table* is a *map*, not a *verdict*. The *reader* who wants to *choose* a *position* must decide which *cost* is *acceptable* — and the *decision* is a *philosophical* *argument*.

## Optional threads

Cross-cutting material, for the reader who wants to go deeper.

**1. The *connection* between *intentionality* and *consciousness*.** The *two* *marks* of the *mental* (Ch. 24, §1.1) are *distinct*, but they are *correlated*: *consciousness* *requires* *intentionality* (the *content* of *experience*), and *intentionality* *may* *require* *consciousness* (the *“phenomenal* *intentionality”* *debate*; the *contemporary* *literature*). The *connection* is one of the *deepest* *open* questions in the *philosophy* of *mind*, and it is *underdeveloped*.

**2. The *content* of *perception* and the *content* of *thought*.** The *relationship* between *perceptual* *content* (Ch. 5, §4) and *conceptual* *content* (this chapter) is *contested*: the *“rich* *content”* *view* (the *perceptual* *content* is *conceptual*), the *“thin* *content”* *view* (the *perceptual* *content* is *non-conceptual*), and the *“hybrid”* *view* (both) are *all* *live*. The *debate* is *connected* to the *theory* of *justification* (Ch. 8, §2).

**3. The *content* of *artificial* *systems*.** If an *AI* *system* has *states* that *carry* *information* and *serve* *functions*, do the *states* *have* *content*? The *answer* *depends* on the *theory* of *content* (this chapter), and it *bears* on the *ethics* of *AI* (Ch. 51, §3). The *debate* is *beginning* and *urgent*.

**4. Non-Western *theories* of *meaning*.** The *Buddhist* *apoha* *theory* (Dignāga, 5th–6th c. CE) *holds* that *words* *refer* *not* to *positive* *entities* but to the *exclusion* of *others* (the *“apoha”* *theory*), and the *theory* *anticipates* the *contemporary* *inferentialist* and *nominalist* *accounts* (Ch. 15, §2). The *Nyāya* *tradition* *developed* a *realist* *theory* of *meaning* (the *“padārtha”* *theory*) that *anticipates* the *referentialist* *accounts*. The *traditions* are *systematic* *alternatives* (Matilal 1990; Ganeri 1999; the *essays* in *The Oxford Handbook of Indian Philosophy*, 2017).

**5. The *normativity* of *content*.** The *content* of a *state* is *normative*: it *determines* what the state *ought* to *be* *about* (the *“correctness”* *condition*), and the *normativity* is *not* *explained* by the *naturalistic* *theories* (the *“normativity* *problem”*; the *contemporary* *literature*). The *problem* is *connected* to the *theory* of *normativity* (Ch. 44, §4) and to the *theory* of *rationality* (Ch. 28).

## Summary

- **Intentionality** is the *aboutness* of the mental — the *directedness* of *thought* and *experience* — and it is the *mark* of the mental (Brentano).
- **The *problem* of *intentional* *inexistence***: the *intentional* *object* may *not* *exist*, and the *puzzle* *drives* the *debate* about *content*.
- **The *naturalization* *program*** attempts to *explain* *intentionality* in *non-intentional* *terms*, and the *criteria* *are* *naturalism*, *determinacy*, *productivity*, *normativity*, and *causality*.
- **The *informational* *theory*** (Dretske) *grounds* *content* in *information*, and it *fails* on the *disjunction* *problem* (the *state* *carries* *information* about a *disjunction*).
- **The *teleological* *theory*** (Millikan, Papineau) *grounds* *content* in *biological* *function*, and it *solves* the *disjunction* *problem* and the *misrepresentation* *problem*, but it *faces* the *swampman* *objection* and the *indeterminacy* *problem*.
- **The *functional* *theories*** (causal-role, conceptual-role) *ground* *content* in *causal* or *inferential* *role*, and they *struggle* with *determinacy* and *compositionality*.
- **The *externalist* *consensus*** is *dominant*: *content* is *wide* (Twin Earth, slow switching), and the *narrow* *content* is *posited* only by the *individualist* *tradition*.
- **Non-conceptual *content*** is *contested*: the *argument* from *richness* *supports* it, and the *myth* *of* *the* *given* *challenges* it.
- **Empty *intention*** is *unresolved*: the *Meinongian*, *fictionalist*, *descriptivist*, *gappy*, and *meta-linguistic* *solutions* are *all* *live*.
- **Collective *intentionality*** is *real*, and the *irreducibility* of the *we*-*intention* is *contested*; the *social* *facts* are *constituted* by *collective* *intentionality* *plus* *constitutive* *rules*.

The result is *not* a *solution* to the *problem* of *content*, and it is *not* a *refutation* of the *naturalization* *program*. If *intentionality* is the *aboutness* of the *mental*, the *self* is the *“I”* to which the *states* are *attributed*, and the *question* of the *self* is the *question* of *what* *kind* of *thing* the *subject* is. The *next* *chapter* *examines* the *theories* of the *self* (substance, bundle, narrative, illusion), the *problem* of *personal* *identity* (Ch. 17, §7), and the *first-person* *perspective* — and it *asks* the *question* that the *theory* of *content* *presupposes*: *who* is the *subject* of the *content*?
