# Chapter 13 — The Criterion Problem, Relativism, and the Scope of Objectivity

> “The problem of the criterion seems to me to be one of the most difficult of all philosophical problems.”
> — Roderick Chisholm, *The Problem of the Criterion* (1973), p. 1

> “Man is the measure of all things, of things that are that they are, and of things that are not that they are not.”
> — Protagoras, fragment 1 (DK 80B1), as reported by Plato and Sextus

> “The problem of the criterion is the problem of the *standards* by which we assess our *standards*. It is the problem of philosophy *as such* — and it is the problem that chapter is about.”
> — this book, in effect

---

Chapter 12 ended with a juxtaposition that needs to be made explicit. The *thought* is anchored in the *object* by a *chain* — causal, testimonial, social — that the *subject* does not fully control. The *inquiry* is anchored in the *world* by a *practice* — perceptual, inferential, testimonial — that the *inquirer* does not fully justify. In both cases, the *anchor* is real, and the *justification* of the anchor is not available from within the practice. The question is whether this is a *defect* of the practice or a *feature* of the epistemic situation.

This chapter is about that question. It is the *meta*-chapter of Part II, and it is the closest the book comes to a *methodological* *conclusion* before Part III. The problem it addresses — the **criterion problem** — is the problem of how we choose among our *sources* of knowledge, our *standards* of assessment, and our *methods* of inquiry. It is the problem that generates *relativism*, and the problem that *relativism* is a response to. It is the problem that makes *scepticism* more than a philosophical curiosity, and the problem that makes *objectivity* more than a slogan.

The chapter proceeds in seven movements. §1 states the criterion problem. §2 examines the three classical responses — methodism, particularism, scepticism — and the modern retreat to reflective equilibrium. §3 examines epistemic relativism, its varieties, and its arguments. §4 examines the self-refutation objection, and its limits. §5 takes up objectivity, the view from nowhere, and the situated-knowledge critique. §6 examines the error theory about knowledge, and the distinction between scepticism as a *position* and scepticism as a *practice*. §7 offers a diagnosis: what a *durable* anti-sceptical stance looks like. §8 closes Part II.

## 1. The Problem of the Criterion

### 1.1 The two questions

Chisholm’s formulation is the standard one, and it is worth stating with precision.

There are two questions that any epistemology must answer:

$$
(\mathrm{A}) \quad \text{What do we know? (What is the *extent* of our knowledge?)}
$$

$$
(\mathrm{B}) \quad \text{How do we know? (What is the *criterion* of knowledge?)}
$$

The **criterion problem** is the problem that these two questions cannot be answered *independently* of each other.

**The structure of the problem.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{To answer (A), we need a criterion (B) by which to *assess* the candidate instances.} \\
2. & \text{To answer (B), we need a body of *instances* (A) against which to *test* the candidate criterion.} \\
3. & \text{Neither is *independent*: each presupposes the other.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Neither can be *justified* without *presupposing* the other.}
\end{array}
$$

The argument is *valid*, and the conclusion is *not* that knowledge is impossible. The conclusion is that the *justification* of the *criterion* cannot be *foundationalist*. It must be *circular*, *holistic*, or *pragmatic*. The *question* is which.

**The two versions.** The problem can be stated *sceptically* (as an argument for *no knowledge*) or *constructively* (as an argument for a *non-foundationalist* *epistemology*). The *sceptical* version is Sextus’s; the *constructive* version is Chisholm’s. The *difference* is *which* premise is *emphasized*.

### 1.2 Sextus and the diallelus

Sextus Empiricus states the problem in the *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* II.20, and the statement is the *origin* of the modern debate.

> “The criterion of truth is the standard by which we judge what is true and what is false. But every judgment requires a criterion, and every criterion requires a judgment. The result is a *diallelus* — a circle — or an infinite regress, or a dogmatic assumption.”

**The terminology.** The **diallelus** — the “wheel” — is the figure of the *circle*. The word is important: it *describes* the *form* of the problem, not its *solution*.

**The *five modes* of Agrippa** (Ch. 6, §3.2) are the *formal* *statement* of the *diallelus*:

1. **Regress**: every criterion requires a further criterion; the regress is *infinite*.
2. **Hypothesis**: at some point, a criterion is *assumed* without justification.
3. **Reciprocity**: the criterion is justified by a *judgment*, and the judgment by the criterion.
4. **The criterion**: the question of *which* criterion is itself *undecided*.
5. **Relativity**: the criterion is *relative* to the *subject*, the *object*, or the *circumstances*.

**The *sceptical* *conclusion*.** The *diallelus* *establishes* that no *criterion* can be *justified* without *circularity*. The *Pyrrhonian* *response* is *suspension* of *judgment* (*epochē*) — not a *denial* of knowledge, but a *refusal* to *assert*.

**The *constructive* *reading*.** The *diallelus* establishes that the *justification* of the criterion cannot be *foundationalist*. It does *not* establish that the criterion cannot be *justified* at all. The *non*-foundationalist *justifications* — *coherentist*, *pragmatic*, *particularist* — are *responses* to the problem.

### 1.3 The structure of the circularity

The circle has a *precise* structure, and the structure is worth distinguishing from *other* circularities.

**The *vicious* *circle*.** A *vicious* *circle* is a *premise* that *depends* on the *conclusion*, and the *dependence* is *substantive*: the premise would not be *accepted* if the conclusion were not *already* accepted. (Ch. 2, §3.4.)

**The *virtuous* *circle*.** A *virtuous* *circle* is a *structure* in which the *elements* *support* one another, and the *support* is *illuminating*: the *structure* *displays* the *connections* among the elements, and the *display* is *itself* a *form* of *justification*. (This is the *coherentist* *intuition*; Ch. 8, §3.)

**The *question*.** Is the criterion problem a *vicious* or a *virtuous* circle?

**The *particularist* *answer*.** The circle is *not* a circle at all: the *particular* instances of knowledge are *more* *certain* than any criterion, and the criterion is *derived* from them. The *direction* of *justification* is *from* the *particulars* *to* the *criteria*, *not* the *reverse*.

**The *methodist* *answer*.** The circle is *vicious* if the criterion is *not* *independently* *justified*. The *method* must be *established* *first*, and the *particulars* are *assessed* by it.

**The *sceptical* *answer*.** The circle is *vicious*, and the *only* response is *suspension*.

The *disagreement* is about the *order* of *justification*, not about the *content* of the *criteria*. It is a *dispute* about *which* *elements* are *epistemically* *prior*.

### 1.4 Why it matters

The criterion problem is not a *technical* *curiosity*. It matters for *three* reasons.

**(i) It is the form of the sceptical problem.** The *sceptical* arguments of Ch. 6 (the dream, the demon, the BIV) are *instances* of the criterion problem: they *question* the *criterion* (perception) by *questioning* the *instances* (the perceptual beliefs). The *responses* to scepticism are *responses* to the criterion problem.

**(ii) It is the form of the relativist challenge.** If the criterion is *not* *universal* — if it is *relative* to a *culture*, a *paradigm*, or a *community* — then the *objectivity* of knowledge is *threatened*. The *relativism* of §3 is the *claim* that the criterion is *local*, and the *objectivity* of §5 is the *claim* that it is *not*.

**(iii) It is the form of the book's thesis.** The book's thesis (Ch. 1, §2) is that philosophy is the study of the *conditions* of *intelligibility*. The criterion problem is the *question* of the *conditions* of the *intelligibility* of *knowledge*. It is the *question* the book is *about*, in its *most general* form.

## 2. Particularism, Methodism, and Reflective Equilibrium

### 2.1 Chisholm's three positions

Chisholm (*The Problem of the Criterion*, 1973) distinguishes *three* *responses* to the criterion problem.

| Position | Starting point | Method | Paradigm |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Methodism** | The criterion (B) | Derive the instances (A) from the criterion | Descartes; Locke; the empiricists |
| **Particularism** | The instances (A) | Derive the criterion (B) from the instances | Reid; Moore; Chisholm |
| **Scepticism** | Neither | Suspend judgment | Sextus; Hume (in the *Enquiry* XII) |

**The *logic* of the *dispute*.** The three positions are *not* *symmetrical*. Each *begins* with a *different* *class* of *propositions* as *epistemically* *prior*, and the *priority* is *itself* what is *at issue*. The *dispute* is *not* about *which* *propositions* are *true*; it is about *which* *propositions* are *basic*.

### 2.2 The particularist argument

The *particularist* *argument* is the *most* *influential* *response* to the criterion problem, and it is the *ancestor* of the *Reidian* *tradition* (Ch. 8, §2.6).

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{There are *particular* *instances* of knowledge that are *more* *certain* than any *criterion*.} \\
2. & \text{Example: I know that I have hands; I know that $2+2=4$; I know that I am in pain.} \\
3. & \text{The *certainty* of the *instances* is *greater* than the *certainty* of any *philosophical* *criterion*.} \\
4. & \text{In a *conflict* of *certainties*, the *greater* *certainty* *wins*.} \\
\hline
5. & \therefore \text{The *criterion* is *derived* from the *instances*, not the *reverse*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *proponents*.** Thomas Reid (*Inquiry*, 1764; *Essays on the Intellectual Powers*, 1785), G. E. Moore (“A Defence of Common Sense,” 1925; “Proof of an External World,” 1939), and Chisholm (*Theory of Knowledge*, 1966; *The Problem of the Criterion*, 1973).

**The *Moore* *version*.** Moore’s *proof* of an external world (Ch. 6, §6.1) is the *paradigm* of *particularism*: the *premise* (“here is one hand”) is *more* *certain* than any *philosophical* *argument* against it, and the *philosophical* *argument* is *rejected* because its *premises* are *less* *certain*.

**The *Reid* *version*.** Reid’s *appeal* to *common sense* is the *systematic* *version*: the *principles* of *common sense* are *constitutive* of *human* *cognition*, and the *philosophical* *criterion* is *assessed* by its *fit* with them.

**The *objection*.** The *particularist* *begs* the *question* against the *sceptic*. The *sceptic* *denies* that the *particular* *instances* are *certain*, and the *particularist* *asserts* that they are. The *dispute* is *not* *resolved*; it is *restated*. (This is the *objection* of Ch. 6, §6.1, and it is the *strongest* *objection* to *Moore*.)

**The *reply*.** The *particularist* *does* not *claim* to *refute* the *sceptic*. She *claims* that the *sceptic*’s *argument* is *less* *certain* than the *instances* it *attacks*, and the *claim* is *itself* a *substantive* *epistemic* *assessment*. The *reply* is *not* *viciously* *circular* (it does not *assume* the *conclusion*); it is *holistically* *circular* (it *places* the *instances* and the *criterion* in a *mutually* *supporting* *structure*).

### 2.3 The methodist argument

The *methodist* *argument* is the *classical* *modern* *response*, and it is the *ancestor* of the *foundationalist* *tradition* (Ch. 8, §2).

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *criterion* is *prior* to the *instances*: the *instances* are *knowledge* only if they *satisfy* the *criterion*.} \\
2. & \text{The *criterion* can be *established* by *reflection* on the *nature* of *knowledge* (Descartes’ *analysis* of *clear* and *distinct* *perception*; the empiricists’ *analysis* of *impressions*).} \\
3. & \text{The *instances* that *fail* the *criterion* are *not* knowledge.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *criterion* is *derived* from the *analysis*, and the *instances* are *assessed* by it.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *proponents*.** Descartes (*Meditations*, 1641), Locke (*Essay*, 1690), Hume (*Treatise*, 1739–40), and the *logical* *positivists* (Ch. 9, §2.3).

**The *virtue*.** The *methodist* *offers* a *justification* of the *criterion* that is *not* *circular*: the *analysis* of the *concept* of knowledge *yields* the *criterion*, and the *criterion* is *assessed* by its *adequacy* to the *concept*.

**The *objection*.** The *analysis* *presupposes* the *criterion*. To *analyse* the *concept* of knowledge, one must *know* which *cases* fall under it, and the *knowledge* *presupposes* the *criterion*. (This is the criterion problem in *analytic* *form*, and it is the *objection* that *Chisholm* *presses*.)

**The *reply*.** The *analysis* is *a priori*: it does *not* *require* *empirical* *cases*; it *requires* only the *competent* *use* of the *concept*. The *reply* is the *conceptual* *analysis* *defence* (Ch. 3, §1), and it is *contested* (Ch. 3, §1.4).

### 2.4 The retreat to reflective equilibrium

The *modern* *consensus* is that *neither* *methodism* *nor* *particularism* is *tenable* in its *pure* form. The *retreat* is to **reflective equilibrium** (Ch. 3, §4).

**The *structure*.** Neither the *instances* nor the *criteria* are *prior*. The *justification* is *holistic*: the *instances* are *adjusted* to *fit* the *criteria*, and the *criteria* are *adjusted* to *fit* the *instances*, *until* a *stable* *equilibrium* is *achieved*.

**The *proponents*.** Goodman (*Fact, Fiction, and Forecast*, 1955), Rawls (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971, §9), and the *coherentist* *tradition* (Ch. 8, §3).

**The *virtue*.** The *view* *avoids* the *begging* *of* *the* *question*: the *instances* and the *criteria* are *mutually* *adjusted*, and the *adjustment* is *itself* the *justification*.

**The *objection*.** The *view* *is* *conservative*: it *starts* with the *instances* and *criteria* that *we* *happen* to *have*, and the *equilibrium* *may* *preserve* *prejudice*. (This is the *objection* of Ch. 3, §4.3, and it is *particularly* *acute* for the *criterion* *problem*, because the *criterion* is *the* *instrument* by which *prejudice* is *detected*.)

**The *reply*.** The *view* *is* *wide*, not *narrow*: the *equilibrium* *includes* the *background* *theories* (metaphysics, psychology, the social *sciences*), and the *background* can *overturn* the *narrow* *equilibrium*. The *reply* *is* *promising*, but the *width* is *not* *guaranteed*, and the *guarantee* is *precisely* what the *sceptic* *asks* for.

### 2.5 The residue

The *criterion* *problem* is *not* *solved* by *any* of the *three* *responses*. It is *relocated*:

| Response | Where the problem is relocated | The residue |
|---|---|---|
| **Methodism** | To the *analysis* of the *concept* | The *analysis* presupposes the *criterion* |
| **Particularism** | To the *certainty* of the *instances* | The *certainty* is *contestable* |
| **Reflective equilibrium** | To the *coherence* of the *system* | The *system* may be *coherent* and *false* |
| **Scepticism** | To the *suspension* of *judgment* | The *suspension* is *not* a *solution* |

The *residue* is the *same* in each case: the *justification* of the *criterion* is *circular*, and the *circularity* is *not* *eliminated*; it is *made* *self-conscious*. The *question* is whether the *self-consciousness* is *a* *form* of *justification* or a *confession* of *failure*.

The *honest* *answer*: it is *both*. The *criterion* *problem* is *not* *solved* in the *sense* that the *foundationalist* *demanded*; it is *solved* in the *sense* that the *circularity* is *displayed*, and the *display* is *informative*. This is *the* *kind* of *progress* that Ch. 1, §5 *described*: not a *verdict*, but a *clarification*.

## 3. Epistemic Relativism

### 3.1 The varieties

The *word* “relativism” *covers* a *family* of *positions*. The *taxonomy* is *essential*, because the *arguments* for and against *differ* *across* the *varieties*.

| Variety | Claim | Domain | Paradigm |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Individual relativism** | Truth is relative to the *individual* | All | Protagoras |
| **Cultural relativism** | Truth/morality is relative to the *culture* | Morality, knowledge | Herodotus; Sumner; Benedict |
| **Conceptual relativism** | Different *conceptual schemes* are *incommensurable* | All | Kuhn (weak); Whorf |
| **Sociological relativism** | The *content* of science is *caused* by *social* *interests* | Science | Bloor; Barnes; Latour |
| **Pragmatic relativism** | Truth is *what* a *community* *agrees* on | All | Rorty |
| **Epistemic relativism** | Justification is relative to an *epistemic* *system* | Knowledge | the *contemporary* debate |

**The *key* *distinction*.** The *relativism* *of* §3 is **epistemic**: the *claim* that *justification* is *relative* to an *epistemic* *system* — a *framework* of *standards*, *methods*, and *presuppositions*. The *claim* is *not* *about* *truth* (the *alethic* *relativism*); it is *about* *justification*.

**The *alethic* *relativism*.** The *stronger* *claim*: *truth* *itself* is *relative*. The *view* is *associated* with *MacFarlane* (2014; Ch. 7, §6.2), and it is *contested*.

### 3.2 Protagoras and Plato's refutation

The *oldest* *statement* of *relativism* is Protagoras’s *homo mensura* *fragment*:

> “Man is the measure of all things, of things that are that they are, and of things that are not that they are not.”

**The *interpretation*.** The *fragment* is *ambiguous*. It can be *read* as:

1. **Individual relativism**: each *person*’s *beliefs* are *true* for *that* *person*.
2. **Species relativism**: the *human* *species* is the *measure* (a *form* of *Kantianism*).
3. **Perceptual relativism**: *perception* is the *measure* of *what* *appears* (a *phenomenalist* *reading*).

The *Platonic* *reading* (*Theaetetus* 152a–172c) is the *first*, and it is the *reading* that *generates* the *self-refutation* *argument*.

**Plato’s *argument* (*Theaetetus* 171a).**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Protagoras: for every } p, \text{ if } S \text{ believes } p, \text{ then } p \text{ is true for } S. \\
2. & \text{Many people believe } \neg(\text{Protagoras}). \\
3. & \text{Therefore, } \neg(\text{Protagoras}) \text{ is true for them.} \\
4. & \text{If the thesis is *universally* true, it is true for them.} \\
5. & \text{Therefore, } \neg(\text{Protagoras}) \text{ is true.} \\
\hline
6. & \therefore \text{Protagoras is false.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *structure*.** The argument is a *reductio*: the *thesis* *generates* its *own* *negation*. It is the *paradigm* of the *self-refutation* *argument* (§4).

### 3.3 The Strong Programme

The *most* *influential* *sociological* *relativism* is the **Strong Programme** in the *sociology* of *scientific* *knowledge* (SSK), associated with the *Edinburgh* *School* (Bloor 1976; Barnes 1974).

**The *four* *tenets* (Bloor, *Knowledge and Social Imagery*, 1976, p. 5).**

1. **Causality**: the *sociology* of *knowledge* *seeks* the *causes* of *belief* — *social* *causes* as well as *natural* ones.
2. **Impartiality**: the *sociology* is *impartial* with respect to *truth* and *falsity*, *rationality* and *irrationality*, *success* and *failure*.
3. **Symmetry**: the *same* *kinds* of *cause* explain *true* and *false* *beliefs*, *rational* and *irrational* *beliefs*.
4. **Reflexivity**: the *sociology* *applies* to *itself*.

**The *symmetry* *thesis* is the *radical* *one*.** It *denies* that *true* *beliefs* have a *special* *epistemic* *status*; *all* *beliefs* — *true* and *false* — are *explained* by *social* *causes*. The *consequence*: the *content* of *science* is *not* *determined* by *nature*; it is *determined* by *social* *interests*.

**The *paradigm* *case*. Latour and Woolgar’s *Laboratory Life* (1979), and Latour’s *Science in Action* (1987): the *“discovery”* of *thyrotropin-releasing* *hormone* (TRH) is *analysed* as a *process* of *social* *negotiation*, in which the *“fact”* is *constructed* by the *actors*, not *discovered* in *nature*.

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *reflexivity* *problem*.** If *all* *beliefs* are *determined* by *social* *interests*, then the *Strong* *Programme* *itself* is *determined* by *social* *interests*, and its *claim* to *truth* is *undermined*. (Bloor *accepts* the *consequence*: the *reflexivity* is a *virtue*; it *shows* that the *theory* is *consistent*. The *reply* is *contested*: the *consistency* is *not* *truth*.)

**(ii) The *“success”* *problem*.** The *Strong* *Programme* *cannot* *explain* *why* *science* *is* *successful*. If *all* *beliefs* are *socially* *caused*, the *success* of *science* is a *coincidence* — and the *coincidence* is *unexplained*. (The *reply*: *success* is *itself* a *social* *category*; the *reply* is *weak*.)

**(iii) The *“truth”* *problem*.** The *Strong* *Programme* *brackets* the *question* of *truth*, and the *bracketing* is *not* *neutral*: it *presupposes* that *truth* is *not* *explanatorily* *relevant*. The *presupposition* is *a* *philosophical* *thesis*, and it is *not* *established* by the *sociological* *method*.

**The *state* *of* the *debate*.** The *Strong* *Programme* *has* *won* *methodological* *battles* (the *social* *dimension* of *science* is *now* *recognized*) and *lost* *philosophical* ones (the *radical* *symmetry* is *widely* *rejected*). The *contemporary* *sociology* of *science* is *more* *modest*: it *studies* the *social* *conditions* of *science* without *denying* the *epistemic* *authority* of *evidence*.

### 3.4 Rorty and the conversation

Richard Rorty’s *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979) is the *most* *influential* *philosophical* *relativism* of the *late* *twentieth* *century*.

**The *thesis*.** The *“epistemological”* *tradition* — the *tradition* that *treats* knowledge as a *mirror* of nature — is *bankrupt*. The *alternative*: *epistemology* is *replaced* by *hermeneutics*; the *search* for *objective* *truth* is *replaced* by the *conversation* of *mankind*.

**The *key* *moves*.**

1. **The *critique* of the *“glassy”* *essence**: the *idea* that the *mind* *mirrors* *nature* is a *metaphor*, not a *theory*.
2. **The *critique* of *representation*: the *idea* that *knowledge* *represents* an *independent* *reality* is a *legacy* of *Descartes* and *Kant*, and it is *not* *defensible*.
3. **The *“ethnocentric”* *alternative*: the *only* *justification* is *solidarity* with *our* *community*; the *search* for a *trans-cultural* *justification* is a *form* of *cruelty*.

**The *famous* *passage* (*Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity*, 1989, p. 198):**

> “There is no such thing as ‘the way the world is’ … The world is out there, but descriptions of the world are not. Only descriptions of the world can be true or false.”

**The *objections*.**

**(i) The *self-refutation* *objection*.** Rorty’s *thesis* *appears* to be a *claim* about *the way the world is* (there is no *way*), and it is *therefore* *self-undermining*. (Rorty’s *reply*: the *thesis* is *not* a *description*; it is a *proposal* for *how* *to* *talk*. The *reply* is *contested*: it *changes* the *subject* from *truth* to *conversational* *utility*, and the *change* is *precisely* what is *at issue*.)

**(ii) The *“solidarity”* *problem*.** If *solidarity* is the *only* *justification*, then the *community* *that* *tortures* *heretics* is *justified* in *its* *beliefs*. The *consequence* is *unacceptable* to most *philosophers*, and Rorty’s *reply* (the *community* *we* *want* is the *liberal* one) is *question-begging*.

**(iii) The *“ethnocentrism”* *problem*.** Rorty *accepts* the *charge*: his *position* is *ethnocentric*. The *question* is *whether* *ethnocentrism* is *a* *position* or a *confession* of the *absence* of a *position*. The *answer* is *contest*.

### 3.5 The arguments for relativism

Boghossian (*Fear of Knowledge*, 2006) *reconstructs* the *arguments* for *relativism* into *four* *families*, and *criticizes* each.

| Argument | Form | Boghossian's critique |
|---|---|---|
| **The argument from epistemic dependence** | Our beliefs are caused by our *social* circumstances; therefore they are *justified* only relative to those circumstances | The *causal* premise does not entail the *normative* conclusion |
| **The argument from equal validity** | There are *equally* *valid* *alternative* *frameworks* | The *premise* is *question-begging*: it *assumes* the *conclusion* |
| **The argument from the underdetermination of theory by evidence** | The evidence does not *uniquely* *determine* the theory; therefore the *choice* is *not* *objective* | The *underdetermination* is *local*; the *choice* can be *objective* *despite* the *underdetermination* (Ch. 40, §3) |
| **The argument from the history of science** | The *history* of *science* is a *history* of *refuted* *theories*; therefore the *norms* are *not* *stable* | The *history* shows that *theories* change, not that *norms* are *relative* |

**The *general* *structure*.** The *arguments* for *relativism* *slide* from a *descriptive* *premise* (beliefs are *caused*, *frameworks* *differ*) to a *normative* *conclusion* (justification is *relative*). The *slide* is an *instance* of the *is–ought* *gap* (Ch. 2, §6.3; Ch. 44, §1). The *relativist* *needs* a *normative* *premise*, and the *premise* is *not* *established* by the *sociology*.

## 4. Self-Refutation and Its Limits

### 4.1 The form of the argument

The **self-refutation argument** is the *standard* *response* to *relativism*, and it is the *standard* *response* to *scepticism* (Ch. 6, §3.3). Its *form* is *simple*:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The thesis } T \text{ implies } \neg T. \\
\hline
2. & \therefore \neg T.
\end{array}
$$

The *argument* is a *reductio*: the *thesis* *refutes* *itself*.

**The *varieties*.**

| Variety | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Logical self-refutation** | $T$ entails $\neg T$ | “This sentence is false” (the Liar) |
| **Performative self-refutation** | The *act* of asserting $T$ *contradicts* $T$ | “I cannot speak” |
| **Pragmatic self-refutation** | The *presuppositions* of asserting $T$ *contradict* $T$ | “All beliefs are caused by interests” (the *assertion* *presupposes* the *validity* of the *argument*) |

**The *classic* *examples*.**

- **Protagoras** (*Theaetetus* 171a; §3.2).
- **The *sceptic*** (Ch. 6, §3.3).
- **The *relativist*** (the *contemporary* *debate*).
- **The *eliminativist* *about* *the* *mental*** (the *statement* of *eliminativism* *presupposes* the *mental* *states* it *denies*; Ch. 23, §6).

### 4.2 The indexical reply

The *standard* *reply* to the *self-refutation* *argument* is the **indexical** *reply*.

**The *structure*.** The *relativist*’s *thesis* is *not* the *universal* *claim* “$p$ is true”; it is the *indexical* *claim* “$p$ is true *for me*.” The *indexical* *claim* is *not* *contradicted* by the *disagreement*; it is *confirmed* by it.

**The *example*.** The *relativist* *says*: “*For me*, the *statement* ‘*torture* is *wrong*’ is *true*; *for you*, it is *false*.” The *two* *claims* are *not* *contradictory*; they are *consistent*.

**The *application* to *Protagoras*.** The *Protagorean* *reply* to Plato: “The *thesis* is *about* the *indexical* *truth* *predicate*; the *disagreement* *confirms* it.”

**The *objection*.** The *indexical* *reply* *changes* the *subject*: the *original* *thesis* was *about* *truth* *simpliciter*, and the *revised* *thesis* is *about* *truth-for-me*. The *revised* *thesis* is *trivial* (everyone *agrees* that *different* *people* *believe* *different* *things*), and it *does not* *support* the *relativist*’s *claims*.

### 4.3 The meta-level reply

A *stronger* *reply*: the *relativist*’s *thesis* is *not* a *first-order* *claim*; it is a *second-order* *claim* *about* the *semantics* of *the* *first-order* *claims*. The *self-refutation* *argument* *assumes* that the *second-order* *claim* is *itself* *subject* to the *relativity* *it* *describes*.

**The *structure*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *first-order* *claims* are *relative* to an *epistemic* *system*.} \\
2. & \text{The *second-order* *claim* — “justification is relative” — is *not* a *first-order* *claim*; it is a *meta*-claim.} \\
3. & \text{The *meta*-claim* is *assessed* by *meta*-standards, which are *not* *the* *standards* of the *first-order* *domain*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *self-refutation* *argument* *does* not *apply*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *objection*.** The *meta*-level *reply* *generates* a *regress*. If the *meta*-standards are *absolute*, the *relativist* *has* *abandoned* *relativism* at the *meta*-level. If the *meta*-standards are *relative*, the *regress* *continues* *indefinitely*. The *reply* *is* *unstable*.

### 4.4 The companions in guilt

The **companions-in-guilt** *argument* is the *most* *powerful* *response* to the *self-refutation* *objection* (Ch. 44, §5).

**The *structure*.** The *self-refutation* *objection* *applies* *equally* to *any* *position* that *grounds* *normativity* in *something* *human*. If the *relativist* is *refuted* by the *objection*, so is the *naturalist*, the *expressivist*, and the *coherentist*. The *objection* *proves* *too* *much*.

**The *example*.** The *naturalist* *says*: “*Moral* *facts* are *natural* *facts*.” The *self-refutation* *objection*: “*If* *moral* *facts* are *natural* *facts*, *then* the *statement* ‘*moral* *facts* are *natural* *facts*’ is *itself* a *natural* *fact*, and it *cannot* *be* *a* *reason*.” The *objection* *applies* *equally*.

**The *consequence*.** The *self-refutation* *argument* *cannot* be *used* *selectively*. If it *refutes* *relativism*, it *refutes* *every* *position* *that* *grounds* *normativity* in *something* *less* *than* *absolute*. The *argument* *must* be *supplemented* by a *positive* *account* of *how* *normativity* *is* *possible* — and the *account* is the *hard* *part*.

### 4.5 The limits of self-refutation

The *self-refutation* *argument* is a *powerful* *weapon*, and it has *limits*.

**(i) It is a *local* *weapon*.** The *argument* *refutes* a *thesis* by *showing* that it *implies* its *own* *negation*. It *does* *not* *establish* the *negation*; it *establishes* that the *thesis* *cannot* be *maintained*.

**(ii) It is *dialectical*.** The *argument* *works* only against a *thesis* that *claims* *universal* *validity*. A *thesis* that *expressly* *limits* its *scope* — *local* *relativism*, *therapeutic* *scepticism*, *hinge* *epistemology* — *escapes* the *argument*.

**(iii) It is *not* a *justification*.** The *argument* *refutes* the *opponent*; it *does* *not* *establish* *one*’s *own* *position*. The *refutation* of *relativism* is *not* the *proof* of *objectivity*.

**(iv) It *may* be *self-refuting*.** The *argument* *itself* *presupposes* *logical* *norms* that the *opponent* may *question*. The *presupposition* *is* *not* *vicious* (the *opponent* *uses* *logic* *too*), but it is *not* *neutral*.

**The *moral*.** The *self-refutation* *argument* is a *necessary* *instrument* of *philosophical* *criticism*, and it is *not* *sufficient* for *philosophical* *construction*. The *critique* of *relativism* is *easy*; the *construction* of *objectivity* is *hard*.

## 5. Objectivity

### 5.1 Nagel and the view from nowhere

Thomas Nagel’s *The View from Nowhere* (1986) is the *most* *influential* *account* of *objectivity* in the *contemporary* *literature*.

**The *thesis*.** Objectivity is the *achievement* of a *perspective* that is *not* *the* *perspective* of *any* *particular* *subject* — the *“view* *from* *nowhere*.” The *achievement* is *gradual*: it is *reached* by *abstraction* from the *particular* *perspective*, and it is *never* *complete*.

**The *structure*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *subjective* *perspective* is the *starting* *point*: the *world* as it *appears* to a *particular* *subject* *here* and *now*. \\
2. & \text{The *objective* *perspective* is *reached* by *abstracting* from the *particularities* — the *here*, the *now*, the *me*.} \\
3. & \text{The *abstraction* is *never* *complete*: there is *always* a *residue* of *subjectivity* — the *phenomenal*, the *evaluative*, the *existential*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Objectivity is a *direction*, not a *destination*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *account* *explains* why *objectivity* is *valuable* and *why* it is *elusive*: the *value* is *detachment* from *parochial* *perspective*, and the *elusiveness* is the *impossibility* of *complete* *detachment*.

**The *objection*.** The *account* *remains* *within* the *individualist* *framework*: the *“view* *from* *nowhere”* is a *view* *from* *somewhere* — the *view* of a *particular* *kind* of *subject* (the *white*, *male*, *Western*, *professional* *philosopher*). The *objection* is the *starting* *point* of the *feminist* *critique* (§5.2).

### 5.2 Haraway and situated knowledge

Donna Haraway’s “Situated Knowledges” (1988) is the *most* *influential* *feminist* *response* to Nagel.

**The *critique*. The *“god* *trick”*** — the *pretence* of *seeing* *everything* *from* *nowhere* — is *false*, and it is *politically* *dangerous*: it *masks* the *particular* *perspective* of the *powerful* as *universal*.

**The *alternative*. **Situated* *knowledge*: the *knowledge* *that* is *achieved* *from* a *particular* *location*, and that is *objective* *because* it is *accountable*.

**The *structure*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{All *knowledge* is *situated*: it is *achieved* from a *particular* *location*, with *particular* *interests*.} \\
2. & \text{The *“view* *from* *nowhere”* is *not* *neutral*; it is the *view* of the *powerful*, *masquerading* as *universal*.} \\
3. & \text{Objectivity is *not* *detachment*; it is *accountability* — the *willingness* to be *answerable* for the *location* *from* which one *knows*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Objectivity is *situated*, not *aperspectival*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *virtue*.** The *account* *explains* why *the* *“neutral”* *view* is *often* *biased*: the *neutrality* is *achieved* by *ignoring* the *perspectives* of the *marginalized*, and the *ignoring* is a *form* of *power*.

**The *objection*.** The *account* *threatens* to *collapse* *into* *relativism*: if *all* *knowledge* is *situated*, *no* *knowledge* is *better* than *any* *other*. (Haraway’s *reply*: *not* all *situations* are *equal*; the *situations* of the *marginalized* are *epistemically* *privileged*, because they *see* the *system* from *below*. The *reply* *is* *contested*: the *privilege* *thesis* *is* a *substantive* *claim* that *needs* *argument*.)

### 5.3 Longino and critical contextual empiricism

Helen Longino’s *Science as Social Knowledge* (1990) and *The Fate of Knowledge* (2002) offer the *most* *systematic* *account* of *social* *objectivity*.

**The *thesis*.** Objectivity is a *property* of the *scientific* *community*, not of the *individual* *scientist*. The *community* is *objective* to the *extent* that it *satisfies* *four* *conditions*.

**The *four* *conditions* (Longino 1990, pp. 76–81; 2002, Ch. 5).**

1. **Recognized venues for criticism**: there are *institutional* *spaces* in which *criticism* can be *voiced*.
2. **Shared standards**: the *critics* and the *criticized* *share* a *set* of *standards* (evidence, coherence, etc.).
3. **Uptake of criticism**: the *criticism* is *actually* *engaged* with, not *ignored*.
4. **Equality of intellectual authority**: the *participants* have *equal* *standing*, regardless of *social* *position*.

**The *virtue*.** The *account* *explains* why *objectivity* is *compatible* with *social* *situatedness*: the *objectivity* is *achieved* *through* the *social* *process*, not *despite* it.

**The *objection*.** The *conditions* are *ideal*: *no* *actual* *community* *satisfies* them *fully*, and the *gap* between the *ideal* and the *actual* is the *space* in which *bias* *operates*.

### 5.4 The fact/value entanglement

Hilary Putnam’s *The Collapse of the Fact/Value Dichotomy* (2002) is the *most* *influential* *account* of the *entanglement*.

**The *thesis*.** The *fact/value* *dichotomy* is *untenable*: the *distinction* between *descriptive* and *evaluative* *concepts* is not *sharp*, and the *epistemic* *concepts* (justification, warranted assertibility, coherence) are *themselves* *value-laden*.

**The *argument* (Putnam, following Murdoch and Anscombe).** The *“thick”* *ethical* *concepts* — *cruel*, *courageous*, *generous*, *brutal* — are *both* *descriptive* *and* *evaluative*. The *description* *entails* the *evaluation*, and the *entailment* is *not* *defeasible*. The *dichotomy* *cannot* *accommodate* the *thick* *concepts*.

**The *extension* to *epistemology*.** The *epistemic* *concepts* are *thick* *in* the *same* *way*: “*justified*,” “*warranted*,” “*rational*” *are* *descriptive* (they *describe* a *cognitive* *state*) and *evaluative* (they *endorse* the *state*). The *epistemic* *norms* *are* *values*, and the *fact/value* *dichotomy* *cannot* *separate* them.

**The *consequence*.** The *objectivity* of *knowledge* is *not* the *objectivity* of *value-free* *fact*. It is the *objectivity* of a *normative* *practice* — a *practice* that is *accountable* to the *world* and to the *community*.

**The *objection*.** The *entanglement* *thesis* *threatens* to *collapse* *the* *distinction* *between* *knowledge* and *ideology*. If the *epistemic* *norms* *are* *values*, *whose* *values* are *they*? (Putnam’s *reply*: the *values* are *the* *values* of *inquiry* — *truthfulness*, *coherence*, *responsibility* — and the *values* are *not* *arbitrary*. The *reply* *is* *contested*.)

### 5.5 The thin objectivity

The *contemporary* *consensus* is a **thin** *conception* of *objectivity*, analogous to the *thin* *conception* of the *a priori* (Ch. 9, §3.4).

| Thick conception (classical) | Thin conception (contemporary) |
|---|---|
| The view from nowhere | The direction of abstraction |
| Aperspectival | Situated, but accountable |
| Value-free | Entangled with value, but constrained |
| Individual | Social, achieved through criticism |
| Certain | Fallible, revisable |

**The *components*.**

1. **Objectivity is a *direction*, not a *destination*** (Nagel).
2. **Objectivity is *situated*, but *accountable*** (Haraway).
3. **Objectivity is *socially* *achieved*** (Longino).
4. **Objectivity is *entangled* with *value*, but *constrained* by the *world*** (Putnam).
5. **Objectivity is *fallible* and *revisable*** (the *fallibilist* *consensus*).

**The *payoff*.** The *thin* *conception* *avoids* *both* *the* *classical* *illusion* (the *view* *from* *nowhere*) and the *relativist* *capitulation* (the *equality* of *all* *perspectives*). It is *objectivity* *enough* for *inquiry*, and *humble* *enough* to *survive* the *critique*.

## 6. Error Theory and the Two Scepticisms

### 6.1 The error theory about knowledge

The **error theory** *about* *knowledge* is the *analogue* of *Mackie’s* *error* *theory* *about* *morals* (Ch. 44, §3).

**The *thesis*.** The *concept* of *knowledge* *presupposes* *objective* *norms* — *norms* that *bind* *independently* of *any* *community* or *practice*. There are *no* *such* *norms*. *Therefore*, *knowledge* *attributions* *are* *systematically* *false*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Knowledge attributions presuppose *objective* *norms* of justification.} \\
2. & \text{There are no *objective* *norms* of justification (the *relativist* *premise*).} \\
\hline
3. & \therefore \text{Knowledge attributions are *systematically* *false*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *self-undermining* *worry*.** The *error* *theorist* *claims* to *know* that there are *no* *objective* *norms*, and the *claim* *presupposes* an *objective* *norm* (the *norm* of *truth*). The *worry* is the *same* as the *worry* *about* *Mackie’s* *error* *theory* (Ch. 44, §3.1).

### 6.2 The self-undermining worry

The *worry* has *two* *forms*.

**(i) The *logical* *form*.** The *error* *theorist* *asserts* $\neg K$ (there is no *knowledge*), and the *assertion* *presupposes* $K$ (the *assertion* *claims* *knowledge*). The *thesis* *implies* its *own* *negation*.

**(ii) The *pragmatic* *form*.** The *error* *theorist* *asks* us to *believe* her *theory*, and the *request* *presupposes* that the *theory* is *better* *justified* than the *alternatives*. If *there* *is* *no* *objective* *justification*, the *request* *cannot* *be* *justified*.

**The *reply*.** The *error* *theorist* *can* *distinguish*:

- **The *object* *level***: the *first-order* *knowledge* *attributions* (the *ordinary* *claims*).
- **The *meta* *level***: the *error* *theory* *itself*, which is an *empirical* *claim* *about* the *concept*, *assessed* by *theoretical* *virtues* (simplicity, explanatory power), *not* by *objective* *norms*.

**The *objection*.** The *distinction* *presupposes* that the *theoretical* *virtues* are *not* *objective* *norms*. If they *are*, the *error* *theory* *presupposes* what it *denies*. If they *are* *not*, the *error* *theory* *cannot* *claim* to be *better* *justified* than the *alternatives*.

The *worry* is *not* *resolved*, and it is the *reason* the *error* *theory* *about* *knowledge* is a *minority* *position*.

### 6.3 Scepticism as position versus practice

The *most* *important* *distinction* of the *chapter* is between *scepticism* as a *position* and *scepticism* as a *practice*.

| Scepticism as position | Scepticism as practice |
|---|---|
| The *thesis* that knowledge is impossible | The *discipline* of suspending judgment |
| *Asserts* a *claim* | *Tests* *claims* |
| Self-undermining (§6.2) | *Compatible* with *knowledge* |
| Pyrrho *the* *theorist* | Pyrrho *the* *practitioner* |

**The *practice*.** The *Pyrrhonian* *practice* — the *discipline* of *suspending* *judgment* — is *not* the *thesis* that *knowledge* is *impossible*. It is the *discipline* of *resisting* *dogma*, *testing* *claims*, and *acknowledging* *uncertainty*. The *discipline* is *compatible* with a *robust* *epistemology*, because the *discipline* *uses* *knowledge* (the *knowledge* of *how* to *test*) and *improves* it.

**The *application*.** The *sceptical* *practice* is the *basis* of:

- The *critical* *method* of *science* (Popper; Ch. 40, §2).
- The *Socratic* *practice* of *questioning* (Ch. 54, §4).
- The *Pyrrhonian* *therapy* of *dogmatism* (Ch. 3, §7).
- The *epistemic* *virtues* of *humility*, *openness*, and *vigilance* (Ch. 4, §4.6).

**The *lesson*.** The *sceptical* *position* is *self-undermining*; the *sceptical* *practice* is *indispensable*. The *confusion* of the *two* is the *source* of much *of* the *confusion* in *epistemology*, and the *distinction* is the *key* to a *durable* *anti-sceptical* *stance*.

### 6.4 The Pyrrhonian practice and the modern academy

The *Pyrrhonian* *practice* has been *recovered* in the *contemporary* *literature* (Ch. 6, §3.3), and it is *relevant* to the *modern* *academy*.

**The *institutional* *form***. The *seminar*, the *referee* *report*, the *peer* *review*, the *replication* *crisis* — these are *institutions* of the *sceptical* *practice*. Their *function* is to *test* *claims*, *suspend* *judgment*, and *resist* *dogma*.

**The *virtue*.** The *practice* *requires* the *virtues* of *humility*, *courage*, and *honesty* (Ch. 4, §4). The *virtues* are *not* *optional*; they are *constitutive* of the *practice*.

**The *threat*.** The *practice* is *threatened* by the *incentives* of the *academy* (Ch. 4, §5.6): the *pressure* to *publish*, the *reward* for *novelty*, and the *erosion* of *gatekeeping* (Ch. 11, §6.5). The *threat* is *structural*, and it is *the* *central* *challenge* for the *contemporary* *discipline*.

## 7. Diagnosis: A Durable Anti-Sceptical Stance

### 7.1 The problem

The *chapter* has *surveyed* the *criterion* *problem* (§1), the *responses* to it (§2), the *relativist* *challenge* (§3), the *self-refutation* *objection* (§4), the *objectivity* *debate* (§5), and the *error* *theory* (§6). The *question* that *remains* is *constructive*: **what does a *durable* *anti-sceptical* *stance* *look* *like*?**

The *answer* is *not* a *single* *argument*. It is a *structure* — a *configuration* of *positions*, *practices*, and *virtues* that *together* *withstand* the *sceptical* *challenge*.

**The *elements*.**

| Element | Content | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Particularism** | Start with particular instances, not with the criterion | Chisholm; Reid; Moore |
| **Reflective equilibrium** | Adjust instances and criteria to mutual coherence | Goodman; Rawls |
| **Hinge epistemology** | Some commitments are exempt from doubt | Wittgenstein; Pritchard |
| **Social calibration** | Objectivity is achieved through criticism | Longino; Fricker |
| **Fallibilism** | All knowledge is revisable | Peirce; Popper |
| **The sceptical practice** | Test claims; resist dogma | Sextus; Popper |

The *elements* are *not* *independent*. They are *mutually* *supporting*, and the *support* is the *structure*.

### 7.2 The particularist starting point

The *first* *element* is *particularism*: the *starting* *point* is *not* the *criterion*, but the *particular* *instances* of *knowledge* that are *more* *certain* than any *philosophical* *argument* *against* them.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Any *philosophical* *argument* *uses* *premises* that are *less* *certain* than the *ordinary* *beliefs* it *attacks*.} \\
2. & \text{The *ordinary* *beliefs* (that I have hands, that $2+2=4$, that I am in pain) are *more* *certain* than the *premises* of *the* *sceptical* *argument*.} \\
3. & \text{In a *conflict* of *certainties*, the *greater* *wins*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *sceptical* *argument* is *unsound*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *caveat*.** The *argument* is *not* a *refutation* of the *sceptic*; it is a *refusal* to *accept* the *sceptic*’s *framework*. The *refusal* is *itself* a *substantive* *epistemic* *stance*, and it is *defensible* — but it is *not* *demonstrative*.

**The *residue*.** The *particularist* *stance* *leaves* the *criterion* *problem* *unresolved*. It *does* not *provide* a *justification* of the *criterion*; it *provides* a *refusal* to *demand* one. The *refusal* is *the* *answer*, and the *answer* is *honest*.

### 7.3 The reflective equilibrium

The *second* *element* is *reflective* *equilibrium*: the *instances* and the *criteria* are *adjusted* to *mutual* *coherence*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *criterion* is *derived* from the *instances*, and the *instances* are *assessed* by the *criterion*.} \\
2. & \text{The *derivation* and the *assessment* are *not* *sequential*; they are *mutual*.} \\
3. & \text{The *mutual* *adjustment* *produces* a *state* in which the *instances* and the *criteria* *support* one another.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *justification* is *holistic*, not *foundational*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *caveat*.** The *equilibrium* is *fallible*, *revisable*, and *potentially* *parochial* (Ch. 3, §4.3). The *caveat* is *not* a *refutation*; it is a *recognition* that the *justification* is *provisional*.

### 7.4 The social calibration

The *third* *element* is *social* *calibration*: the *objectivity* is *achieved* through the *critical* *process* of the *community* (Longino, §5.3).

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *individual* *inquirer* is *subject* to *bias*, *error*, and *parochialism*.} \\
2. & \text{The *community* can *correct* the *bias* — *if* it *satisfies* the *conditions* of *criticism*.} \\
3. & \text{The *correction* is *not* *guaranteed*, but it is *the* *best* *available* *mechanism*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *objectivity* is *socially* *achieved*, not *individually* *guaranteed*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *caveat*.** The *community* can *fail* (the *incentives* of §6.4; the *epistemic* *injustice* of Ch. 11, §4). The *social* *calibration* is *a* *condition* of *objectivity*, not a *guarantee*.

### 7.5 The fallibilist humility

The *fourth* *element* is *fallibilism*: all *knowledge* is *revisable*, and the *recognition* of the *revisability* is *itself* a *condition* of *knowledge*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *history* of *inquiry* is a *history* of *refuted* *theories* and *revised* *norms*.} \\
2. & \text{The *recognition* of the *history* *undermines* *the* *claim* to *certainty*.} \\
3. & \text{The *recognition* *does* not *undermine* the *claim* to *justification*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{Knowledge is *justified*, *fallible*, and *revisable*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *caveat*.** Fallibilism is *not* *scepticism*: the *recognition* that a *belief* *might* be *wrong* is *not* the *recognition* that it *is* *wrong*. The *distinction* is *the* *key* to *the* *stance*.

### 7.6 The virtues

The *fifth* *element* is *the* *epistemic* *virtues*: *humility*, *courage*, *honesty*, *openness*, and *vigilance*.

**The *argument*.**

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{The *justification* of a *belief* is *not* *sufficient* for *good* *inquiry*.} \\
2. & \text{The *inquirer* *needs* the *dispositions* to *seek* *objections*, *acknowledge* *error*, and *resist* *dogma*.} \\
3. & \text{The *dispositions* are the *virtues*.} \\
\hline
4. & \therefore \text{The *virtues* are *constitutive* of *good* *inquiry*.}
\end{array}
$$

**The *caveat*.** The *virtues* are *not* *sufficient*: the *inquirer* can be *virtuous* and *wrong*. The *virtues* are *necessary* conditions, not *sufficient* ones.

### 7.7 What the stance does not claim

The *stance* is *durable* because it *does* not *claim* what it *cannot* *deliver*.

**It does not claim:**

- **Certainty.** The *stance* *accepts* *fallibilism*.
- **A *refutation* of the *sceptic*.** The *stance* *acknowledges* that the *sceptic*’s *argument* is *not* *refuted*; it is *resisted*.
- **A *foundation*.** The *stance* *accepts* the *criterion* *problem*; it *does* not *solve* it.
- **A *view* *from* *nowhere*.** The *stance* *accepts* that *objectivity* is *situated* and *social*.
- **The *elimination* of *disagreement*.** The *stance* *accepts* that *disagreement* is *permanent* (Ch. 9, §6).

**It does claim:**

- **The *practice* of *inquiry* is *justified* — by its *track* *record*, its *self-correction*, and its *fruits*.**
- **The *justification* is *not* *foundational*, but it is *real*.**
- **The *alternative* — *relativism*, *scepticism*, *quietism* — is *less* *defensible* than the *practice*.**

### 7.8 The dialectical map

| Stance | What it preserves | What it gives up | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dogmatism** | Certainty | Fallibility | Unstable |
| **Classical foundationalism** | A foundation | The criterion problem | Refuted (Ch. 8, §2) |
| **Scepticism** | Consistency | Knowledge | Self-undermining |
| **Relativism** | Tolerance | Objectivity | Self-undermining |
| **Quietism** | Peace | The philosophical project | Unsatisfying |
| **The durable stance** | Knowledge, fallibly | Certainty | Defensible |

The *table* is a *map*, not a *verdict*. The *durable* *stance* is *not* *the* *only* *option*; it is the *option* that *survives* the *argument* *best*.

## 8. Coda: The End of Part II

### 8.1 The arc of Part II

Part II began with *perception* (Ch. 5), and it ended with *the* *criterion*. The *arc* is *not* *accidental*: the *perceptual* *starting* *point* *generates* the *sceptical* *problem* (Ch. 6), the *sceptical* *problem* *generates* the *analysis* of *knowledge* (Ch. 7), the *analysis* *generates* the *theory* of *justification* (Ch. 8), the *theory* *generates* the *theory* of the *a* *priori* (Ch. 9) and *induction* (Ch. 10), the *two* *sources* *generate* the *social* *turn* (Ch. 11), the *social* *turn* *generates* the *theory* of *reference* (Ch. 12), and the *theory* of *reference* *generates* the *criterion* *problem* (this *chapter*). The *arc* is a *spiral*: each *step* *returns* to the *starting* *point*, but at a *higher* *level of *complexity*.

**The *result*.** The *result* is *not* a *system*. It is a *map* of the *options*, a *set* of *distinctions*, and a *repertoire* of *arguments*. The *map* is *the* *contribution*.

### 8.2 What Part II established

- **The *perceptual* *starting* *point* is *not* *foundational* (Ch. 5; Ch. 8).
- **The *sceptical* *problem* is *not* *refuted*, but it is *resisted* (Ch. 6; this *chapter*).
- **The *analysis* of *knowledge* is *not* *complete*, but it is *illuminating* (Ch. 7).
- **The *theory* of *justification* is *not* *settled*, but it is *structured* (Ch. 8).
- **The *a* *priori* and *induction* are *real* *sources*, but *thin* *and* *fallible* (Chs. 9–10).
- **The *social* *turn* is *essential*, not *ornamental* (Ch. 11).
- **The *theory* of *reference* is *not* *Russellian*, but *Russell’s* *distinctions* *survive* (Ch. 12).
- **The *criterion* *problem* is *not* *solved*, but it is *diagnosed* (this *chapter*).

### 8.3 What Part III will do

Part III turns from *knowledge* to *reality*. The *questions* are:

- What does it mean to say that something *exists*? (Ch. 14)
- Are there *universals*, *properties*, *abstract* *objects*? (Ch. 15)
- What is *modality*? (Ch. 16)
- What is *time*? (Ch. 17)
- What is *causation*? (Ch. 18)
- What is *matter*? (Ch. 19)
- Is *reality* *mind-dependent*? (Ch. 20)
- What is *reduction*? (Ch. 21)
- What is *metaphysical* *method*? (Ch. 22)

The *connection* to Part II is *direct*. The *criterion* *problem* (this *chapter*) is the *epistemological* *form* of the *metaphysical* *question*: *what* *is* *real*? The *relativism* *question* is the *epistemological* *form* of the *realism* *question*: *is* there a *fact* of the *matter*? The *objectivity* *question* is the *epistemological* *form* of the *question* of *ontological* *commitment*: *what* *are* *the* *standards* of *existence*?

The *metaphysical* *questions* are *not* *independent* of the *epistemological* *ones*. The *whole* *book* is a *study* of the *conditions* of *intelligibility*, and the *conditions* of *intelligibility* of *knowledge* are *also* the *conditions* of *intelligibility* of *reality*. The *next* *part* *explores* the *second* *half* of the *study*.

### 8.4 The hinge

The *chapter* — and *Part II* — *ends* with a *hinge*. The *hinge* is the *recognition* that *inquiry* is *possible* *despite* the *criterion* *problem*, and that *knowledge* is *real* *despite* the *sceptical* *challenge*. The *recognition* is *not* a *proof*; it is a *commitment*. And the *commitment* is the *starting* *point* of *metaphysics*.

**The *Wittgensteinian* *formula* (*On Certainty*, §341):**

> “The questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn.”

**The *application*.** The *hinge* is the *exemption* of the *practice* of *inquiry* from the *demand* for a *foundation*. The *exemption* is *not* a *refusal* to *justify*; it is a *recognition* that the *justification* is *internal* to the *practice*, and the *practice* is the *condition* of the *justification*.

**The *closing* *thought*.** The *criterion* *problem* is *not* solved; it is *lived* *with*. The *living* *with* — the *practice* of *inquiry*, the *discipline* of *criticism*, the *virtue* of *humility* — is the *answer*. It is *not* the *answer* the *foundationalist* *wanted*, and it is *the* *answer* that *survives* the *argument*. The *next* *part* *begins* from *this* *answer*, and it *asks* *what* *reality* *must* *be* *like* for the *answer* to be *possible*.

## Summary

- **The criterion problem** is the problem that the *extent* of knowledge (A) and the *criterion* of knowledge (B) cannot be *justified* *independently* of each other. The *problem* is stated by *Sextus* (the *diallelus*) and *Chisholm* (the *two* *questions*).
- **The three responses** — *methodism* (criterion first), *particularism* (instances first), *scepticism* (neither) — each *presuppose* what they *seek* to *justify*. The *modern* *retreat* is to *reflective* *equilibrium*, which *adjusts* the *instances* and the *criteria* to *mutual* *coherence*, and *leaves* the *circularity* *self-conscious*.
- **Epistemic relativism** is the *claim* that *justification* is *relative* to an *epistemic* *system*. Its *varieties* include *individual* (Protagoras), *cultural*, *conceptual*, *sociological* (the Strong Programme), and *pragmatic* (Rorty). The *arguments* for *relativism* *slide* from *descriptive* *premises* to *normative* *conclusions*, and the *slide* is *unjustified* (Boghossian).
- **The self-refutation argument** is the *standard* *response* to *relativism* and *scepticism*. Its *limits*: it is *local* (it *refutes* but does not *establish*), *dialectical* (it *works* only against *universal* *claims*), *non-justificatory* (it *refutes* the *opponent* but does not *establish* one’s *own* *position*), and *potentially* *self-refuting* (it *presupposes* *logical* *norms*).
- **Objectivity** is *not* the *view* *from* *nowhere*. It is a *direction* (Nagel), *situated* and *accountable* (Haraway), *socially* *achieved* (Longino), *entangled* with *value* (Putnam), and *fallible* (the *fallibilist* *consensus*). The *thin* *conception* is the *best* *available*.
- **The error theory about knowledge** is the *analogue* of *Mackie’s* *moral* *error* *theory*. It is *self-undermining* (the *assertion* *presupposes* the *norm* it *denies*), and the *replies* are *unstable*.
- **Scepticism as a *position*** is *self-undermining*; **scepticism as a *practice*** is *indispensable*. The *confusion* of the *two* is the *source* of much *confusion*, and the *distinction* is the *key* to a *durable* *anti-sceptical* *stance*.
- **The durable stance** is *particularist* (start with *instances*), *equilibrist* (adjust to *coherence*), *social* (calibrate through *criticism*), *fallibilist* (accept *revisability*), and *virtuous* (cultivate *humility*, *courage*, *honesty*). It *does* not *claim* *certainty*, a *refutation* of the *sceptic*, a *foundation*, or a *view* *from* *nowhere*. It *does* claim that the *practice* of *inquiry* is *justified* — by its *track* *record*, its *self-correction*, and its *fruits*.
- **Part II ends** with a *hinge*: the *recognition* that *inquiry* is *possible* *despite* the *criterion* *problem*, and that *knowledge* is *real* *despite* the *sceptical* *challenge*. The *hinge* is the *starting* *point* of *metaphysics*.
