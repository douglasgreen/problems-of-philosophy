# Chapter 7 — The Analysis of Knowledge

> “This kind of acquaintance, which may be called self-consciousness, is the source of all our knowledge of mental things.”
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912), Ch. V

> “To know something is to have a true belief that is not an accident.”
> — A paraphrase of the intuition behind every analysis since Plato, and the source of every problem in this chapter

---

Chapter 6 set out a paradox: three principles, each plausible, which cannot all be true. Every response to the sceptic was a claim about which principle to give up — and every such claim presupposes an answer to a more basic question: **what is knowledge?**

That question is the subject of this chapter. It is the most analysed concept in the history of philosophy. It has been the target of the most productive twenty years in twentieth-century epistemology, and of the most frustrating fifty. And it is the clearest case in the book of a philosophical problem that is *both* a live research programme *and* a candidate for dissolution.

We proceed in eight movements. §1 presents the classical analysis of knowledge as justified true belief, and examines each of its three conditions. §2 presents Gettier’s counterexamples and the general argument they generate. §3 surveys the fourth-condition industry that followed. §4 presents the knowledge-first alternative, which rejects the project of analysis altogether. §5 presents virtue epistemology, the most promising contemporary synthesis. §6 examines the semantics of “knows” — contextualism, relativism, and pragmatic encroachment. §7 takes up the value problem, which many think is the deepest issue in the area. §8 gives an honest verdict on the state of the debate.

---

## 1. The Classical Triad

### 1.1 The prehistory: the *Theaetetus*

The analysis of knowledge as justified true belief is often attributed to Plato, and the attribution is *nearly* right. The *Theaetetus* (c. 369 BCE) is a sustained inquiry into the nature of knowledge, and it contains the triad — though it does not state it as an analysis, and it ends in *aporia*.

Plato’s dialogue proceeds through three definitions:

1. **Knowledge is perception** (151e–187a). Refuted by the relativity of perception — the same thing looks different from different vantage points — and by the **dream argument** (187b–188d): if a prisoner in a jail might dream that he is free, then perception can mislead.
2. **Knowledge is true belief** (187a–201c). Refuted by the **jury case**: a jury, persuaded by a clever advocate, may form a true belief about a crime — but they do not *know*, because they were not eyewitnesses (201a–c). True belief is not sufficient for knowledge.
3. **Knowledge is true belief with an account** (*logos*) (201c–210a). Refuted by the **dilemma**: the account is either of what is already known (circular) or of something distinct (then it does not make the belief knowledge).

The dialogue ends without a definition: “knowledge is neither perception, nor true belief, nor true belief with an account” (210a, paraphrased). The *aporia* is not a failure; it is the demonstration that the obvious answers do not work.

The **jury case** deserves emphasis. It is, in effect, a **proto-Gettier case**: the jury has justified true belief (the advocate’s argument is good, the belief is true) but lacks knowledge, because the justification is not *connected* to the truth in the right way. Plato saw the problem, and the *Theaetetus* is the first text to feel its force. What Plato did not have was the *general recipe* — the demonstration that the problem is not a quirk of the jury case but a structural feature of any fallibilist analysis.

**The received view.** For most of the twentieth century, the standard formulation was:

$$
(\mathrm{JTB}) \quad K_S p \leftrightarrow (J_S p \wedge T p \wedge B_S p)
$$

where $K_S p$ = “$S$ knows that $p$,” $J_S p$ = “$S$ is justified in believing that $p$,” $T p$ = “$p$ is true,” and $B_S p$ = “$S$ believes that $p$.” The conditions were held to be individually necessary and jointly sufficient.

The formulation is *deceptively* simple. Each condition is a site of controversy, and the controversies are not independent.

### 1.2 The belief condition

**The condition.** If $S$ knows that $p$, then $S$ believes that $p$. Formally: $K_S p \to B_S p$.

**The argument.** Knowledge is a *cognitive achievement* — it is something the subject does or attains. If the subject does not believe that $p$, the question “does she know it?” does not arise. To know is to be right about something one has affirmed; a subject who is indifferent to $p$, or who denies it, cannot be said to know it.

**The challenge: knowledge without belief.**

- **The French lessons case** (Radford 1966). Jean, an Englishman, has taken French lessons and is genuinely competent. But he is convinced he does not know French, and if asked whether he knows French, he sincerely denies it. In a test, he answers correctly. Radford’s claim: Jean *knows* French, but does not *believe* he does. Therefore knowledge does not entail belief.
- **Animal knowledge.** A dog can know where its food is; does it *believe*? If belief requires conceptual content, and dogs lack concepts, then dogs can know without believing. (The argument is contested: many philosophers hold that animals have beliefs, in a thin sense.)
- **Implicit knowledge.** A skilled driver knows how to change gear, but may not have the occurrent belief “this is how one changes gear.” If knowledge requires only dispositional belief, the case is unproblematic; if it requires occurrent belief, the case is a counterexample.

**The standard replies.** (i) The French lessons case shows that Jean *believes* he does not know, not that he does not believe the propositions he answers. His behaviour is evidence of a dispositional belief that his avowal does not report. (ii) The avowal may be a *performance error*: Jean is wrong about his own mental states, which is not the same as lacking the states. (iii) The case may show that “knowledge” is *graded* — Jean has some knowledge, but not the kind that supports the avowal. The replies are not decisive, but the consensus is that the belief condition survives, in a dispositional form.

**Why it matters.** If knowledge does not entail belief, the entire JTB framework is wrong at the first step, and the Gettier problem is a problem about a concept that does not have the structure the analysis attributes to it. The debate about the belief condition is therefore not a side issue; it is a test of whether the analysis is the right *kind* of project.

### 1.3 The truth condition

**The condition.** If $S$ knows that $p$, then $p$ is true. Formally: $K_S p \to p$. This is **factivity**, and it is the one condition on which there is near-universal agreement.

**The argument.** Knowledge is *factive*: you cannot know something false. If you believe that the earth is flat, and you are justified, and it is false, you do not *know* — you are simply mistaken. The concept of knowledge is the concept of *getting it right*; a false “knowledge” is a contradiction in terms.

**The challenges.**

- **The fallibilist challenge.** If knowledge requires truth, and our evidence never guarantees truth, then knowledge requires a kind of luck: the belief must happen to be true. Fallibilism accepts this — knowledge does not require certainty, only truth plus adequate justification — and the acceptance is what generates the Gettier problem (§2).
- **The “knowledge in science” challenge.** Scientific theories are accepted as knowledge and later rejected. Does this show that knowledge does not require truth, or that the theories were never knowledge? The consensus: the latter. “Newtonian mechanics was known to be true” was a loose way of saying “Newtonian mechanics was accepted as true”; the strict sense of “know” requires truth, and the strict sense is the one that matters for the analysis.
- **The “knowledge of fiction” challenge.** We say “I know that Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street.” This is true only in the fiction; the strict sense requires the truth of the proposition *within the relevant domain*. The case is a useful reminder that “knows” is context-sensitive in ways that the analysis must accommodate (§6).

**Factivity and the sceptic.** The sceptical argument of Ch. 6, §1.2 is *modus tollens* on closure: if I cannot know $\neg\mathrm{BIV}$, and if knowing $H$ would require knowing $\neg\mathrm{BIV}$, then I do not know $H$. The factivity condition is not the problem; the closure principle is. This is worth noting because it shows that the analysis of knowledge is not a neutral framework: the choice of conditions determines which responses to scepticism are available.

### 1.4 The justification condition

**The condition.** If $S$ knows that $p$, then $S$ is justified in believing that $p$. Formally: $K_S p \to J_S p$.

**The argument.** Knowledge is not mere true belief. A lucky guess is true, but it is not knowledge; a belief formed by superstition may be true, but it is not knowledge. What distinguishes knowledge from lucky true belief is that the belief is *appropriately connected* to the truth — and “appropriately connected” is what the justification condition is meant to capture.

**What justification is.** This is the central question of Ch. 8, but we can state the options now, because the analysis of knowledge depends on which one is chosen.

| Conception | What justifies a belief | Paradigm | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Evidentialism** | The subject’s evidence | Conee & Feldman 1985 | What counts as evidence? |
| **Reliabilism** | The reliability of the process that produced it | Goldman 1979 | The generality problem |
| **Virtue** | The exercise of epistemic competence | Sosa 1980; Zagzebski 1996 | What is competence? |
| **Coherentism** | The belief’s coherence with the rest of the system | BonJour 1990 | The isolation objection |
| **Foundationalism** | The belief’s derivation from basic beliefs | Chisholm 1966 | The Sellarsian dilemma |
| **Proper functionalism** | The proper functioning of cognitive faculties | Plantinga 1993 | The “design plan” is speculative |

The choice matters for the Gettier problem because different conceptions of justification generate different counterexamples. The *classical* Gettier cases work against an evidentialist conception of justification. The *reliabilist* requires a different kind of case (the barn facades, §3.7). The *virtue* conception is designed to handle both (§5).

**The two-component structure.** Most analyses separate the *justification* condition into two parts:

1. **The ground**: what makes the belief justified (evidence, reliability, competence).
2. **The connection**: the relation between the ground and the truth of the belief.

The Gettier problem, on this reading, is a problem about the **connection**: the ground is present, the truth is present, but the connection is accidental. This is why the fourth-condition industry is a search for a condition on the connection.

### 1.5 The fallibilist assumption

The classical analysis is usually understood as **fallibilist**: it allows that a justified belief can be false. This is important, because it is the assumption that generates the Gettier problem.

If justification were **infallible** — if a justified belief could not be false — then Gettier cases would be impossible, because a justified true belief would be *guaranteed* to be knowledge. The classical foundationalists (Descartes, and the sense-datum theorists) held something like this view. The fallibilist consensus of the twentieth century is that infallibilism is untenable: our evidence does not guarantee truth, and knowledge is compatible with the possibility of error.

The fallibilist assumption is the price of the Gettier problem. The infallibilist avoids the problem by making knowledge unattainable. The debate between them is a debate about whether the concept of knowledge is *worth having* on the infallibilist reading — and most philosophers have concluded that it is not.

## 2. Gettier’s Counterexamples

### 2.1 The paper

Edmund Gettier’s “Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?” was published in *Analysis* in 1963. It is two and a half pages long. It contains two counterexamples to the JTB analysis, and it changed the course of epistemology.

The paper’s structure is simple. Gettier first notes two assumptions that the JTB analysis requires:

1. **Fallibilism**: it is possible for a person to be justified in believing a proposition that is false.
2. **Closure of justification under deduction**: if $S$ is justified in believing $p$, and $S$ deduces $q$ from $p$ and accepts $q$ on that basis, then $S$ is justified in believing $q$.

He then argues that these two assumptions, together with the JTB analysis, generate counterexamples.

### 2.2 Case I: the job and the coins

> “Suppose that Smith and Jones have applied for a certain job. And suppose that Smith has strong evidence for the following conjunctive proposition: (d) Jones is the man who will get the job, and Jones has ten coins in his pocket. Smith’s evidence for (d) might be that the president of the company assured him that Jones would in the end be selected, and that he, Smith, had counted the coins in Jones’s pocket ten minutes ago. Proposition (d) entails: (e) The man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. Let us suppose that Smith sees the entailment from (d) to (e), and accepts (e) on the grounds of (d), for which he has strong evidence. In this case, Smith is clearly justified in believing that (e) is true. But imagine, further, that unknown to Smith, he himself, not Jones, will get the job. And, also, unknown to Smith, he himself has ten coins in his pocket. Proposition (e) is then true, though proposition (d), from which Smith inferred (e), is false. In our example, then, all of the following are true: (i) (e) is true, (ii) Smith believes that (e) is true, and (iii) Smith is justified in believing that (e) is true. But it is equally clear that Smith does not *know* that (e) is true; for it is a matter of luck that the number of coins in Smith’s pocket is the same as the number in Jones’s.” (Gettier 1963, pp. 122–123)

**The structure.** The case has four features:

1. Smith has a justified belief in a false proposition $d$.
2. Smith deduces $e$ from $d$, and is justified in believing $e$ (by closure).
3. $e$ is true, but for reasons *unrelated* to Smith’s justification.
4. Smith does not know $e$.

The case is an instance of **justified true belief without knowledge**, and it refutes the JTB analysis if it is a genuine case.

### 2.3 Case II: the Ford and Barcelona

> “Let us suppose that Smith has strong evidence for the following proposition: (f) Jones owns a Ford. Smith’s evidence might be that Jones has at all times in the past within Smith’s memory owned a car, and always a Ford, and that Jones has just offered Smith a ride while driving a Ford. … Now let us imagine that Smith has another friend, Brown, of whose whereabouts he is totally ignorant. Smith selects three place names quite at random and constructs the following three propositions: (g) Either Jones owns a Ford, or Brown is in Boston; (h) Either Jones owns a Ford, or Brown is in Barcelona; (i) Either Jones owns a Ford, or Brown is in Brest-Litovsk. … Imagine that Jones does not own a Ford, but is at present driving a rented car. And imagine further that, by the most coincidental of circumstances, Brown is in Barcelona. Then, although Smith does not know that Brown is in Barcelona, he is justified in believing (h), and (h) is true.” (Gettier 1963, p. 123)

**The structure.** The case differs from Case I in the form of the inference: instead of *deducing* a consequence, Smith *constructs a disjunction* from a justified belief. The disjunction is true because one disjunct is true (Brown is in Barcelona), but the truth is not connected to Smith’s justification (which is about the other disjunct).

**Why both cases matter.** The two cases are structurally different:

| Feature | Case I | Case II |
|---|---|---|
| Inference | Deduction from a false conjunction | Disjunction introduction from a justified belief |
| The false premise | $d$ (Jones will get the job and has ten coins) | $f$ (Jones owns a Ford) |
| Why the conclusion is true | Coincidence about Smith’s coins | Coincidence about Brown’s location |
| Closure requirement | Closure under deduction | Closure under disjunction introduction |

The second case is more general, because closure under disjunction introduction is a weaker assumption than closure under deduction. This is what makes the Gettier problem *structural*: it does not depend on the particular form of the inference.

### 2.4 The general recipe

Linda Zagzebski (1994) gave the general form of the Gettier problem, which is one of the most important results in the literature.

**The recipe.**

1. **Start** with a case of justified false belief: $S$ is justified in believing $p$, and $p$ is false.
2. **Construct** a proposition $q$ such that $S$ is justified in believing $p \vee q$ (by disjunction introduction), or $\exists x\, Fx$ (by existential generalisation), or some other logical consequence.
3. **Arrange** the world so that $q$ is true (for reasons unrelated to $S$’s justification).
4. Then $S$ has justified true belief in the disjunction $p \vee q$, but not knowledge — because the truth of $q$, which is what makes the disjunction true, is not connected to $S$’s justification.

**The theorem.** Zagzebski’s conclusion: **any** analysis that accepts (i) fallibilism, (ii) closure of justification under the relevant logical operations, and (iii) the JTB structure is vulnerable to this recipe. The Gettier problem is not a quirk of two examples; it is a **structural consequence** of the fallibilist framework.

**The escapes.** There are three ways out:

1. **Deny fallibilism.** If justification is infallible, the recipe cannot get started. (This is the infallibilist option, and it is costly.)
2. **Deny closure.** If justification is not closed under disjunction introduction (or deduction), the recipe fails. (This is the “relevant alternatives” / “no closure” option, and it is also costly: Ch. 6, §5.)
3. **Add a condition that is not analysable in JTB terms.** This is the fourth-condition industry, and Zagzebski argues that it cannot succeed, because any such condition will itself be subject to the recipe.

The third option is the one that the literature has pursued, and the debate about whether Zagzebski is right is the central methodological question in the field.

### 2.5 The Gettier industry: a methodological reflection

The literature that followed Gettier’s paper is enormous — hundreds of articles, dozens of proposed analyses, and a whole sub-industry of counterexamples and replies. It is worth pausing to ask: **is this progress?**

The case for **yes**: the industry has produced a much sharper understanding of the conditions of knowledge; it has generated the distinction between justification and warrant; it has produced the virtue epistemology and knowledge-first programmes; it has clarified the relation between knowledge and luck.

The case for **no**: the industry has the structure of a **patching** exercise (Ch. 3, §1.6). Each analysis is refuted by a new case, and each new case is handled by a new clause, which is in turn refuted by a yet newer case. The process has the appearance of progress without the substance: the *number* of clauses increases, but the *adequacy* of the analysis does not.

The honest assessment is that both are partly right. The industry has produced genuine conceptual advances (the distinction between *justification* and *warrant*, the notion of *safety*, the virtue-theoretic framework), and it has also produced a great deal of ad hoc patching. The methodological lesson is the one Ch. 3, §1.6 stated: **a theory that has been patched ten times is on the defensive.** The question is whether the defence is *principled* — whether each clause has independent motivation — or merely *reactive*.

### Workshop: the Gettier generator

The recipe is best learned by using it. Here is a template, with three worked instances.

**Template.** Let $p$ be a proposition that $S$ is justified in believing but that is false. Let $q$ be a proposition whose truth is independent of $S$'s justification. Form the disjunction $p \vee q$ (or the existential $\exists x\, Fx$ where $S$'s evidence concerns a particular $a$ that is $F$ but not the thing that makes the existential true). The result is a Gettier case if:

1. $S$ is justified in believing the disjunction (closure);
2. the disjunction is true (because $q$ is true);
3. the truth is not connected to $S$'s justification;
4. $S$ does not know the disjunction.

**Instance 1 (the coins).** $p$ = “Jones will get the job and has ten coins”; $q$ = “Smith has ten coins and will get the job”; disjunction = “the man who will get the job has ten coins.”

**Instance 2 (the Ford).** $p$ = “Jones owns a Ford”; $q$ = “Brown is in Barcelona”; disjunction = “Jones owns a Ford or Brown is in Barcelona.”

**Instance 3 (your own).** Choose a domain in which you have justified false beliefs — a scientific theory you accept that is about to be refuted, a person you trust who is about to betray you, a map you are using that is slightly wrong. Construct the disjunction. Ask: is the resulting case a Gettier case? If not, why not? If so, what does the analysis need to add to exclude it?

**The methodological point.** The generator shows that Gettier cases are *cheap*: they can be manufactured in any domain in which fallible justification is possible. This is why the fourth-condition industry has not converged: the supply of counterexamples is inexhaustible. The question is not whether an analysis can handle *this* case, but whether it has a *principled* account of the connection between justification and truth — and that is a question about the nature of justification, not about the number of clauses.

## 3. The Fourth Condition

The literature since 1963 has proposed a bewildering variety of fourth conditions. We can group them into five families.

### 3.1 No false lemmas

**The condition.** $S$ knows that $p$ only if the justification for $p$ does not depend on any false lemma. Formally: no essential premise in the chain of justification is false.

**The motivation.** Both of Gettier’s cases involve a false premise: $d$ in Case I, $f$ in Case II. If the false premise is excluded, both cases are excluded.

**Proponents.** Chisholm (1977), Shope (1975), and many others.

**The objection.** The condition handles the original cases but not the **barn facade** case (Goldman 1976): Henry is driving through a county full of fake barns (cardboard facades). He looks at one and believes “that is a barn.” The belief is true (it happens to be a real barn), and the justification does not depend on a false lemma (his visual experience is veridical). But he does not know, because he would have believed the same thing if he had looked at any of the facades. The case shows that the problem is not *false* lemmas but *unreliable* ones; the condition needs to be strengthened.

**The generality problem.** The barn case also shows that the no-false-lemma condition is sensitive to how the justification is *described*. If the justification is “I see a barn-shaped object,” the lemma is true and the condition is satisfied. If the justification is “I see a barn,” the lemma may be false (in the counterfactual sense: he cannot discriminate barns from facades). The choice of description is the choice of the “relevant” process, and that is the generality problem (§3.4 and §3.5).

### 3.2 Defeasibility

**The condition.** $S$ knows that $p$ only if there is no *defeater* — no true proposition $d$ such that, if $S$ were to believe $d$, $S$ would no longer be justified in believing $p$.

**The motivation.** The Gettier cases can be described as cases in which there is a defeater that $S$ does not possess: in Case I, the defeater is “Smith will get the job”; in Case II, the defeater is “Jones does not own a Ford.” If the analysis requires the *absence* of such defeaters (not merely the absence of *known* defeaters), the cases are excluded.

**Proponents.** Lehrer and Paxson (1969), Klein (1981), Pollock (1986), and many others.

**The objections.** (i) **The “defeaters” are too powerful**: if any true proposition that would undermine the justification is a defeater, then almost no belief is knowledge, because there is almost always *some* true proposition that would undermine it (the sceptical hypothesis, the evil demon, the possibility of error). The condition needs a restriction, and the restriction is hard to state without circularity. (ii) **The “false defeater” problem**: if the defeater is false (as in the case of a misleading but false piece of evidence), the condition may exclude knowledge that should be included. (iii) **The “generality” problem again**: the condition is sensitive to the description of the defeater, and the description is underdetermined.

### 3.3 The causal theory

**The condition.** $S$ knows that $p$ only if the fact that $p$ is *causally connected* to $S$’s belief that $p$.

**The motivation.** In the Gettier cases, the truth of the belief is not causally connected to the justification: Smith’s belief that the man who will get the job has ten coins is caused by his evidence about Jones, not by the fact that Smith will get the job. If knowledge requires a causal connection, the cases are excluded.

**Proponents.** Goldman (1967), and the causal theorists of the 1960s and 1970s.

**The objections.** (i) **The problem of abstracta**: mathematical knowledge, logical knowledge, and knowledge of the future are not plausibly causal. (ii) **The problem of generality**: the causal connection must be of the *right kind*, and the kind is hard to specify without the generality problem. (iii) **The problem of deviant causal chains**: a belief can be causally connected to the fact in a way that does not constitute knowledge (e.g., the fact that $p$ causes a brain state that causes the belief, but the subject has no access to the connection). The theory was largely abandoned in the 1980s, though its descendants (the “causal-informational” theories of Dretske and Fodor) survive in the philosophy of mind.

### 3.4 Tracking

**The condition.** $S$ knows that $p$ only if $S$’s belief *tracks* the truth: if $p$ were false, $S$ would not believe it; if $p$ were true, $S$ would believe it (Nozick 1981).

**Formal statement.** Using the counterfactual conditional $\Box\!\to$:

$$
(\mathrm{Sensitivity}) \quad \neg p \ \Box\!\to\ \neg B_S p
$$

$$
(\mathrm{Adherence}) \quad p \ \Box\!\to\ B_S p
$$

**The motivation.** In the Gettier cases, the belief is not sensitive: if $p$ were false, Smith would still believe it (his evidence would be the same). The tracking condition excludes the cases.

**The objections.** (i) **The generality problem**: the counterfactual must be evaluated with respect to a *method*, and the method is underdetermined. (ii) **The conjunction problem**: sensitivity is not preserved under conjunction — the conjunction of two sensitive beliefs need not itself be sensitive, so the tracking theory cannot say that knowledge is closed under conjunction. (iii) **The “abominable conjunction”**: Nozick’s own example makes the point vivid — it would be abominable if someone knew both that they had hands and that they were not a brain in a vat, yet did not know the conjunction. (iv) **The sceptical residue**: the theory concedes that we do not know $\neg\mathrm{BIV}$, which many find unacceptable (Ch. 6, §5.3).

The tracking theory is important because it is the first *modal* analysis of knowledge, and it set the stage for the safety theory.

### 3.5 Safety

**The condition.** $S$ knows that $p$ only if $S$’s belief is *safe*: in all (or most) nearby possible worlds in which $S$ believes $p$, $p$ is true.

**Formal statement.** Let $\mathcal{N}(w)$ be the set of worlds near $w$:

$$
(\mathrm{Safety}) \quad \forall w' \in \mathcal{N}(w)\, [B_S p \text{ at } w' \to p \text{ at } w']
$$

**The motivation.** Safety handles the barn facade case: in nearby worlds, Henry believes “that is a barn” while looking at a facade, and the belief is false. So the belief is unsafe, and knowledge is excluded. Safety also handles the Gettier cases: in nearby worlds, Smith’s belief is false (the coincidence does not obtain), so the belief is unsafe.

**Proponents.** Sosa (1999, 2007), Pritchard (2005, 2012), Williamson (2000), and many others.

**The objections.** (i) **The “nearby” problem**: the safety condition requires a similarity ordering on worlds, and the ordering is contested (Ch. 37, §3). (ii) **The “method” problem**: safety is sensitive to the *method* by which the belief is formed, and the method is underdetermined (the generality problem again). (iii) **The “safety” vs. “sensitivity” debate**: safety and sensitivity give different verdicts in some cases, and the choice between them is contested. (iv) **The “lottery” problem**: a belief that a lottery ticket will lose can be safe (in nearby worlds, the ticket loses) even though it is not knowledge. (The lottery problem is a serious challenge to safety, and it has generated a large literature.)

**Safety and scepticism.** Safety is the most important anti-luck condition in contemporary epistemology, and it is the basis of the most influential response to scepticism (Ch. 6, §5.4). The connection is direct: if knowledge requires safety, and if the BIV world is *not* nearby, then ordinary perceptual beliefs are safe even if they are not sensitive.

### 3.6 The comparative table

| Fourth condition | Core idea | Handles Gettier? | Handles barn? | Main problem |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **No false lemmas** | No false premise in the justification | Yes | No | The generality problem; the description of the lemma |
| **Defeasibility** | No true proposition would undermine | Yes | Contested | The defeater is too powerful; the restriction is circular |
| **Causal** | The fact causes the belief | Yes | No | Abstracta; deviant causal chains; generality |
| **Tracking** | The belief is sensitive | Yes | Yes | Conjunction problem; generality; sceptical residue |
| **Safety** | The belief could not easily be false | Yes | Yes | The “nearby” and “method” problems; lottery |
| **Virtue** | The truth is creditable to competence | Yes | Yes (with adjustment) | The credit question; environmental luck (§5.5) |

The table is not a verdict. It is a map of the trade-offs. The reader who wants to choose a condition must decide which cost is acceptable — and the decision is a philosophical argument, not a matter of taste.

Two entries in the “barn” column deserve a word. The **causal theory** fails the barn case for a reason that is worth stating plainly: in the county of fake barns, Henry’s belief *is* caused by the real barn, so the causal condition is satisfied, and the causal theory therefore counts it as knowledge. That is exactly the objection Goldman pressed in 1976, and it is why the causal theory was abandoned. The **defeater** analysis is more delicate: it turns on whether one counts a proposition such as “most of the structures in this county are facades” as an undefeated defeater of Henry’s justification. If one does, the condition excludes the case; if one does not, the case stands as a counterexample. That is why the entry is marked *contested* rather than *yes* or *no*.

### 3.7 The “barn facade” and the limits of the industry

The barn facade case is worth dwelling on, because it shows the limits of the fourth-condition project.

**The case** (Goldman 1976). Henry is driving through a county that contains many fake barns — cardboard facades that look exactly like barns. He looks at one of the few real barns and says “that is a barn.” His belief is true, justified (his visual experience is veridical), and he has no false lemmas. But he does not know, because he cannot discriminate real barns from facades.

**Why it is hard.** The case is a **veritic** luck case: the belief is true, but its truth is due to the *environment*, not to the subject’s competence. The subject’s cognitive faculties are functioning well; the world is arranged to defeat them. This is different from the Gettier cases, where the luck is in the *connection* between the justification and the truth. The fourth-condition industry was designed for Gettier luck; the barn case shows that there is another kind of luck, and the conditions must handle both.

**The moral.** The analysis of knowledge is not a single problem but a family of problems, each generated by a different kind of luck. The “Gettier problem” is the *first* of them, not the only one. A solution to the Gettier problem that does not address environmental luck is not a solution to the *problem of knowledge*; it is a solution to a *part* of it.

## 4. Knowledge-First Epistemology

### 4.1 The programme

**Knowledge-first epistemology** is the view, developed most systematically by Timothy Williamson (2000), that knowledge is not analysable into more basic components — belief, truth, justification — but is itself a fundamental epistemic notion.

The programme has four core claims:

1. **Knowledge is factive.** $K_S p \to p$.
2. **Knowledge is not analysable.** The project of the JTB analysis, and of the fourth-condition industry, is misguided: there is no decomposition of knowledge into independent conditions.
3. **Knowledge is a state of mind**, not a species of belief. It is a factive mental state, with belief as its “inner” component. (Williamson’s slogan: “Knowing is a state of mind.”)
4. **Knowledge is the norm of assertion and practical reasoning.** One should assert $p$ only if one knows $p$; one should use $p$ as a premise in practical reasoning only if one knows $p$.

The programme is a **dissolution** (Ch. 3, §7) of the Gettier problem: the problem is not solved; the project that generated it is abandoned.

### 4.2 The arguments

**(i) The norm of assertion.** Williamson (2000, Ch. 11) argues that the speech act of assertion is governed by a **knowledge norm**: assert $p$ only if you know $p$. The argument is an inference to the best explanation of the conversational data: the knowledge norm explains the propriety of assertion, the impropriety of asserting lottery propositions (Ch. 33, §1.6), and the connection between assertion and inquiry better than the alternatives (the truth norm, the justified-belief norm, the reasonable-belief norm).

**(ii) The norm of practical reasoning.** Williamson (2000, Introduction §1 and Ch. 11) argues that knowledge is the norm of practical reasoning: you may use $p$ as a premise in practical reasoning only if you know $p$. The argument is that the knowledge norm explains the “epistemic” constraint on action better than the alternatives.

**(iii) The anti-luminosity argument.** Williamson (2000, Ch. 4) argues that there are no **luminous** mental states — states such that, if one is in them, one is in a position to know one is. The argument is a sorites: for any mental state that comes in degrees (e.g., feeling cold), there is a margin of error such that, if one is in the state, one cannot reliably discriminate it from a slightly different state. The conclusion: knowledge of one’s own mental states is not a special, infallible kind of knowledge; it is subject to the same margin-of-error constraints as knowledge of the external world.

The argument is important because it undercuts the **Cartesian** picture of self-knowledge (Ch. 12, §6) and the **internalist** picture of justification (Ch. 8, §5). If there are no luminous states, then the internalist’s demand for access to one’s own justification is unattainable.

**(iv) The “knowledge first” in philosophy of mind.** Williamson (2000, Ch. 2) argues that knowing is a mental state in its own right — “the most general factive mental state” — rather than a species of belief. Because the state is externalist, this has consequences for how the contents of thought are individuated: the contents of our beliefs are not fixed independently of our environment. This is a radical claim, and it has been widely discussed.

### 4.3 Objections

**(i) The “unanalysability” claim is hard to assess.** If knowledge is unanalysable, what is the argument for it? Williamson’s argument is largely negative: the analysis fails, and the alternatives are worse. But “the analysis fails” does not entail “knowledge is primitive”; it entails only that we have not found the right analysis.

**(ii) The knowledge norm of assertion has counterexamples.** The “lottery” cases (you can assert “I will not win the lottery” even though you do not know it), the “selfless assertion” cases (you can assert something false for a good cause), and the “dogmatism” cases (you can assert what you believe without knowing) are all cited as counterexamples. The replies (the “contextualist” norm, the “interest-relative” norm) are contested.

**(iii) The anti-luminosity argument is contested.** Critics have argued that the argument proves too much: it would undermine the possibility of *any* knowledge of one’s own mental states, including the knowledge that one is in pain. Williamson’s reply is that it undermines only *luminous* knowledge, not all self-knowledge; the debate is technical.

**(iv) The “knowledge first” is a research programme, not a result.** The programme has generated a great deal of work, and it has not (yet) produced a settled account of knowledge. Whether it is a *better* programme than the analysis is a judgment about fruitfulness (Ch. 4, §3.1), and the judgment is contested.

### 4.4 What knowledge-first does to the Gettier project

The knowledge-first response to Gettier is not a fourth condition; it is a **change of subject**. The question “what is the fourth condition?” is replaced by the question “what is the role of knowledge in assertion, action, and inquiry?” The Gettier cases become *data* about the concept of knowledge — evidence that the concept is not decomposable in the way the analysis assumed — rather than problems to be solved.

This is a **dissolution** in the sense of Ch. 3, §7, and it must be assessed by the test of a good dissolution: does it explain why the problem seemed compelling? The knowledge-first answer: the problem seemed compelling because we assumed that knowledge must be analysable into more basic components, and the assumption is a *philosophical* prejudice, not a datum. Whether the explanation is satisfying is a matter of philosophical temperament; the reader should notice that the dissolution is *itself* a substantive thesis, and it is subject to the same demand for argument as the analyses it rejects.

## 5. Virtue Epistemology

### 5.1 The core idea

**Virtue epistemology** is the view that knowledge is to be understood in terms of the **intellectual virtues** — the competences, skills, and character traits that make a person good at inquiry.

The view has two sources. The first is **Aristotelian**: the virtues are excellences of character, and knowledge is the exercise of epistemic excellence. The second is **reliabilist**: the virtues are reliable belief-forming dispositions, and knowledge is the output of reliable processes. The synthesis is the view that knowledge is **true belief that is creditable to the agent’s epistemic competence**.

The canonical statements are Sosa (1980, 2007, 2011), Zagzebski (1996), Greco (2010), and Pritchard (2012).

### 5.2 Sosa: the AAA structure

Sosa’s **AAA** model:

| Level | Name | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | **Accuracy** | The belief is true | The archer hits the target |
| **AA** | **Adroitness** | The belief is produced by competence | The archer shoots well |
| **AAA** | **Aptness** | The belief is true *because* it is produced by competence | The archer hits the target *because* she shoots well |

Knowledge, on Sosa’s view, is **apt belief**: true belief that is true *because* of the exercise of competence. The structure is meant to solve the Gettier problem: in a Gettier case, the belief is accurate and adroit, but not *apt* — the truth is not explained by the competence.

The **AAA** model is elegant, and it has been widely influential. Its key virtue is that it makes the connection between justification and truth *explanatory*: the competence explains the truth, and that is what knowledge is.

### 5.3 Zagzebski: credit

Zagzebski’s (1996) **credit** theory: $S$ knows that $p$ iff $S$ has a true belief that $p$, and $S$ deserves *credit* for the truth. The credit is earned by the exercise of intellectual virtue.

The **credit** condition is stronger than Sosa’s aptness: it requires that the agent be *praiseworthy* for the truth, not merely that the truth be caused by her competence. The difference matters in cases where the agent’s competence is exercised but the truth is overdetermined, or where the agent is not responsible for the competence (e.g., innate talent).

**The objection.** The credit condition may be too strong: in cases of **testimonial knowledge** (Ch. 11, §1), the agent’s competence is the *acceptance* of testimony, and the credit for the truth belongs to the testifier, not the believer. If credit is required, testimonial knowledge is not knowledge. The reply (Greco 2010): credit can be *distributed*; the believer is creditable for the *acceptance*, even if the testifier is creditable for the *content*.

### 5.4 Greco: achievement

Greco’s (2010) **achievement** theory: knowledge is a **cognitive achievement** — a success that is explained by the agent’s ability. The theory is a development of Sosa’s, with an emphasis on the *explanatory* relation: the agent’s ability must *explain* the success, not merely be *present* in its production.

Greco’s key distinction is between **achievement** and **lucky success**:

| Success | Explained by ability? | Knowledge? |
|---|---|---|
| Achievement | Yes | Yes |
| Lucky success | No | No |
| Success despite inability | No | No |

The distinction is meant to capture the Gettier intuition: in a Gettier case, the success is not explained by the agent’s ability; it is explained by the coincidence. In the barn facade case, the success is explained by the ability *plus* the environment, and the question is whether the environment’s contribution is enough to defeat the achievement.

### 5.5 Pritchard: the anti-luck virtue epistemology

Pritchard (2012) proposes a **two-condition** analysis:

1. **The ability condition**: the belief is the product of the agent’s epistemic competence.
2. **The anti-luck condition**: the belief is safe (in the sense of §3.5).

The two conditions are meant to be **jointly sufficient** for knowledge. The rationale:

- The **ability condition** handles the Gettier cases (the truth is not creditable to competence).
- The **anti-luck condition** handles the **environmental luck** cases (the barn facade: the competence is exercised, but the belief is not safe).

The account is called **anti-luck virtue epistemology** (ALVE), and it is one of the most discussed positions in the contemporary literature.

**Objections.** (i) The two conditions may *conflict*: in some cases, the ability condition is satisfied but the anti-luck condition is not (the barn facade), and in others, the anti-luck condition is satisfied but the ability condition is not (the "lucky" cases). ALVE handles both by requiring both, but the *justification* for requiring both is a matter of argument, not of intuition. (ii) The account is *not* a reductive analysis: it takes “competence” and “safety” as primitives, and the question is whether they are more basic than knowledge. (iii) The “credit” question: does the agent deserve credit for the truth in the cases ALVE classifies as knowledge? The answer is not obvious.

### 5.6 The value turn

The virtue-theoretic accounts are motivated, in large part, by the **value problem** (§7): they offer an explanation of why knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief. The explanation: knowledge is an *achievement*, and achievements are valuable in a way that lucky successes are not. The value is not a separate condition; it is the value of *agency* — of getting things right *because of one’s competence*.

Whether this is a solution to the value problem is the subject of §7.

## 6. Contextualism, Relativism, and Pragmatic Encroachment

### 6.1 Contextualism

**Contextualism** is the view that the truth conditions of “$S$ knows that $p$” depend on the **context of the attributor** — the person making the knowledge claim. In ordinary contexts, the standards are low; in philosophical contexts, they are high.

The canonical statements are Cohen (1986, 1988), DeRose (1992, 1995), and Lewis (1996). The **standard** version has three components:

1. **The semantic thesis**: “knows” is an indexical or context-sensitive expression, like “here” or “tall.”
2. **The relevance thesis**: the context determines which alternatives are *relevant*; knowledge requires the elimination of relevant alternatives only.
3. **The sceptical solution**: the sceptic is right in the philosophical context and wrong in the ordinary context; the paradox of Ch. 6, §1.3 is resolved by relativising the principles to contexts.

**The bank cases** (DeRose 1992, 1995) are the standard illustration. Case A: I am at the bank on Friday, and I need to deposit a check; I know the bank will be open on Saturday because I was there two weeks ago on a Saturday. Case B: the check is large, and if the bank is not open on Saturday I will default on my mortgage; the same evidence no longer seems sufficient for knowledge. The contextualist explains the difference by the *attributor’s* standards: in Case A, the standards are low; in Case B, they are high.

**The objections.** (i) The **“semantic” objection**: the evidence for the context-sensitivity of “knows” is contested; the experimental literature is mixed (§6.4). (ii) The **“ad hoc” objection**: the posited context-sensitivity is not independently motivated. (iii) The **“stability” objection**: if the sceptical context is always available, the ordinary knowledge is always vulnerable (the “fragility” objection). (iv) The **“dissolution” objection**: contextualism explains away the sceptical question rather than answering it (Ch. 6, §6.4).

### 6.2 Relativism

**Relativism** (MacFarlane 2005, 2014) is a stronger thesis: the truth of “$S$ knows that $p$” depends on the **context of the assessor** — the person evaluating the claim — not the attributor. The view is a form of **assessment-sensitivity**, and it is motivated by the **“faultless disagreement”** (Kölbel 2010) and **“retraction”** data:

- **Faultless disagreement**: two people can disagree about whether a subject knows, and both can be *right* — not in the sense that the truth is relative to a perspective, but in the sense that the truth-value is not fixed until the assessor’s context is specified.
- **Retraction**: a person who has made a knowledge claim can later *retract* it, saying “I was wrong to say I knew,” without implying that the original claim was false at the time. The retraction data are the strongest evidence for relativism.

**The objections.** (i) The view is *metaphysically* odd: it makes truth depend on the assessor, which is a strange kind of dependence. (ii) The data are contested: the retraction intuitions may be explained by a change in the subject’s *evidence*, not by a change in the truth conditions. (iii) The view is *unstable*: if truth is assessor-relative, what is the status of the relativist’s *own* claim? (This is a version of the self-refutation objection of Ch. 6, §3.3.)

### 6.3 Pragmatic encroachment

**Pragmatic encroachment** is the view that whether a subject knows a proposition can depend on the **practical stakes** of the situation — not on the subject’s evidence, but on what is *at issue* for the subject.

The canonical statements are Hawthorne (2004), Stanley (2005), and Fantl and McGrath (2009). The **bank cases** are again the standard illustration: in the low-stakes case, the subject knows; in the high-stakes case, she does not, even though her evidence is the same.

**The two versions.**

- **Interest-relative invariantism (IRI)** (Stanley 2005): the truth conditions of “knows” are invariant, but *knowledge itself* is interest-relative. The subject’s practical interests are part of what determines whether she knows.
- **Contextualism about knowledge** (the view of §6.1): the *attributor’s* context determines the standards. IRI is a *subject*-sensitive view; contextualism is an *attributor*-sensitive view.

**The arguments.**

1. **The “action” argument** (Hawthorne 2004; Hawthorne & Stanley 2008): knowledge is the norm of practical reasoning; if the stakes are high, more is required for a belief to be usable as a premise in action, and *that* is the sense in which knowledge is stake-sensitive.
2. **The “ignorance” argument** (Stanley 2005): in high-stakes cases, subjects are reluctant to *assert* knowledge claims, and the reluctance tracks a genuine epistemic difference.
3. **The “lottery” argument** (Hawthorne 2004): lottery propositions are not knowledge, even though the evidence is strong; the explanation is that the practical stakes of the lottery are high (or that the *context* of the lottery raises the standards).

**The objections.** (i) The view is **counterintuitive**: knowledge seems to be a matter of evidence, not of stakes. (ii) The **“shifting”** objection: if knowledge shifts with stakes, then the same belief can be knowledge at one moment and not at another, without any change in the subject. (iii) The **“error theory”** objection: the folk concept of knowledge may be *invariantist*, and the pragmatic encroachment data may reflect *performance* rather than *competence*. (iv) The **“action” argument** is contested: the norm of practical reasoning may be *justification*, not knowledge (Fantl & McGrath 2009; Brown 2008).

### 6.4 The experimental evidence

The empirical literature on “knows” is large and contested. The most robust findings:

- **The epistemic side-effect effect** (Beebe & Buckwalter 2010): subjects are more inclined to attribute knowledge when the side effects of the belief are *bad* than when they are *good*. The finding is a version of the Knobe effect (Ch. 3, §6.3) applied to knowledge attributions.
- **The “stakes” effect**: subjects’ knowledge attributions are sensitive to the stakes of the *subject* (the bank cases), though the effect size varies across studies.
- **The “culture” effect**: some studies find cross-cultural variation in knowledge attributions; the findings are contested (the replication crisis applies here too).

**The philosophical upshot.** The experimental data are consistent with several views: contextualism, IRI, and a *deflationary* view on which the data reflect pragmatic *implicature* rather than semantic content. The data do not settle the semantic question; they constrain the options, and they raise the question of whether the *folk concept* of knowledge is the one the philosopher is analysing.

### 6.5 The comparative table

| View | What determines the truth of “S knows p” | Sceptical solution | Main problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Invariantism** | The subject’s evidence and reliability | Scepticism is true or false, not context-dependent | Cannot explain the bank cases |
| **Contextualism** | The attributor’s context | The sceptic is right in her context | The semantic evidence is contested |
| **Relativism** | The assessor’s context | The sceptic and the ordinary speaker can both be right | Metaphysically odd; self-refutation worry |
| **IRI** | The subject’s practical interests | Knowledge is stake-sensitive, so the sceptic is partly right | Counterintuitive; the “shifting” objection |
| **Knowledge-first** | Knowledge is primitive | The sceptical question is a category mistake | The dissolution is a substantive thesis |

The table is a map, not a verdict. The reader who wants to choose a view must decide which cost is acceptable — and the decision is a philosophical argument.

## 7. The Value Problem

### 7.1 The *Meno*

The value problem is stated by Plato in the *Meno* (97a–98b). Socrates asks: why is knowledge more valuable than true belief? Meno’s answer is that true belief is *just as useful* as knowledge — the man who truly believes the road to Larissa will get there just as well as the man who knows it. Socrates’ reply: knowledge is **tethered** — it is tied down by the “calculation of the cause” (*aitias logismos*), and so it is *stable*; true belief is like the statues of Daedalus, which run away unless they are tied down.

The *Meno* problem is the problem of explaining **why knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief**. The problem is not merely academic: it is the question of why the concept of knowledge *matters*.

### 7.2 The swamping problem

The contemporary formulation of the value problem is due to Kvanvig (2003) and Pritchard (2005). The **swamping** argument:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
1. & \text{Knowledge is justified true belief plus a fourth condition (or some analysis of the same form).} \\
2. & \text{True belief is valuable.} \\
3. & \text{The value of justification is \textit{instrumental} to true belief: justification is valuable because it tends to produce true belief.} \\
4. & \text{If justification’s value is instrumental to true belief, then once the belief is true, the justification adds no \textit{additional} value.} \\
\hline
5. & \therefore \text{Knowledge is not more valuable than true belief.}
\end{array}
$$

The argument is called **swamping** because the value of the truth *swamps* the value of the justification: once you have the truth, the justification has no further value to contribute. The conclusion is counterintuitive: knowledge *seems* more valuable than true belief, and the analysis must explain why.

**The structure of the problem.** The swamping argument is a *reductio* of the JTB analysis, or at least of the instrumentalist conception of justification. If the analysis is correct, and if justification is instrumentally valuable, then knowledge is not more valuable than true belief. Since knowledge *is* more valuable, one of the premises must be false.

### 7.3 The solutions

**(i) The virtue solution** (Sosa 2007; Zagzebski 2003; Greco 2010). Knowledge is an *achievement* — a success that is creditable to the agent. The value of knowledge is the value of *agency*: it is valuable to get things right *because of one’s competence*, not merely to get things right. The solution denies premise 3: justification is not merely instrumentally valuable; it is *constitutively* valuable, because it is the exercise of a competence.

**(ii) The understanding solution** (Kvanvig 2003). Knowledge is not the highest epistemic good; **understanding** is. Understanding is valuable in itself, because it is a *coherent* grasp of the relations among truths, and coherence is intrinsically valuable. The solution *rejects* the question: knowledge is not more valuable than true belief, because knowledge is not the highest good; understanding is.

**(iii) The “knowledge-first” solution** (Williamson 2000). Knowledge is the norm of assertion and practical reasoning; it is valuable because it is the *standard* that governs our epistemic practices. The value is not instrumental; it is *constitutive* of the practice of inquiry.

**(iv) The “secondary value” solution** (Pritchard 2010). Knowledge has *secondary* value: it is valuable because it is the *product* of a valuable process (inquiry), even if the product is not more valuable than true belief. The value is *derived* from the value of the process, not from the value of the product.

### 7.4 The value of knowledge in practice

The value problem is not merely theoretical. It connects to the practical question: **why does knowledge matter?**

- **Epistemic**: knowledge is the norm of assertion and practical reasoning (Williamson); it is what we *need* to act, to inquire, and to trust.
- **Moral**: knowledge is a condition of autonomy (Ch. 47, §4.3); it is what makes informed consent possible, and what makes testimony reliable.
- **Political**: knowledge is a condition of democracy (Ch. 47, §6); a citizenry that cannot distinguish knowledge from opinion cannot govern itself.
- **Existential**: knowledge is a condition of the examined life (Ch. 54, §4); the Socratic demand is to *know* what one is doing, not merely to believe it.

The value problem is the question of why the concept of knowledge *earns its keep*. The answers are plural, and the pluralism is honest: knowledge matters for different reasons in different domains, and the philosophical analysis should not pretend that there is a single answer.

## 8. The State of the Debate

### 8.1 What is settled

- **Factivity.** Knowledge requires truth. This is not contested, and it is the one condition that the analysis has not had to revise.
- **The inadequacy of JTB.** The Gettier cases are genuine counterexamples. The classical analysis is false.
- **The structural nature of the problem.** Zagzebski’s recipe shows that the problem is not a quirk of two cases; it is a consequence of fallibilism plus closure plus the JTB structure.
- **The importance of luck.** The analysis of knowledge is, in large part, the analysis of *epistemic luck* (§3.7; Ch. 12, §5.5).
- **The plurality of responses.** There is no consensus on the fourth condition, but there is a consensus on the *space* of options: no-false-lemmas, defeasibility, causal, tracking, safety, virtue, knowledge-first.

### 8.2 What is open

- **Whether knowledge is analysable.** The knowledge-first programme denies it; the virtue programme offers a non-reductive analysis; the fourth-condition industry assumes it.
- **Which fourth condition is correct.** The debate has not converged, and the structural argument (Zagzebski) suggests that it may not converge.
- **The semantics of “knows.”** Contextualism, relativism, IRI, and invariantism are all live, and the experimental evidence is contested.
- **The value problem.** The virtue, understanding, knowledge-first, and secondary-value solutions are all contested, and the problem may be a *cluster* of problems rather than a single one.
- **The relation between knowledge and action.** The pragmatic encroachment debate is the newest front, and it is unresolved.

### 8.3 The methodological lesson

The chapter is a case study in the method of Ch. 3 and Ch. 4.

- **Conceptual analysis** (§1 of Ch. 3) generated the classical analysis and the fourth-condition industry.
- **Thought experiments** (§2 of Ch. 3) generated the Gettier cases and the barn facade.
- **Reflective equilibrium** (§4 of Ch. 3) is the method by which the conditions and the cases are adjusted.
- **Negative capability** (§7 of Ch. 3) is the response of the knowledge-first programme: the problem may be a pseudo-problem generated by a false assumption about the analysability of knowledge.
- **The criteria of a good theory** (Ch. 4, §3) — especially simplicity and fertility — are the criteria by which the analyses are assessed.
- **The ethics of discourse** (Ch. 4, §4) is tested by the Gettier industry: the temptation to patch, to straw-man, and to overclaim is everywhere in the literature.

The honest verdict: the analysis of knowledge is a **research programme in transition**. It has produced genuine advances; it has not produced a settled answer; and the most interesting work is currently being done at the *meta*-level — asking whether the project of analysis is the right project, and what the concept of knowledge is *for*.

## Optional threads

Cross-cutting material, for the reader who wants to go deeper.

**1. The generality problem.** Every reliabilist and virtue-theoretic analysis faces the question: *which* process type is the relevant one? The problem is pressed by Conee and Feldman (1985, 1998), and it is the most serious technical challenge to the externalist programme. The proposed solutions (the “contextualist” solution, the “dispositional” solution, the “epistemic” solution) are all contested.

**2. The “knowledge without belief” debate.** Radford (1966), and the subsequent literature, is the best entry point into the question of whether belief is a condition of knowledge. The debate connects to the nature of implicit cognition (Ch. 30, §5) and to the self-knowledge literature (Ch. 12, §6).

**3. Epistemic injustice and the analysis of knowledge.** Fricker (2007) argues that the analysis of knowledge has neglected the *social* conditions of knowledge: whose testimony is believed, whose competence is recognized, and whose ignorance is excused. The “virtue” tradition has begun to incorporate this (Ch. 11, §4), but the analysis of knowledge has not yet fully absorbed it.

**4. Non-Western epistemologies.** The Indian *pramāṇa* tradition (Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, Buddhist) developed a sophisticated analysis of knowledge (*pramā*) that anticipates many of the contemporary debates: the *svataḥ prāmāṇya* (self-validity) debate about the default status of cognition, the *khyāti* (error) theories, and the *pramāṇa-samuccaya* theory of the sources of knowledge. These are systematic alternatives, not curiosities (Ch. 11, optional thread).

**5. Knowledge and artificial intelligence.** If knowledge requires belief, and belief requires consciousness, then AI systems cannot know. If knowledge is a functional state, they can. The debate about machine knowledge (Ch. 29, §3) is a test case for the analysis, and it has practical stakes for the design of AI systems.

## Summary

- The **classical analysis** of knowledge is justified true belief (JTB). Each condition — belief, truth, justification — is a site of controversy. The belief condition is challenged by Radford’s French-lessons case; the truth condition (factivity) is nearly uncontested; the justification condition is the subject of the internalism/externalism debate (Ch. 8).
- **Gettier’s cases** (1963) refute the JTB analysis. Case I (the job and the coins) and Case II (the Ford and Barcelona) are structurally different, but both show that justified true belief can fail to be knowledge.
- **Zagzebski’s recipe** shows that the Gettier problem is *structural*: any fallibilist analysis that accepts closure is vulnerable. The escapes are infallibilism, the denial of closure, or a condition that is not analysable in JTB terms.
- **The fourth-condition industry** has produced five families of conditions: no-false-lemmas, defeasibility, causal, tracking, and safety. Each handles the original Gettier cases; each has characteristic costs. The **barn facade** case shows that the problem is broader than Gettier luck: **environmental luck** is a distinct problem.
- **Knowledge-first epistemology** (Williamson) rejects the project of analysis. Knowledge is factive, unanalysable, and the norm of assertion and practical reasoning. The programme is a **dissolution** of the Gettier problem, and it must be assessed as a dissolution.
- **Virtue epistemology** (Sosa, Zagzebski, Greco, Pritchard) analyses knowledge as **creditable true belief** — the exercise of epistemic competence. The **AAA** structure (Sosa) and the **anti-luck virtue epistemology** (Pritchard) are the most developed versions. The virtue approach is motivated in part by the **value problem**.
- **The semantics of “knows”** is contested: contextualism, relativism, pragmatic encroachment (IRI), and invariantism are all live. The **bank cases** and the **experimental evidence** constrain the options without settling them.
- **The value problem** (the *Meno* problem): why is knowledge more valuable than mere true belief? The **swamping** argument makes the problem acute for the JTB analysis. The solutions — virtue, understanding, knowledge-first, secondary value — are contested, and the problem may be a cluster of problems rather than a single one.
- **The state of the debate**: factivity and the inadequacy of JTB are settled; the fourth condition, the analysability of knowledge, the semantics of “knows,” and the value problem are open. The chapter is a case study in the method of Part I, and in the limits of analysis.

4. Evaluate the no-false-lemmas condition against the barn facade case. What does the case show about the relation between justification and truth?
5. Is safety or sensitivity the better anti-luck condition? How does each handle the lottery problem?
6. Is knowledge analysable? State the strongest argument for and against, and give your view with a calibrated confidence.
7. Assess the knowledge norm of assertion. Does it survive the lottery and selfless-assertion cases? What follows for the analysis of knowledge?
8. Is pragmatic encroachment true? Are the bank cases evidence for the *semantics* of “knows” or for a *pragmatic* feature of knowledge attributions?
9. Is the swamping argument sound? If so, what follows for the JTB analysis? If not, which premise fails?
10. “The Gettier industry is a case of patching, not progress.” Assess. What would count as progress in the analysis of knowledge?
11. Construct your own Gettier case using the generator. Then construct a case that your preferred fourth condition handles but a rival fails. What does the exercise show about the state of the debate?
12. What is the value of knowledge? Give your own answer, and connect it to the practical domains of §7.4.

> *A closing note.* The analysis of knowledge is the clearest case in the book of a problem that is *both* a live research programme *and* a candidate for dissolution. The Gettier industry has produced genuine conceptual advances, and it has also produced a great deal of patching. The knowledge-first programme has offered a way out, and it has been accused of changing the subject. The virtue programme has offered a synthesis, and it has been accused of leaving the hard questions unanswered. The honest verdict is that the debate is unresolved, and that its unresolved character is *instructive*: it shows what philosophical progress looks like — not a settled answer, but a sharper understanding of what the question is, and of what would count as an answer.

> *And a bridge.* The next chapter turns to the structure of justification, which the analysis of knowledge has presupposed. If knowledge requires justification, how is justification itself structured? The regress problem, foundationalism, coherentism, and reliabilism are the subject of Chapter 8 — and they are the theories on which the responses to scepticism (Ch. 6) and the analyses of knowledge (this chapter) depend. The connection is direct: every account of knowledge is an account of the conditions under which a belief is *warranted*, and the theory of warrant is the theory of justification.
